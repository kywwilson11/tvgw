# Design decisions

A log of the choices that shape tvgw, in the style of architecture decision records. Each entry gives the context, the decision, and its consequences, good and bad. Add an entry when you make a choice that someone would otherwise undo by accident. If a decision is reversed, keep the old entry and add a new one that says so.

The overview is in [architecture.md](how-it-works/architecture.md).

## 1. The TV uses the direct uplink, not the VPN

*Decided by the owner, 2026-10-27.*

**Context.** The Pi runs a VPN client for its own traffic. tvgw can send the TV's traffic either straight to the home router (`UPLINK=direct`, through policy-routing table 100) or into the tunnel (`UPLINK=vpn`, out the tunnel `VPN_IF` names or finds, `tun0` or `wgpia0` by default, dropped while the tunnel is down, MSS clamped to the tunnel's MTU). Both modes are in `deploy/nftables/tvgw-apply`.

**Decision.** The TV stays on the direct uplink. `UPLINK=vpn` stays in the code and is not used. The reasons:

- Streaming services (Peacock, Max, Netflix, Prime Video) flag VPN exit ranges and refuse to play.
- vpn mode fails dark. Every time the tunnel reconnects, the TV loses its network.
- The TV moves 60 to 70 GB of video a day. Pushing that through the tunnel on a Wi-Fi uplink costs throughput for no gain.
- The trackers are blocked either way. DNS filtering, the firewall and the URL filter all act before routing. And the services know the account whatever the source address.

**Consequences.** Direct mode exposes two things:

- The ISP sees the destination addresses and TLS server names of the TV's connections, and those names are most of what the TV looks up. It does not read the TV's DNS itself, and the reason is DoH by IP address, not the tunnel: AdGuard Home asks its upstreams over DNS-over-HTTPS at literal addresses, and a TLS client sends no server name for an address (Go's `crypto/tls` leaves it out, and RFC 6066 forbids it), so on either path the ISP sees an unnamed TLS session to the provider. What the tunnel adds is that the DoH provider sees a VPN exit instead of the home address. Under PIA's `killswitch=auto` there are windows (a daemon start before its first connection, `piactl disconnect`, a daemon that is not active) in which the Pi's own traffic, DoH included, leaves by the uplink; the leak counters of [decision 44](#44-the-tunnel-is-a-setting-and-the-pis-own-leaks-are-counted) count them. *Corrected 2026-11-01 from the November research (section 10); the earlier text credited the tunnel.*
- LG, the CDNs and the streaming services see the home's public address.

In direct mode tvgw-apply clears the VPN client's routing mark on TV packets (`undo PIA routed-packet tag`) and adds `ip rule` priority 60 to table 100. Rule 60 sits before PIA's forwarded-packet rule (priority 70), so the TV's data path never depends on the tunnel; the mark is cleared as a second guard. `tvgw verify` checks both the route and the rule. To revisit the decision, set `UPLINK=vpn` in `/etc/tvgw/tvgw.conf` and run `sudo tvgw-apply`. `verify` then expects the TV's route to leave by the tunnel `VPN_IF` names or finds.

*Amended by [decision 49](#49-the-gateway-answers-the-tv-through-the-tv-port-table-100-carries-the-tv-subnet) (2026-11-03): rule 60 also catches the gateway's own answers to the TV, so in direct mode table 100 also carries the TV subnet on the TV port, and `tvgw verify` checks where those answers leave (`gw_reply_route`). Before that, only the VPN client's own rule ahead of rule 60 let the reference gateway answer the TV.*

## 2. Isolate first, then filter

**Context.** The TV sweeps the LAN, probes networks it remembers, and tries hardcoded resolvers such as 8.8.8.8. A DNS filter on its own can be bypassed by any device that ignores the DHCP-assigned resolver.

**Decision.** The TV gets its own subnet on its own cable, and the Pi is its only route out. The firewall forces every DNS packet to AdGuard and rejects the known bypasses (DoT, DoQ, QUIC, DoH resolver addresses, tunnels). Filtering is layered on top of that.

**Consequences.** The Pi needs two network paths. The TV must forget its Wi-Fi networks, or it has an unfiltered fallback. Devices on the home network cannot open connections to the TV, so casting and remote apps that need a LAN connection do not work across the gateway. The LAN probes become evidence in the brief instead of a risk.

## 3. No TLS interception

**Context.** Decrypting the TV's TLS would need a certificate the TV trusts. Apps pin their certificates, and installing a CA on the TV would need root (entry 4).

**Decision.** tvgw does not decrypt anything. It sees names (DNS, TLS server names, plain-HTTP hosts), sizes and timing. QUIC is rejected (`BLOCK_QUIC=yes`) so apps fall back to TCP and their server names stay visible.

**Consequences.** Ads stitched into a video stream cannot be removed: Prime Video's come from the same CDN as the show (recorded in `lg-webos.rules`). Telemetry is judged by where it goes and how much, not by what it says. The capture never holds encrypted payload.

## 4. No rooting of the TV

**Context.** Root exploits exist for several webOS generations; the profile's `[[generations]]` table lists them. On the CX's firmware (04.64, webOS 5) the LAN API refuses writes.

**Decision.** tvgw leaves the TV's software alone. Settings are changed by hand with the remote. tvgw reads them hourly over the LAN API (`tvgw tvcheck`), stores changes, and warns if Live Plus (ACR) comes back on.

**Consequences.** Settings that only the remote can change stay manual. Firmware updates keep arriving, since `snu.lge.com` is must-allow for security fixes, and each one may bring new endpoints; the brief's Firmware section lists the names that follow an update.

## 5. Fail closed

*2026-10-26.*

**Context.** A firewall that half-loads, or a stop that removes the rules before forwarding is off, leaves the TV online without a filter.

**Decision.** `tvgw-apply` takes a lock, loads the whole ruleset in one atomic `nft` transaction, and switches forwarding on last, globally and for both interfaces. Stopping `tvgw.service` turns forwarding off on the TV interface before it deletes the rules.

**Consequences.** A stopped service leaves the TV dark, never unfiltered. The stop path changes state the start path must restore; missing that once left the TV with no route ([incidents.md](incidents.md)). `install.sh` therefore re-applies in place when the service is already up. While the service is stopped, the input chain is gone too, so the Pi's own services can be reached from the TV subnet.

## 6. AdGuard Home is configured as code

**Context.** Changes made in AdGuard's web UI leave no history and drift silently from what the repo says.

**Decision.** `tvgw adguard apply` builds AdGuard's lists, custom rules, upstreams, the TV client entry and a few settings from the profile and `tvgw.conf`. It owns only those keys, and every key that could unfilter the TV is among them: the HTTP bind address; under `dns` the upstreams, bootstrap servers, `upstream_dns_file` (empty), optimistic cache, client-address anonymization, the private-PTR settings, the allowed and disallowed clients and `fallback_dns` (all empty); the query log and statistics; under `filtering` the enabled flags, `protection_disabled_until` (null), `blocking_mode` (`default`, the `0.0.0.0` answers that `verify` and the brief read as blocked), the blocked TTL and the legacy rewrites (empty); the filter entries it adds; the DNS allowlists (`whitelist_filters`, empty: AdGuard checks them before any rule); `user_rules` wholesale; and the TV's client entry (ids, tags, global settings, no per-client upstreams, logging on). It validates the candidate with `AdGuardHome --check-config`, swaps it in atomically under a lock, and restores the backup (taken before the stop, written back atomically) unless the resolver resolves a must-allow name within 30 seconds. `tvgw adguard diff` shows drift, and a hardware test allows none. Since 2026-10-27 it also owns `dns.aaaa_disabled` (true: the TV segment is v4-only with IPv6 dropped at the wire, so AAAA answers are withheld locally instead of fetched) and `dns.handle_ddr`, and generates one type-scoped rule withholding HTTPS/SVCB records from the TV's address (`[adguard] strip_qtypes`), which keeps Encrypted ClientHello keys and address hints away from it.

**Consequences.** A change made in the UI is reverted on the next apply. The one manual step is AdGuard's first-run wizard. Each apply that changes something leaves a `.bak-` file that nothing prunes. `apply` uses the profile inside the installed package, so policy changes go through a deploy.

*Since [decision 46](#46-the-blocklists-are-watched-and-adguard-changes-only-on-purpose) (2026-11-01) the plan also owns the list update interval, the evidence and cache keys and the two retention intervals, and `fallback_dns` follows `DNS_FALLBACK`, empty by default.*

*Amended by [decision 52](#52-setup-fetches-adguard-homes-own-release-pinned-and-hash-checked-and-seeds-it-without-the-wizard) (2026-11-03): the wizard is no longer a manual step, since `tvgw adguard install` answers it through AdGuard's API on loopback; with `ADGUARD_LISTEN=gateway` the plan also owns `dns.bind_hosts` and `dns.port`.*

*Amended by [decision 59](#59-site-rules-etctvgwlocalrules-written-by-tvgw-allow-and-tvgw-block-applied-after-the-profile-and-before-machine-rules-under-the-same-lint) (2026-11-03): policy no longer changes only through a deploy; `tvgw allow` and `tvgw block` write the site rules on the gateway, which `tvgw adguard apply` plans between the profile's rules and the machine's. AdGuard's web UI is still never where a rule is made.*

*Note (2026-11-04): the plan also owns `dns.ratelimit` as `0`, no limit, because AdGuard's default of 20 queries a second dropped the TV's bursts with no line in the query log; `dns.edns_client_subnet` off; and `dns.upstream_mode` as `load_balance`, AdGuard's default. [What tvgw adguard apply owns](how-it-works/adguard.md#what-tvgw-adguard-apply-owns) gives each reason.*

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: two of the November research's owed tests, measured.* Over the whole store of the reference gateway, read on 2026-11-05 (2026-10-25 to 2026-11-05), the CX asked for A records 38,635 times and AAAA records 8,007 times, and for no other record type. So it never asked for the HTTPS or SVCB types (the November research's test T3, [section file 02](research/deep-dive-2026-11/02-naming-beyond-sni.md#tests-exact-to-run-on-the-gateway-later)): the `strip_qtypes` rule has had nothing to withhold from this TV, and it stays for a stack that would ask. In the same store it never looked up `resolver.arpa`, `use-application-dns.net` or the Private Relay names (test T8), so no answer of `dns.handle_ddr` has reached it. What AdGuard answers to such a lookup, which T8 as written asks of the gateway itself, is still not measured; [decision 46](#46-the-blocklists-are-watched-and-adguard-changes-only-on-purpose)'s note says why it matters.*

## 7. Must-allow exceptions are generated from the TOML

**Context.** Blocking the wrong LG host breaks the TV: the clock host, firmware updates, the connectivity check, the Content Store. Blocklists change several times a day.

**Decision.** Every host in `[must_allow]` becomes an `@@||host^$important` exception, generated into AdGuard's custom rules. The modifier, not the position, is what makes the exception hold: AdGuard gives a `$important` block precedence over a plain exception whatever the order, and only an exception carrying `$important` outranks it. Nobody writes these by hand. The `$dnsrewrite` rules for the URL-filter hosts are generated the same way, from `[url_filter].hosts` and the `GW_IP` setting. The profile lint refuses a block, rewrite or regex that covers a must-allow host, a block or rewrite at the apex of a `never_block_apex` zone (or a wildcard-only chain above it) and a regex matching across one, and an exception that sits above or equals a blocked name or is a regex (which names it lifts cannot be checked). It also refuses the rules that switch others off: any `$badfilter`, and an `@@...$dnsrewrite` exception over a URL-filter host or a rewrite. `tvgw verify`, the brief's red line and the hardware suite all check the must-allow names.

**Consequences.** There is one place to edit. The lint runs in CI through the unit tests and again in `tvgw adguard` before anything is planned, so a profile loaded from a path is never applied unlinted. Regex rules are compiled case-insensitively, as AdGuard compiles them, and matched against every must-allow host; a rule in any other form than `||host^`, a one-name hosts line or a regex is refused, because AdGuard reads a pattern without `||` as a substring and one without `^` as a prefix; hosts-syntax blocks, `$dnsrewrite` rules and `$dnstype` rules that withhold any type but HTTPS, SVCB or AAAA are read as the blocks they are.

*Amended by [decision 59](#59-site-rules-etctvgwlocalrules-written-by-tvgw-allow-and-tvgw-block-applied-after-the-profile-and-before-machine-rules-under-the-same-lint) (2026-11-03): the owner's site rules join the plan after the profile's rules and before the machine's, linted beside the profile; an owner's allow may sit at or under a blocked name, and a block that would cover a must-allow host is refused there as here.*

## 8. A URL filter for LG's file service instead of a DNS block

*2026-10-26.*

**Context.** LG's GFTS hosts carry app packages and icons, which the TV needs, and the ACR config file, the telemetry schedule, LG Shopping data, promo thumbnails and the LG Channels guide, which it does not. Blocking the hosts broke a Prime Video update on 2026-10-25. DNS cannot split one host.

**Decision.** AdGuard answers the GFTS names with the gateway address. nginx on the Pi forwards plain-HTTP requests and returns 403 when the URI matches `block_regex` (`biz_code=PREMIUM|CDP|LGSHOPPING|MAS|IBS`). HTTPS to the same names is passed through by server name, undecrypted.

**Consequences.** The regex lives in two places, the profile and `deploy/nginx/tvgw-gfts.conf`; a hardware test checks that the nginx file contains the profile's. nginx becomes a dependency of app installs, and `tvgw verify` checks it is up. The filter is a deny list: a new `biz_code` passes until it is added. HTTPS content from those hosts is not filtered.

## 9. DoH upstreams by IP address

**Context.** The Pi's VPN client blocks plain DNS to anything but its own resolver. A DoH upstream given by name needs a plain-DNS bootstrap first.

**Decision.** AdGuard's upstreams are DoH URLs with literal addresses (`DNS_UPSTREAMS`, default `https://1.1.1.1/dns-query,https://1.0.0.1/dns-query`; bootstrap `1.1.1.1,1.0.0.1`). nginx resolves the real GFTS names with `URL_FILTER_RESOLVER`, which should be the VPN provider's resolver when the client blocks other DNS.

**Consequences.** The TV's lookups are encrypted on the way out and, while the tunnel is up, leave through it. The DoH provider sees them, from the VPN exit. Because the upstreams are addresses, the TLS handshake carries no server name, so the ISP learns nothing from it on either path ([decision 1](#1-the-tv-uses-the-direct-uplink-not-the-vpn)). AdGuard's optimistic cache keeps answering while the tunnel reconnects, but only for names already asked: a failed refresh keeps an entry, up to `cache_optimistic_max_age` (12 hours by default) past its expiry, while a name never asked fails at once, and every AdGuard restart empties the cache, which tvgw's own applies, reverts, `undo` and deploys cause. The two default upstreams are one failure domain: Cloudflare withdrew the prefixes of both `1.1.1.1` and `1.0.0.1` for 62 minutes on 2025-07-14. *Corrected 2026-11-01 from the November research (section 10).* `URL_FILTER_RESOLVER=1.1.1.1`, the template's value, cannot answer nginx behind PIA's kill switch, which rejects the Pi's plain DNS to any server but PIA's once it has connected; the settings template says so.

## 10. A rollback journal, not WAL

**Context.** WAL mode needs `-wal` and `-shm` side files that every reader can write. `tvgw brief` and the other read commands run unprivileged.

**Decision.** The store uses `journal_mode=DELETE`. Readers open it `mode=ro` and need read access to one file.

**Consequences.** A reader can block briefly behind the hourly writer; connections wait up to 60 seconds. Ingest commits per batch of 10 000 log lines (with the cursor that covers them) and per pcap, so the lock is held per batch, never for a whole backlog. Backups with SQLite's online backup are safe while ingest runs. Writers run `synchronous=FULL`: with a rollback journal, `NORMAL` leaves a window for corruption on power loss, and the cost is one fsync per commit, a few dozen an hour.

## 11. Row-id watermarks for the hosts counters

**Context.** Counting rows by time window double-counted rows that fell in the overlap between hourly runs, and missed rows that arrived late.

**Decision.** `refresh_hosts` keeps, per source table, the highest row id already counted (`meta.hosts_wm`) and counts only rows above it.

**Consequences.** A row is counted once, however late it arrives. Without a watermark, the first run rebuilds the table from the base tables. The watermark also records its row's time: when that row is gone or re-stamped (a table emptied by prune or by hand, after which SQLite reuses the ids), the table is rebuilt too, and the counters then mean the retained rows. Deleting old rows by hand otherwise does not lower the counters.

## 12. Each flow is named by its own TLS hello

**Context.** CDN addresses serve many names. Naming a flow by its destination address mixes, say, a streaming service's video with the same CDN's telemetry.

**Decision.** A flow takes the name of the TLS hello with the same destination, destination port and TV source port, inside its lifetime, with 300 seconds of slack because conntrack's start times run late. See [architecture.md](how-it-works/architecture.md#naming-a-connection).

**Consequences.** Upload attribution is per connection. A flow without a captured hello (UDP, plain HTTP, a missed packet) is unnamed and shows as Unresolved or by address.

## 13. Counters, not logs, prove the firewall held

**Context.** A rule that matched nothing looks exactly like a rule that works, and a quiet log is not evidence.

**Decision.** Every rule worth reporting has a `counter` and a `comment`. `tvgw verify --record` snapshots every counter every 10 minutes. The brief's "Firewall held" line sums the positive steps per rule.

**Consequences.** Rule comments are an interface: `queries.FW_LINES` matches them by prefix and `verify` looks for key rules by their text. Renaming a comment silently drops its line from the brief. A re-apply resets counters; `tvgw-apply` lists them either side of the reload and the next `verify --record` stores both listings, so a reset costs nothing unless a second apply follows within the same interval. The gate's headline is never below the samples it logged, each of which was counted first.

## 14. Red lines are the alarm model

**Context.** The brief is long, and most of it is context. A silent source can look like a clean day.

**Decision.** A few sentences that must never be true go at the top as `RED LINE:` lines: a must-allow name blocked, a clock host blocked or never looked up while the TV was on, failed `verify` runs, ingest warnings. A Coverage line comes first and says `NOT ASSESSED` for any source not read in the last two hours.

**Consequences.** Nothing pages anyone. The brief is read by hand, and a failed `verify` shows in `systemctl --failed`. Each red line has a runbook entry in [operations.md](use/daily-brief.md#red-lines). The ACR red line fires on an ACR-shaped upload or on the adtech port wall's counter, never on a timer ([decision 45](#45-the-acr-red-line-is-an-upload-test-and-a-port-wall)).

*Amended by [decision 58](#58-the-brief-has-a-plain-form-with-three-tiers-the-login-message-shows-its-status-and-action-and-tvgw-doctor-explains-every-check) (2026-11-03): presentation only. The brief also has a plain form, in which each red line has one of three tiers, act, doctor or detail; the engineering brief keeps every red line as one tier, as here. `tvgw doctor` explains every check that did not pass in plain words and offers its fix, and `tvgw status` says in three lines whether the last check held. A check new to `tvgw verify` is a WARN at most until a later change promotes it, so it adds no red line while it is new, and a must-allow name that answers when asked again 3 seconds later is a WARN, not a failed run. Still nothing pages anyone or reaches the TV's screen.*

## 15. Blocklist entries come from evidence, with grades

**Context.** Community lists disagree, and a wrong block breaks a TV in ways that are hard to trace.

**Decision.** Every custom rule in `lg-webos.rules` carries a dated reason: what the host is, how it was seen, what blocking it cost. Rules tried and rejected stay as comments. Entries not seen on the CX are marked "unverified". Generation and hardware rows carry a confidence grade: `observed`, `community`, `spec` or `low`.

**Consequences.** The profile can cover newer webOS releases while being honest about what was only reported. The code does not validate the grade; review does. See [../src/tvgw/profiles/README.md](../src/tvgw/profiles/README.md).

*Amended by [decision 59](#59-site-rules-etctvgwlocalrules-written-by-tvgw-allow-and-tvgw-block-applied-after-the-profile-and-before-machine-rules-under-the-same-lint) (2026-11-03): a rule written on the gateway with `tvgw allow` or `tvgw block` carries a dated comment line with the owner's reason instead of a graded source; the profile's own rules keep this decision's discipline.*

## 16. QuickSet and Hue stay blocked, with the cost written down

**Context.** Universal Electronics' QuickSet hosts identify HDMI-connected devices; the CX polled them about every 45 seconds. Philips Hue bridge discovery polled about every 5 minutes.

**Decision.** `ueiwsp.com`, `quicksetcloud.com` and `meethue.com` stay blocked.

**Consequences.** The TV cannot detect or set up other remotes and devices through QuickSet, and Hue sync and control on the TV do not work. The costs are recorded next to the rules. To change this, remove the rule in `lg-webos.rules` and deploy.

## 17. STRICT_PORTS stays off until two clean weeks

**Context.** The TV has only been seen using TCP 80 and 443 and DNS. NTP (UDP 123) stays open in case a firmware uses it. Rejecting every other port makes apps fail fast instead of hanging, but a dependency nobody has seen yet would break.

**Decision.** `STRICT_PORTS=no` until two consecutive weeks of briefs show `Unexpected ports: none` or only entries that were looked up and accepted. Until then, other ports are logged, counted and allowed.

**Consequences.** The `odd ports` counter and the brief's "Unexpected ports" line are the evidence. The criteria and the steps to flip it are in [operations.md](use/blocking.md#strict_ports). The comment in `deploy/tvgw.conf.example` still says "a week or so"; this entry supersedes it. Since 2026-11-01 the adtech port wall resets LG's ad-tech ports before the odd-port rules, so "Unexpected ports" counts only unknown ports and an ACR or ad port no longer keeps it from being empty ([decision 45](#45-the-acr-red-line-is-an-upload-test-and-a-port-wall)).

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: the clock port is counted by server.* UDP 123 stays open to any address, and that made it the widest path left open by design, with nothing saying where the TV's NTP went. `tvgw-apply` now keeps a table of its own, `inet tvgw_ntp`, after every forward chain that can refuse: one rule counts the TV's UDP 123 and updates the dynamic set `ntp4` (at most 1024 addresses, each leaving 10 days after its last request, a timeout no poll period a TV is known to use comes near) with a counter per address. It refuses nothing, and no setting turns it off. It is written with `add` statements, as the gate's table is ([decision 18](#18-dns-gated-egress-starts-as-a-count-in-its-own-table)), so a re-apply keeps every address and its count; the kernel never changes an existing set's size or timeout, so a set of other properties is deleted and made again at the next apply, which says so; a full set adds no address and lets the packet through, a missing table changes nothing for the TV, and a table nft refuses only warns. `tvgw verify --record` stores the rule's total, how many addresses the set holds and its 32 most recently requested addresses with their counts and expiries, and the brief's engineering form prints one line, `Clock servers (NTP): ...`, with the most used addresses and the names the store's answers give them, `not counted` when the gateway checked itself and no snapshot listed the set, and nothing on a day the count was taken without NTP ([the brief's sections](reference/brief-sections.md#clock-servers)). The count is evidence for this decision's end state, not a change to it: whether a firmware needs NTP, and to which servers, is what it measures. The commit that adds the table carries `Deploy-window: tv-off`.*


## 18. DNS-gated egress starts as a count, in its own table

**Context.** Every rule so far judges a destination by name or by list. A TV that connects to a hardcoded address, or resolves a name through a channel the gateway does not see, is invisible to both. The October 2026 R&D survey's one new idea was to allow only addresses the TV's own lookups returned; enforcing that on day one would break anything that legitimately uses a cached or embedded address.

**Decision.** `DNSGATE=count` (the default): `tvgw-dnsgate` adds every IPv4 address AdGuard hands the TV to the set `inet tvgw_gate resolved4` with a one-day timeout and an expiry of the same length, so each answer starts the address's day again on kernel 6.12 and later (until 2026-11-01 the add carried no expiry and every address kept its first one, [decision 43](#43-the-flow-log-hears-the-tv-alone-and-the-gates-day-starts-at-each-answer)), and a rule in that table counts, and logs a sample of (six a minute), each new connection the TV opens to an address the set does not hold. Nothing is dropped. The table is separate from `inet tvgw` so its dynamic set does not disturb the baseline `tvgw verify` compares, and it is created with `add` statements so a re-apply keeps the addresses. The brief carries the count and the logged samples with the TLS name a hello gave each address, which tells a forgotten lookup (a day old, or before the Pi last booted) from a hardcoded server. An enforce mode is a later decision, after two weeks of counts, and it must fail closed only when the feeder is proven to see every answer.

**Consequences.** One more root service holding `CAP_NET_RAW` and `CAP_NET_ADMIN`. conntrack calls every packet of a flow new until a reply is seen, so the rule counts a flow's tuple once (a dynamic set, `seen4`, holds it for two minutes) rather than every retransmitted SYN to a server that is down. The first day after a Pi reboot overstates: the TV's cache outlives the Pi's set. The feeder is passive: it adds an address a few milliseconds after the answer leaves the Pi, and the TV can connect sooner, so the first connection to an address new to the set may be counted too; the brief marks a sample that came within two seconds of an answer naming its address (`just looked up`). `DNSGATE=off` removes the table and the count. The feeder reads every UDP answer the Pi sends the TV, including those to DNS the TV aimed at another resolver (the redirect makes them leave with that resolver's address); a TCP answer (a truncated response retried over TCP) is not added and its address would count once.

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: dnsmasq's 2026 overflow, and its place today.* An enforce mode needs a feeder in the answer's path, and one design for it puts dnsmasq in front of AdGuard Home, where it would parse every lookup the TV sends. dnsmasq 2.93 fixes a heap overflow in its name parser, `extract_name()`, which a crafted DNS message can trigger (CVE-2026-2291). The reference gateway runs Debian 13's dnsmasq 2.91-1+deb13u2, and Debian's security tracker lists trixie's package as fixed from 2.91-1+deb13u1 (DSA-6264-1), so the revision it runs carries the fix ([the tracker's entry](https://security-tracker.debian.org/tracker/CVE-2026-2291), read 2026-11-05). Today dnsmasq only hands the TV its address: its DNS is off (`port=0` in [its configuration](../deploy/dnsmasq/README.md#the-config-line-by-line)), so no lookup of the TV's reaches that parser. The tracker's open dnsmasq issues of the same day are in TFTP, DNS over TCP, CNAME caching, DHCPv6, DHCP leasequery and DNSSEC, none of which the gateway's dnsmasq runs.*

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: the count is its own race, and a second filler stands in the answer's path.* Five days of the count on the reference gateway held 322 misses, 320 of them within two seconds of the answer naming the address: the passive feeder losing to the TV's connect, as the consequences above expect, and only 2 that the gate is for. With `DNSGATE_FILL=inpath` a second set is filled before each answer leaves and counted beside this one ([decision 64](#64-the-gates-second-set-is-filled-in-the-path-of-the-answer-beside-the-passive-feeder-and-still-only-counts)); this decision's set, rule, feeder and count stay exactly as they are, as the comparison. The condition for an enforce mode, a filler proven to see every answer, is what the second count and its `answers offered` line exist to measure.

## 19. A daily analyst that only proposes

**Context.** The brief is thorough and the owner reads it once a day. Judging a new name (telemetry or the app doing its job?) takes context about vendors, SDKs and how the TV behaved, which an analyst model has and a rule list does not. The owner asked for the analysis to be built once API access existed, and the standing rule is that policy changes are approved one at a time.

**Decision.** `tvgw-analyst.timer` runs `tvgw analyst` at 03:50: one streamed request to `claude-opus-5-5` (effort high, adaptive thinking) with yesterday's evidence pack, answered inside a JSON schema. The pack is built from the day report and minimized on purpose: names, counts, categories, the current rules, public server addresses, hours on and the apps seen; never the TV's or the LAN's addresses, MAC addresses, device or host names, LAN probe targets, reverse-lookup names, request paths or query strings, or an hourly timeline. A scrub over every string enforces it whatever the queries return, and the hardware suite checks the real pack. Every proposed rule is vetted exactly as a profile rule would be (the lint, the must-allow hosts, rules already in place, an unblock of a rule that exists) and the verdict is kept next to the proposal. Nothing is applied: proposals land in `reports/proposals/<day>.md` and `.json` for the owner, who adopts a rule by editing the profile's `.rules` file and deploying. The API key is one root-only file, `/etc/tvgw/claude.key`; without it the job skips quietly, and `--dry-run` writes the pack and the prompt and sends nothing.

**Consequences.** About $0.05 to $0.15 a day at the model's list price (the pack is a few thousand tokens; the record keeps each day's usage and cost). What leaves the Pi is readable before it leaves (`tvgw analyst --dry-run`) and documented in [privacy-and-retention.md](how-it-works/privacy-and-retention.md). The model's text is data too: a proposal that fails the lint is shown as refused, never silently dropped, and a refusal by the model's safety layer is a line in the day's file. Strings in the pack come from the TV and the internet; the system prompt says so and the vetting does not trust the answer either.

## 20. The gateway blocks on its own, under three watches

**Context.** Decision 19 left every rule to the owner, and the owner said that was the wrong shape: either the proposals go unread and nothing happens, or they arrive every day and are more trouble than they are worth. The gateway should act on its own. But a rule list cannot tell telemetry from the connection a stream rides on, one model's confident mistake could undo months of work, a mistake has to be fixed within minutes rather than at the next report, and a day with nothing new should cost nothing.

**Decision.** With `ANALYST_APPLY=auto`, a `block` the daily model proposes is applied by the gateway itself when every one of these agrees, in this order.

1. *The code's gates.* The proposal is vetted (the profile's lint) and rated low risk by the proposer; the name was looked up on at least two distinct days; its traffic shape in the window, read from the flows the TLS hello named, carried no payload: a median under 16 KB down per connection and under 2 MB down in the window; and fewer than three autonomous blocks were made in the last 24 hours. A shape is evidence the model cannot talk its way around: a name that moved 300 MB is content, whatever it is called.
2. *A second key.* A different model (`ANALYST_VERIFIER_MODEL`, `claude-fable-5-1` at xhigh effort by default; `verifier_for` never returns the proposer, whatever the settings say) is handed the proposal, the shape and the context and asked to refute it. A veto, low confidence, a refusal or an error leaves the rule unapplied.
3. *A file the machine owns.* The rule goes into `AUTO_RULES` (`/var/lib/tvgw/auto.rules`), which `tvgw adguard apply` appends to the profile's rules after the same lint, so a machine rule can never cover a must-allow host. The profile's `.rules` file is never edited by the machine; the owner's work and the machine's are separate files with separate histories.
4. *A live canary.* Two readings of the bytes the Pi has sent the TV, a minute apart before the block, give the download rate; for ten minutes after it, a reading every 30 s. Three readings running under a fifth of that rate while the TV stays on and was streaming (at least 1 MB a minute), or the streaming app in the foreground giving way to the launcher, take the rule out again and re-apply AdGuard, within minutes of the block. With the TV off there is nothing to watch, and the hourly judge takes over.
5. *A day of probation.* `tvgw-autoblock.timer` runs `tvgw autoblock check` every hour. A must-allow or clock red line since the block, or the last full TV hour's streaming volume (bytes down in the expected categories per hour the TV was on) under a fifth of the three days before, reverts. When the day is up, the whole window is judged the same way at a half, and the rule passes when the TV was on too little to tell.
6. *The owner's hand.* `sudo tvgw undo` takes the newest machine rule out of the file and AdGuard (`--all` for every one); deleting the file does the same at the next hourly check, and the record follows. Every step is recorded: `auto_rules` in the store, `Applied:` on the proposal, `tvgw autoblock list`, and a section in the brief.

(Decision 41 changes items 1, 2, 4 and 5: a shape with no connection no longer passes, the verifier sees no proposer prose, a canary with nobody watching is armed, one rule is on trial at a time, and a trial passes only on TV hours with demand.)

A request is made only when the window holds something the store does not already know. The analyst is not asked when the TV was off and nothing was looked up, nor when the only names seen were judged within the last week (every proposal's verdict is remembered by name in `judgments`; a block refused for too few days of evidence, the daily cap or a transient failure is not a verdict and is asked again) and no red line, blocked name reached, ACR-like cadence, gate sample, unresolved destination or firmware change appeared. Such a day writes a one-line file and spends nothing; `--force` asks anyway. (Decision 22 narrows this: only a gate sample nothing explains counts, a blocked oddity never does, and a pass that proposes nothing is remembered.) The pack carries each candidate's shape and the previous judgements, so the model reasons from how the TV used a name, not from the name alone.

**Consequences.** The machine can only add blocks, only of names whose connections look like telemetry, only with two models agreeing, and only three a day; it cannot touch the owner's rules, allow anything, or change the firewall. The failure the owner feared, a stream breaking, is caught three ways: live within minutes when the TV is on, hourly when it is not, and by one command. The second request costs about as much as the first on the days it happens; quiet days cost nothing, and the longer the gateway runs the more days are quiet. The verifier sees the same minimized, scrubbed evidence as the proposer and nothing more ([privacy-and-retention.md](how-it-works/privacy-and-retention.md)). The gates are code and are tested without a model; the model calls are tested with fakes; the hardware suite checks that the file, the store and the live resolver tell one story and that the hourly check runs clean. The check has its own unit rather than a line in `tvgw-analyze`, whose read-only system would have made a revert's re-apply fail silently; a re-apply that fails is recorded on the decision and fails the unit. Known limits: a name blocked as telemetry can still be one an app checks before it plays (the canary and the hourly judge answer that, not the gate); the download-rate canary needs the TV to have been streaming before the block; an effect that shows only days later (a feature that stops working) is the owner's to notice, and `tvgw undo` the remedy.

*Amended by [decision 59](#59-site-rules-etctvgwlocalrules-written-by-tvgw-allow-and-tvgw-block-applied-after-the-profile-and-before-machine-rules-under-the-same-lint) (2026-11-03): the machine never proposes or applies a block of a name the owner allowed in the site rules, and an owner's allow takes a machine block under it out of `AUTO_RULES`; the owner's rules now also live in a file on the gateway, which the machine never writes.*

## 21. A continuation inherits its name

**Context.** Decision 12 names a flow by its own TLS hello, inside its lifetime with 300 seconds of slack. When conntrack loses the entry of a live connection (the policy-route churn of the 2026-10-30 incident, a deploy, a table-full eviction), the kernel picks the connection up again and the flow log gets a second row for the same `dst`, `dport` and TV source port, starting seconds after the first ended, with no hello of its own. That row was unnamed and the brief showed its bytes by address.

**Decision.** A flow with no hello in its own window, whose start falls within `FLOW_SLACK` of the end of an earlier flow on the same tuple, looks for the hello from that earlier flow's start: one hop back. The latest hello before the flow's end still wins, so a connection with a hello of its own is never renamed by an inherited one. `tvgw host` gathers one name's flows with the same rule.

**Consequences.** Continuations join the connection they belong to in Top upload, the categories and the autonomy judge's streaming volume (an unnamed continuation used to drop out of that volume, which could have read as a collapse). The caveat is a reused source port: a new connection to the same address and port, within 300 seconds of the old one ending, whose own hello was not captured, inherits the old name. It is the same server, so the name is probably right, and it was shown as a bare address before. Chains of two or more continuations are not followed.

## 22. A pass that proposes nothing is remembered

**Context.** The novelty check of decision 20 was meant to keep a day that looks like yesterday free of requests, and it did not. In its first week every viewing day bought a request, each answered "nothing to do", for three reasons that recur by construction: the DNS gate's samples, most of which are the feeder losing its race with the TV's connect or the gate's day-long memory running out (the set kept each address's first expiry however often the TV asked again, which [decision 43](#43-the-flow-log-hears-the-tv-alone-and-the-gates-day-starts-at-each-answer) ends); the same three fixed hostnames flagged as DNS oddities every day (a long hex label and two "random" labels that are the same every day), all already blocked; and an allowed name outside the expected categories, `www.google.com`, that the model looked at and dismissed, then was shown again the next day because a dismissal left no verdict in `judgments`. The owner's rule is that a request is made only when something changed.

**Decision.** Three narrowings, in `novelty`, `asked_about` and `gate_unexplained`. 1. A pass that proposes nothing about a name it was asked about (a new host, an allowed name outside the expected categories, an unblocked oddity) leaves a `quiet` verdict in `judgments`, so the daily pass treats the name as judged for a week; a real verdict younger than a week is never overwritten by it. The weekly review ignores quiet verdicts and looks again at what the daily passes waved through, with the stronger model, so a wrong dismissal lasts at most a week. 2. The gate counts toward novelty only through `dns_gate.unexplained`: a sample neither within `FEEDER_LATE` of an answer naming its address nor with that address in any A answer of the `KNOWN_DAYS = 7` before it. A name in such a sample or none, it is a hardcoded server or a resolver bypass, which is what the gate is for. The brief lists these under the gate's table. 3. An oddity a rule already blocks never counts: the model is told never to re-propose a rule in place, so there is nothing it could say. Red lines about the TV (a must-allow name blocked, the clock, Live Plus, an ACR-like cadence (since [decision 45](#45-the-acr-red-line-is-an-upload-test-and-a-port-wall): the ACR-shaped upload and port attempt lines), Encrypted ClientHello), blocked names reached, unresolved destinations and firmware or settings changes still buy a request whenever they appear; red lines about the gateway's own health (`HEALTH_RED_LINES`: firewall checks, ingest warnings, a producer gone quiet) do not, since the model cannot act on them and the brief and `tvgw verify` already carry them. The first run under this decision, 2026-11-01, was bought in part by "ingest warnings: 2", the flow logger's own lost events, which is how the exception was found.

**Consequences.** A steady week costs the daily pass nothing after its first request about each name; the weekly review still runs once when anything was waved through. The price is a week's blindness to a name the daily model wrongly dismissed, bounded by the weekly review and lifted by any other reason. The whole judged set, not the 40 verdicts the model is shown, decides novelty (`_judged`, stripped by `analyst.public` before anything is written or sent), so a long quiet list can never hide a verdict or buy a request. `tvgw analyst --dry-run` now says what a real run would ask about, so the gate can be read without spending.

*Note (2026-11-03), from the improvement design's calibration: a fourth narrowing.* A name first seen still bought a request on every viewing day, because YouTube's video nodes are new strings each day: on 2026-11-02, 17 of the 18 names first seen. A first-seen name under a registrable domain the store already knew, in one of the profile's expected categories, allowed, and carrying content by the gates' payload limits (`MAX_TOTAL_DOWN`, `MAX_MEDIAN_DOWN`, read from the pack's `shapes`) is routine and buys no daily request (`analyst._routine`). The telemetry under the same domain is in another category, and a name with a telemetry shape carries no content, so both still count; the weekly review counts routine names too, so a wrong exemption lasts at most a week, the bound this decision already accepts.

## 23. The .deb runs install.sh

*Decided 2026-11-01, with the packaging workstream (R&D survey §8.2).*

**Context.** The survey's verdict was a wheel, a hash-pinned wheelhouse and nfpm, with units in `/usr/lib/systemd/system`, a `config|noreplace` settings file and `StateDirectory=`. `install.sh` already holds twenty ordered, idempotent steps that the reference gateway and CI's hardware job depend on: the install lock, the two-phase discovery of the TV, the nginx check before a reload, the in-place firewall re-apply, the per-unit restart stamps, the `sync` before the long first run. A package that installed its own copies of the units in other paths would be a second installer to keep in step, and a gateway moved from one to the other would run duplicates (`/etc/systemd/system` overrides `/usr/lib/systemd/system`). The settings file is written by `tvgw discover --write` and by a setup tool, so as a dpkg conffile it would raise a prompt on every upgrade and stop an unattended one.

**Decision.** The .deb is a carrier for the installer. It ships the tracked `deploy/` tree as `/usr/lib/tvgw/deploy`, the wheelhouse as `/usr/lib/tvgw/wheels` with its lock and `SHA256SUMS`, and four maintainer scripts. `install.sh` recognizes that layout and installs the Python package from the wheelhouse, offline and hash-checked; every other step is the same as from a checkout, in the same paths (`/opt/tvgw-venv`, `/usr/local/sbin`, `/etc/systemd/system`). The postinst runs it with `TVGW_POSTINST=1` and stdin closed, so it never prompts; a TV not yet identified (exit 75) and a `FAIL` from the closing verify (exit 76) are reported and leave the package configured, and any other stop fails the configuration so dpkg says so and `dpkg --configure -a` retries. An `ERR` trap makes every other stop exit 1, since under `set -e` alone a command's own status (nmcli's 3 and 4) escaped as the installer's and was read as a deliberate exit. Without a running systemd (an image build) the postinst does nothing. On an upgrade, prerm stops nothing; on removal it stops the timers, then the capture, then the firewall through its own stop path, so the TV goes dark rather than unfiltered (decision 5). postrm removes what `install.sh` wrote; purge also removes `/etc/tvgw`, `/var/log/tvgw` and `tv-lan`, and keeps `/var/lib/tvgw`, saying how to delete it.

**Consequences.** One installer, tested by the CI deploy on every push, is also the package's; the tests tie the package layout, the shared AdGuard check and postrm's list of paths to `install.sh`. The price is Debian policy: the package writes into `/usr/local` and `/etc/systemd/system`, and dpkg does not know those files, so `dpkg -S` cannot name them and postrm keeps a list (checked by a test against what `install.sh` writes). The settings file is not a conffile, so dpkg never offers a merged copy; the template is in `/usr/lib/tvgw/deploy/tvgw.conf.example`. A source install and a package install on the same gateway overwrite each other's Python package; the last one owns it. Purge keeping the store is a deliberate departure from the convention that purge removes everything: the store is up to a year of evidence, and its deletion is one command the postrm prints.

*Amended by [decision 53](#53-releases-carry-a-hash-checked-bootstrap-for-two-architectures-with-provenance-under-immutable-releases-updates-are-checked-weekly-installed-only-by-the-user-judged-by-verify-and-rolled-back-a-new-settings-key-never-changes-the-meaning-of-an-old-keys-value) (2026-11-03): the package is built for amd64 as well as arm64, and a release carries `get.sh`, which installs it with apt. Installing a newer release with `tvgw upgrade`, the verdict taken from `tvgw verify` and a rollback to the kept package, is decided there and not built.*

## 24. The TV side comes from the settings file

*Decided 2026-11-01, with the packaging workstream (R&D survey §8.4, item 1).*

**Context.** `eth0`, `10.10.10.0/24` and `10.10.10.1` were written into `tvgw-apply`, `tvgw.service`, the dnsmasq template, both nginx files, the NetworkManager files and `install.sh`, while `GW_IP` moved only the Python side. A second gateway with another TV port or a home network on `10.10.10.0/24` had to edit scripts, and the survey ruled out renaming `eth0` until every literal was gone. A templated subnet is also a new way to break the firewall: `TV_NET=0.0.0.0/0` would masquerade, and route through table 100, everything the Pi forwards.

**Decision.** Three settings, `TV_IF`, `TV_NET` and `GW_IP`, describe the TV side, defaulted to the values the files used to spell, so the reference gateway's rules, DHCP pool and addresses are unchanged. `tvgw-apply` and `install.sh` share one `tvgw-net` shell block, byte for byte, that checks them and derives the broadcast address, the netmask and the DHCP pool (the 10th to 20th host); `config.tv_side` applies the same rules in Python, and the tests run both on the same cases. The rules: `TV_IF` is an interface name other than `WAN_IF`, `lo` and `tun0`; `TV_NET` is a canonical private IPv4 network from `/16` to `/27`; `GW_IP` is a host address in it outside the pool; `TV_IP` is a host address in it other than `GW_IP`; and, in `install.sh`, no address of `WAN_IF` lies in a network overlapping `TV_NET`. `tvgw-apply` refuses a failing value before it loads anything, so the running ruleset stays. `config.load` checks only the shape of the two new keys, so a reader still works on a settings file the installer would refuse. `tvgw-apply` records the three keys in `settings.applied`, and `tvgw verify`'s `applied_current` compares them like the other firewall keys. `install.sh` moves an existing `tv-lan` connection only when its interface or address differs from the settings. Between a `TV_IF` edit and the install that moves `tv-lan`, the TV must never be forwarded unfiltered: `tvgw-apply` stops when `GW_IP` is on another interface than `TV_IF`, the forward chain drops the TV subnet on any other port before it accepts non-TV traffic, masquerade covers only what came in on `TV_IF`, the previous apply's port stops forwarding once the new one is filtered, and `tvgw.service`'s stop darkens the port the loaded filter was built for (`settings.applied`), not the one the edited settings name. The capture takes the three from a drop-in `install.sh` writes, because its user cannot read the settings file.

**Consequences.** A second site edits the settings file and re-runs `install.sh`. A test fails when a literal `eth0`, `wlan0` or `10.10.10.x` reappears in a deploy file outside a default, a comment or a listed exception; the settings template is the only file listed. The pool's position is fixed (hosts 10 to 20), which is why `/27` is the smallest subnet. Renaming the TV port to a stable name such as `tvlan0` with a `systemd.link` file is now possible and is not done.

*Note (2026-11-03), from the improvement design (W15): the router joins the settings it is found by.* `WAN_GW_MODE` (absent `literal`, `WAN_GW` as written) takes, with `auto`, the router from `WAN_IF`'s own default route at each apply, then the address the last apply took from it (`/run/tvgw/wan_gw.last`), then `WAN_GW`. The resolver, `wan_router`, sits in the `tvgw-net` block that `install.sh` and `tvgw-apply` share, and `settings.applied` records the mode and the address used (`WAN_GW_USED`). The reference gateway, which has no `WAN_GW_MODE`, routes exactly as before.

## 25. AdGuard Home is a checked dependency, never bundled

*Decided 2026-11-01, with the packaging workstream (R&D survey §8.2).*

**Context.** AdGuard Home has no .deb or .rpm, only its install script, Docker and Snap. tvgw configures it as code (decision 6) and `tvgw verify` already failed below a version floor, v0.107.73, the release that fixed an authentication bypass over h2c. A package could carry a copy of the binary, and then the resolver for the whole TV segment would be patched only as fast as tvgw is released.

**Decision.** tvgw never ships AdGuard Home. The package's preinst, `install.sh` (step 1, right after its lock, before anything is written) and `tvgw verify` each check that `/opt/AdGuardHome/AdGuardHome` exists and reports at least the floor, and each prints AdGuard's own install command, or its update command when it is too old. The preinst and `install.sh` share one POSIX `sh` block; its floor equals `verify.ADGUARD_MIN`, which a test checks. A preinst refusal happens before dpkg unpacks anything, so the host keeps what it had. Only the native install in `/opt/AdGuardHome` is accepted, because that is where tvgw's config-as-code reads and writes.

**Consequences.** A gateway is always running a resolver its own project patches, and an outdated one cannot be installed over. A Snap or Docker install of AdGuard Home is refused, which is narrower than the survey's proposal; detecting them is left until a site needs it. `install.sh` from a checkout now stops on a host without AdGuard Home, where it used to print a reminder and finish with a failing verify; a host whose wizard has not run yet still gets the reminder.

*Since [decision 46](#46-the-blocklists-are-watched-and-adguard-changes-only-on-purpose) (2026-11-01) the floor is v0.107.79, and AdGuard runs with `--no-check-update`: a new binary comes in only through `tvgw adguard upgrade`.*

*Amended by [decision 52](#52-setup-fetches-adguard-homes-own-release-pinned-and-hash-checked-and-seeds-it-without-the-wizard) (2026-11-03): tvgw may fetch AdGuard Home's own release, pinned and hash-checked, and set it up; the package still carries none of it. The preinst's refusal is to go once setup installs AdGuard itself, and stands until then.*

## 26. The workflows are the most privileged code in the repository

**Context.** A push to `main` runs the `hardware` job on the gateway as the Pi's user with `sudo`, so the workflow files, the actions they call and the right to push to `main` are, together, root on the firewall (section 10 of the October 2026 report). The workflow already pinned its actions by commit and had read-only token permissions; nothing checked either, nothing covered a workflow added later, the dependabot file watched only the actions, `main` was unprotected, and the repository asked approval for a fork's workflow run only from first-time contributors (all three still true on 2026-11-01). The owner pushes to `main` directly and the Pi deploys from it, so any control that puts a pull request or a status check in front of a push breaks the deploy path.

**Decision.** Controls that make the route to the Pi narrow without adding a step to the owner's push. 1. Every action in every workflow is pinned by its 40-hex commit with its tag in a comment, every workflow sets `permissions` at the top with no write access, `pull_request_target` and `workflow_run` are not used, no `run:` step expands text an outsider writes, and a job that can reach the self-hosted runner (any `runs-on` label that is not GitHub-hosted, since a job goes to any runner holding all its labels) runs only for a push to `main` of this repository; `tests/unit/test_supply_chain.py` fails `make gate` when any workflow drifts. The `ci.yml` checkouts set `persist-credentials: false`. 2. Dependabot watches the actions and the Python dependencies weekly, with minor and patch bumps grouped, majors alone and a seven-day cooldown. 3. `main` is to get branch protection that blocks force pushes and deletion and requires a linear history, enforced for administrators too, with no required checks, pull requests or signatures; outside contributors' workflow runs are to always need approval; secret scanning, push protection and dependabot security updates are to be on. These are documented with the commands that apply them on `docs/supply-chain.md` and were not applied as of 2026-11-01; this line flips to applied once the owner has read them back. 4. Signing is documented, not required: it needs a key only the owner can make, the Pi's runner would not check it, and web-editor commits are signed by GitHub, not the owner. 5. `docs/supply-chain.md` states what the Pi's runner may and may not be trusted with. The settings are applied with the `gh` commands on that page, because GitHub holds them, not the repository; the protection goes on only after every branch is integrated by fast-forward or rebase, or a merge commit would be refused.

**Consequences.** The protection costs the owner two habits: history stays linear (fast-forward or rebase a branch onto `main`, never a merge commit), and rewriting pushed history needs the protection lifted and restored, which the page shows in three commands. Including administrators is what gives the rules any force with a single-account repository. Because the pin test covers every workflow, a new workflow (a release job, the virtual gateway job) that uses an action by tag, or has no top-level `permissions`, fails the gate until it is fixed. The boundary stays the owner's GitHub account: a stolen session or a token with the `workflow` scope can still edit `ci.yml` and deploy to the Pi, and the page names the account's own protection, the optional required-reviewer environment and a signature check on the Pi as the next steps. A pin is only as fresh as dependabot's pull requests, so an unmerged bump is a known lag, not a gap.

*Amended by [decision 53](#53-releases-carry-a-hash-checked-bootstrap-for-two-architectures-with-provenance-under-immutable-releases-updates-are-checked-weekly-installed-only-by-the-user-judged-by-verify-and-rolled-back-a-new-settings-key-never-changes-the-meaning-of-an-old-keys-value) (2026-11-03): `release.yml` creates a release as a draft and publishes it once every file is uploaded. Two of its jobs hold more than the top's read access, on the job: `assets` holds `id-token: write` and `attestations: write` to attest the packages and `get.sh`, and runs none of the build's third-party code; `publish` holds `contents: write` and runs on a tag only. Every container image a workflow uses is named by digest. `tests/unit/test_supply_chain.py` checks all three in every workflow.*

*Amended by [decision 63](#63-behavior-changing-deploys-to-the-reference-wait-for-a-quiet-window-decided-on-the-gateway-nothing-the-gateway-prints-where-others-can-read-it-carries-a-home-identifier-acceptance-that-needs-systemd-runs-in-a-container-tier) (2026-11-03): the hardware job also runs on a 30-minute schedule, which GitHub runs on the default branch alone, to deploy a commit deferred to the gateway's quiet window; a commit that changes how the gateway treats the TV deploys only in that window, and every step of the job runs under `pipefail` with its output piped through the scrubber.*

## 27. A namespace copy of the gateway in CI

**Context.** Nothing under `deploy/` ran before it reached the Pi: the unit suite reads `tvgw-apply` as text and runs it against stub commands, and only the hardware suite, after a deploy, could say whether nft accepted a ruleset or a rule did what its comment says. The R&D report (state-of-the-art-2026-10.md, sections 3.3 and 11) rated two remedies ADOPT NOW: `nft -c -f` on every rendered ruleset variant, and a virtual gateway in network namespaces on CI's runners, which have sudo; and a reachability matrix kept as a golden file as a prototype. `tvgw-apply` had no way to render a ruleset without loading it, and it reads `/etc/tvgw/tvgw.conf` and writes `/run/tvgw` with no override for either.

**Decision.** Four parts. 1. `tvgw-apply --check` renders the rulesets the conf would load, prints them and has `nft -c` check each against the running kernel, and changes nothing: in that mode `modprobe`, `sysctl` and `install` are no-ops, `nft` is a function that checks loads and skips deletes and listings, the pre-reload counter listing is not taken, and the script exits before it publishes anything or touches forwarding and routing. A side table nft refuses fails the check, though an apply carries on without it, since finding that before a deploy is the point. Without the flag none of this is defined. 2. `tests/virtual` builds three namespaces (TV, gateway, internet) with the interface names the script hardcodes, and runs the real script in the gateway namespace inside a private mount namespace with temporary directories bind-mounted over `/etc/tvgw`, `/var/lib/tvgw` and `/run/tvgw`, rather than writing the host's conf and restoring it: the host's files are never touched, so the suite cannot overwrite a real gateway's settings or `tvgw verify`'s baseline even when run on one, and adds no environment override to the script. 3. The expected behaviour is a data file, `tests/virtual/matrix.txt`: per scenario, per probe, the verdict and the comment of the rule whose counter must move, with every other drop or reject counter required to stay still. The report suggested `nft monitor trace` for which rule matched; counter deltas answer the same question deterministically, with no trace parsing that differs between nft releases, and decision 13 already makes the counters the interface. 4. The switches' values come from the script's own `case` arms, so a value added there (an enforcing DNS gate, say) is checked at once. The workflow fails, not skips, when root or a tool is missing.

**Consequences.** A ruleset nft refuses, a rule that refuses the wrong traffic, a counted rule that no longer counts, a routing churn on re-apply, and a key rule `tvgw verify` would no longer find in the kernel's own spelling each fail a pull request in about 35 seconds, before any deploy. A firewall change that changes reachability shows up as a reviewed diff of `matrix.txt`. The namespaces are not the Pi: no VPN client, no AdGuard, nginx or dnsmasq (stand-ins answer instead), CI's nftables is older than the Pi's (1.0.9 against 1.1.3; both pass locally), and the kernel log is silent in a namespace, so the hardware suite stays the last word on the live gateway. Building the suite found a gap: rule 60 also sends the gateway's own packets to the TV (from 10.10.10.1) into table 100, which holds only the default route, so on a plain kernel they leave by `wlan0`; the Pi is answered by a route from outside tvgw. The suite works around it with a helper route and records it as a strict xfail that turns red when `tvgw-apply` closes the gap, rather than changing the production routing in this change.

*Amended by [decision 49](#49-the-gateway-answers-the-tv-through-the-tv-port-table-100-carries-the-tv-subnet) (2026-11-03): the gap is closed. `tvgw-apply` puts the TV subnet in table 100, the helper route and the strict xfail are gone, and every scenario starts without that route, so the whole matrix runs with no route from outside the script.*

*Amended by [decision 63](#63-behavior-changing-deploys-to-the-reference-wait-for-a-quiet-window-decided-on-the-gateway-nothing-the-gateway-prints-where-others-can-read-it-carries-a-home-identifier-acceptance-that-needs-systemd-runs-in-a-container-tier) (2026-11-03): beside the namespace copy, a container tier runs what needs systemd, NetworkManager and the real `install.sh` (`tests/container`, the `container` workflow): a privileged `debian:13` container with systemd as PID 1 and a TV in a network namespace behind its TV port, where AdGuard Home is installed from the pin, the installer's two setup passes, a deploy and a boot with the TV port unplugged run, and `tvgw verify` must exit 0. The namespace copy stays the firewall's test. On that container, with no VPN client and Debian 13's reverse-path setting, the gap above also cut the TV off from the gateway after the first apply, until decision 49 closed it: the TV's ARP request for 10.10.10.1 failed the TV port's strict reverse-path check once rule 60 was in. The tier also found that the bringup pass let NetworkManager's dispatcher load the firewall; the pass now leaves the dispatcher out.*

## 28. tvgw setup drives the existing steps; it does not replace them

*2026-11-01, implementing §9 of [the R&D survey](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#9-installer-ux) ("A `tvgw setup` wrapper", ADOPT NOW).*

**Context.** A first install meant reading the quick start, copying the template, editing `WAN_IF` and `WAN_GW` by hand, running `install.sh`, letting it run `tvgw discover --write`, then pairing the LAN API with a separate command. Each step already existed and was tested; what was missing was the order, the questions and an unattended form. The installer is also what `sync-install.sh`, `make deploy` and CI's hardware job run, and packaging (§8.2) will turn it into a `postinst`, so a second copy of its logic would drift from it.

**Decision.** `tvgw setup` (src/tvgw/setup.py) is a state machine over the existing pieces: `discover.gather()` finds the TV, `lgwebos.read()` pairs and reads it, `config.save_conf()` writes the settings file, `install.sh` applies, and `verify.check()` gives the verdict, which setup restates in plain words. It renders no config file, loads no rule and restarts no service itself. When the TV side has no address yet, setup runs the installer once to bring it up (with no TV known, the installer stops after that, by design), then looks again. Setup takes `/run/tvgw/install.lock` before it writes the settings file and holds it until the installer ends, handing it to `install.sh` as fd 8 with `TVGW_INSTALL_LOCKED=1`, the way `sync-install.sh` does; every question is asked before the lock is taken. The installer runs with no terminal on stdin, so its own interactive discovery never starts under setup's. `python3 -m tvgw` runs the CLI from a checkout, so setup can run before anything is installed. Every system call is a field of `setup.Env`, and the unit tests drive every path with fakes.

**Consequences.** The deploy path is unchanged: `install.sh` and `sync-install.sh` behave as before, with or without setup. A deploy that starts while setup is applying waits for it, as it waits for another deploy. Setup inherits the installer's limits, among them the TV subnet `install.sh` checks, until packaging templates them; it reads `TV_IF` and `GW_IP` through `Settings`, so a templated interface reaches it without a change here. Pairing on a first install from a checkout has to wait for the installer to create the virtualenv that holds the LAN-API library, so setup pairs after the install when it must. A first install runs the installer twice, the first time only as far as the TV side.

*Amended by [decision 54](#54-a-tvs-lan-api-is-a-module-the-profile-names-a-reading-never-pairs-a-profile-declares-what-it-can-check) (2026-11-03): setup pairs with `lgwebos.pair()`, not `lgwebos.read()`, which no longer pairs, and pairing runs only in setup and in `tvgw tv pair`.*

## 29. The settings file is edited in place and refused whole

*2026-11-01, for §9 of [the R&D survey](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#9-installer-ux).*

**Context.** `/etc/tvgw/tvgw.conf` is the owner's file: it carries their comments and keys tvgw may not know, and it is sourced by bash as root by `install.sh` and `tvgw-apply`. `discover.write_identity()` wrote four keys and normalized their comments. A general writer for setup has to handle every key, quote values the shell would split, and never write anything bash would execute or `config.load()` would refuse, since a refused file stops every command and an unbootable one leaves the TV dark.

**Decision.** `config.update_conf()` changes only the value of each changed key: comments, blank lines, order, unknown keys, unparsed lines and the line's own quoting stay byte for byte, and a trailing comment keeps its column where the new value leaves room. A line of a changed key that `parse_conf()` cannot read plainly (a bare value with a quote in it, such as the first line of a quoted value that runs over two lines) is refused, never rewritten: the writer cannot tell where bash thinks the value ends. A key the file lacks is appended under one `# ---- set by tvgw setup ----` header. Every value is checked first (`config.check_value()`): the key must be one Settings reads or one of the shell-only `WAN_GW`, `URL_FILTER_RESOLVER` and `DOH_LIST_URL`; the value may hold only letters, digits, spaces and `_ . , : / @ % + = * -`, so no `$`, backtick, quote, backslash, `;`, `~` or `#` can reach bash; `TV_MAC`, `TV_NAME` and the addresses must have the forms `install.sh` and `tvgw-apply` expect. Then the whole result goes through `config.load()` and, where bash is installed, `bash -n`, since `parse_conf()` skips lines it cannot read and bash does not; a file either refuses is refused whole, with nothing written. `config.save_conf()` writes through `discover.write_conf()`, the existing atomic writer that keeps the mode and owner, and a missing file starts from the shipped template so its comments come along.

**Consequences.** Setup can never write a settings file that stops the next command, and a hand-written file survives setup with its comments. The value alphabet is narrower than the shell's: a value that needs another character, such as a calendar expression with `~` (systemd's form for the last days of a month) or a URL with a `?` query, must be written by hand. A file that is already broken (an unquoted space somewhere) is refused for any change until the owner fixes that line, which is the honest outcome. A key packaging adds to `CONF_KEYS` is writable at once, because `config.writable_keys()` reads `CONF_KEYS` when it is called.

*Note (2026-11-03), from the improvement design (W15; its review's E-F14): a moving router is followed without a writer.* `WAN_GW_MODE=auto` is resolved at each apply and never written back: no dispatcher or apply edits the settings file, which stays the owner's and setup's alone. Setup writes `auto` only on a new install whose uplink holds a lease, and keeps the literal `WAN_GW`, which an older release reads as before.

## 30. An unattended setup takes the answers file in the settings format and never guesses

*2026-11-01, for §9 of [the R&D survey](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#9-installer-ux) (`--non-interactive --answers FILE`; cloud-init `runcmd` as a later prototype).*

**Context.** An unattended install (cloud-init, a rebuilt image, CI) needs every question answered in advance. The survey suggested TOML or YAML. The settings file is already a `KEY=VALUE` format that the shell reads, `config.parse_conf()` reads it, and an owner who has a working gateway has a working file. A guess made with nobody watching lands silently: `discover --yes` takes the first device, and the template's example router would be installed as `WAN_GW` on a Pi with no default route yet.

**Decision.** The answers file is the settings file's format: any settings key is written to `tvgw.conf`, and `SETUP_TV`, `SETUP_PAIR`, `SETUP_APPLY`, `SETUP_SSDP`, `SETUP_WAIT` and `SETUP_INSTALLER` steer the run and are never written. Every line is checked before the first step, with the writer's own checks. Unattended (`--yes`), setup stops rather than guess: when several devices could be the TV and `SETUP_TV` does not name one (one device a bundled profile recognises among others is not a guess), when the Pi has no default route to read `WAN_IF` and `WAN_GW` from and the answers do not give them, and when the TV is not found within `SETUP_WAIT` and none is configured. `SETUP_TV=configured` skips discovery for a TV known in advance. The TV's identity is always a device discovery found: a real `TV_MAC` in the answers acts as `SETUP_TV` and must match a device found, a device picked with another MAC is refused, the template's placeholder MAC names no TV, and the answers' `TV_IP` is used only when the answers name the TV. `SETUP_APPLY=no` is never overridden, not even to bring the TV side up. Unattended, `SETUP_PAIR=auto` does not pair (nobody may be at the TV to accept its prompt, and each LAN-API call would wait for it); `SETUP_PAIR=yes` does. Every question still prints with the answer it took, so the log of an unattended run reads like an attended one.

**Consequences.** One parser and one set of checks serve the settings file and the answers, and a copy of a working `tvgw.conf` reinstalls a gateway. An unattended run fails more often than `discover --yes` would, and each failure names the key that would have fixed it. TOML answers are not accepted; nothing in the flow needs nesting.

## 31. Reference records name their evidence or their source

**Context.** The R&D survey's profile registry (sections 6.1 and 6.4 of [the state-of-the-art survey](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#64-a-profile-registry-for-many-vendors)) asks for LAN-API and privacy-setting records, a `peer-reviewed` grade between `observed` and `community`, and CI that fails any entry without a source. Decision 15 graded generation and hardware rows, but the code never checked a grade, and nothing tied a fact to where it came from.

**Decision.** `[[lan_api]]` and `[[privacy_setting]]` records carry a provenance block that `from_dict` checks. `verified` means seen on a set this project ran: the grade is `observed` and the record names its evidence, one of `capture`, `lan-api`, `on-screen` or `breakage`. `unverified` means at least one http(s) URL in `sources` and a grade from `peer-reviewed`, `community`, `spec` or `low`. Any other combination, and any key the record does not know, fails the load. A fact seen on one generation and only reported for another is two records. The grade list gains `peer-reviewed`. Generation and hardware rows keep decision 15's unchecked grade, and a unit test holds the bundled rows to the five known grades. In `lg-webos`, only what the profile already said was seen on the CX is verified: the webOS 5 SSAP reader, Live Plus and the Voice Information agreement.

**Consequences.** A record without evidence or a citation cannot reach the gateway, because every command loads the profile and CI loads every bundled one. The records block nothing and open nothing; they are what `tvgw profile` prints. The check is that a source is given, not that it still resolves: the unit tests have no network, so a dead link is found by a reader. The rubric for promoting an entry is in [grading evidence](reference/profile-format.md#grading-evidence).

## 32. A vendor profile without hardware is DNS-only, cited and subscribed

**Context.** The survey's verdict on other vendors is a prototype: DNS-only `samsung-tizen`, `roku` and `vizio-smartcast` profiles graded from HaGeZi's native lists and the PoPETs 2027 study. No such TV has been behind a tvgw gateway. HaGeZi's lists are GPL-3.0, the PoPETs 2027 list is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, and tvgw is MIT. Perflyst's Smart-TV list blocks `www.samsungotn.net`, which its own comments say firmware updates need, and its regex extension blocks the whole `samsungcloudsolution.net` zone, where the same comments put the firmware hosts.

**Decision.** Such a profile subscribes to maintained lists in `[adguard] filters` (HaGeZi's vendor list where one exists, HaGeZi Pro, HaGeZi's DoH list) and pins only what it relies on being blocked, a handful of rules with ACR first, in groups whose comment cites at least one URL and says `unverified`; `profile.uncited_rules()` must find nothing. Its must-allow hosts come from breakage reports, each reason marked `unverified` and naming its report, and each resolving on the day it was added, since `tvgw verify` fails a must-allow host that does not answer. No list is copied into the repository, and Perflyst's is not subscribed for Samsung. `tvgw discover` does not name these profiles yet, so the owner sets `PROFILE` by hand.

**Consequences.** A gateway can run one of these profiles today, with the gaps any profile but `lg-webos` has: no TV readings in the store, so no power-state sections and no Live Plus-style red line. An unverified must-allow host may turn out to be telemetry, and an unverified block may cost a feature; where a cost has been reported, the group's comment names the rule to lift first. The profiles become verified entry by entry, by [how a profile graduates](reference/profile-format.md#how-a-profile-graduates).

*Amended by [decision 57](#57-consent-the-tv-will-not-report-is-the-owners-dated-word-including-unknown-asked-again-after-a-software-download-samsung-is-read-without-pairing) (2026-11-03): with no reading of the TV, the brief says "Not checked: the TV's Wi-Fi" until the owner confirms on the TV's own screen, with `sudo tvgw tv confirm wifi-forgotten yes`, that its Wi-Fi is off; the owner's answers are stored with the TV's settings. Reading a Samsung TV without pairing is decided there and not built.*

*Amended by [decision 62](#62-a-tv-gets-a-vendor-profile-only-on-evidence-or-its-owners-answer-otherwise-the-general-profile-which-isolates-records-briefs-and-blocks-only-shared-lists) (2026-11-03): setup writes one of these profiles when the TV's owner answers yes to the brand question it asks on one signal, and a TV no profile is assigned to gets the general profile, `generic-tv`, instead of the template's LG one.*

## 33. Parse the held-back first segment of a split hello

*R&D state of the art 2026-10, sections 1.1 and 10.*

**Context.** A ClientHello with a post-quantum key share is about 1.8 kB, two TCP segments, and Chromium-family stacks shuffle their extensions, so the server name can sit in either. The capture filter keeps the segment that begins a hello and drops the rest. tshark, with reassembly on, then holds that first segment back as an unfinished PDU and never dissects it: the hello was lost even when its name was in the bytes kept. Turning reassembly off (data-architecture section 2.2's suggestion) would rescue that case but cut every hello whose segments were all captured, the case position-based capture is meant to make normal, down to its first segment, and with it the full lists JA4 needs.

**Decision.** Keep reassembly on, stated on the command line, and add the held-back data to the same tshark pass: the display filter also keeps `tcp.segment_data`, and `clienthello.parse_record`, a small parser that never raises, reads a ClientHello from those bytes. A name wholly inside them is stored with `partial = 1` and no JA4; a hello whose name is not there goes to `tls_noname` with `partial = 1`. `tshark.dedupe` keeps one hello per transport and four-tuple within 30 seconds and lets a whole hello replace a partial one, so a split hello that was captured whole is one row, and so are a retransmitted hello and a repeated QUIC Initial.

**Consequences.** Hellos lost to the capture's cut now show, named when the name was early, counted when it was not, so the size of the problem can be read off the store. The one-pass design holds. Stored hello counts drop by the repeats the old extractor kept; `pcaps_done.tls_rows` keeps meaning named hellos, and `extract(keep_repeats=True)` reproduces the old count for a comparison. The parser is a second reader of hostile input, and it runs inside the ingest process, not the sandbox; it is bounded (`MAX_HELLO`) and the property tests feed it cut and random input. A name in a segment that was not captured stays lost until the capture keeps whole handshakes.

## 34. A hello without a name gets its own table

**Context.** The old display filter required a server name, so a hello without one, the most suspicious kind (an app connecting where no lookup named), was never stored. `tls.sni` is `NOT NULL`, and a dozen readers (`FLOW_NAME` and through it the categories, Top upload and the autonomy judge; Unresolved flows; the DNS-gate samples; `v_ip_names`; `hosts`) take a `tls` row's `sni` for the connection's name. Data-architecture section 2.2 proposed a nullable `sni`.

**Decision.** Hellos without a name go to a new table, `tls_noname`, with the same address and port columns and the JA4, transport and partial flags. `tls` keeps meaning "a hello that named its server", and no reader of it changes. `queries.tls_noname` reads the new table and, for each JA4, the names the same fingerprint carried.

**Consequences.** No existing query can take an empty name for a name, and a nameless hello on a named connection cannot shadow it. A nullable `sni` would have needed a table rebuild (SQLite cannot drop `NOT NULL` in place) and a guard in every reader. The cost is a second table to prune, count and document, and a query that wants every hello must read both.

## 35. JA4 is computed from the fields, not taken from tshark

**Context.** JA4 is the stable TLS stack fingerprint (BSD-3-Clause) the R&D survey adopts in place of JA3, which Chromium's extension shuffling broke. tshark has a native `tls.handshake.ja4` field from 4.2. Debian 12 ships 4.0.17 and Debian 13 4.4.19, and tshark refuses to start when asked for a field it does not have, so using the native field means probing the version and running two field lists.

**Decision.** `clienthello.ja4` computes JA4 from fields that exist in both versions (cipher list, extension types, `supported_versions`, ALPN, the signature lists), and from the bytes for a hello the parser read. The signature list is told apart from `signature_algorithms_cert` and `delegated_credentials`, which tshark prints under the same field, by walking their lengths in extension order; when they do not add up, no fingerprint is stored. The computation follows Wireshark's own and is pinned by the spec's worked example and the golden corpus, which on the Pi is also run through the installed tshark.

**Consequences.** One code path on every supported release, and the same string from either reader. If our implementation and FoxIO's ever disagree, our stored values are the ones that are wrong, and a re-read of the retained pcaps is the only repair for the last `PCAP_DAYS` days. JA4 names a stack, not an app, and nothing blocks on it.

## 36. A known ECH public name is real ECH

**Context.** The ECH canary (schema 4) tells real ECH from GREASE ECH by the name alone: a hello whose name the TV looked up is GREASE. A stack that really uses ECH may also look its provider's public name up, and then the canary would call real ECH GREASE and stay quiet on the day it should fire. Cloudflare's `cloudflare-ech.com` is the outer name of 99.99% of ECH deployments in 2026.

**Decision.** `queries.ECH_PUBLIC_NAMES` lists the known public names. A hello carrying the extension under one of them is real ECH whatever the TV looked up; any other name falls back to the lookup test. The list is applied when the brief runs, not at extraction, so a name added later applies to the stored history. The outer name is `tls.sni` itself: it is the only name on the wire, so no separate column holds it.

**Consequences.** The canary cannot be silenced by the TV resolving the public name. A provider whose public name is not listed is still caught by the lookup test, unless the TV looked that name up too; a new provider means a line in the list. On a partial hello the extension may lie in the segment the capture did not keep, so the canary can undercount on split hellos ([limits](how-it-works/extractor.md#limits)).

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: Vercel's outer name.* Vercel's CDN supports ECH since 2026-08-14 for domains on Vercel's DNS, and an observer sees a connection to its shared ECH host name, `vercel-ech.com` ([Vercel's changelog](https://vercel.com/changelog/encrypted-client-hello-now-supported-on-vercel-cdn), read 2026-11-05). It is the second provider with a public name of its own, so `ECH_PUBLIC_NAMES` holds `cloudflare-ech.com` and `vercel-ech.com`, and, the list being applied when the brief runs, the stored history is read with it too. `tests/unit/test_extractor_store.py` reads a hello carrying the extension under that name from its bytes and holds it to real ECH, and the red line, with the name looked up.*

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: the CX never asked for an ECH key.* In the reference gateway's whole store, read on 2026-11-05 (2026-10-25 to 2026-11-05), the CX asked for A and AAAA records only, never for the HTTPS or SVCB types that carry ECH keys (the November research's test T3; the counts are in [decision 6](#6-adguard-home-is-configured-as-code)'s note of the same day). So none of its stacks fetches an ECH key by DNS, a type-65 query from it would be news by itself, and this canary's lookup test has had no ECH to judge on this TV. A key shipped inside an app needs no lookup, and the canary is still the only watch for that.*

## 37. The tshark sandbox has no weaker fallback

**Context.** Wireshark published dozens of dissector advisories in 2026 (R&D section 10), and the dissectors read packets from the TV and the internet. tshark already ran as `nobody` in a `systemd-run` unit with no network, a read-only system, the service system calls and memory and time limits; before that, `setpriv` dropped the user and nothing else.

**Decision.** The unit gains the rest of the usual hardening (`tshark.HARDENING`): private IPC, protected kernel logs, clock, hostname and process list, no namespaces, realtime or set-id files, locked personality, a private keyring, `umask 077`, no core dumps and 64 tasks. `SystemCallErrorNumber=` is not set, so a refused call kills tshark, which ingest sees as a failed file and retries, instead of returning `EPERM` and letting tshark finish the file with rows silently missing. As root without `systemd-run`, ingest still refuses to run tshark; the old `setpriv` drop does not come back as a fallback.

**Consequences.** A dissector bug gets less to work with, and a crash leaves no core with the TV's packets in it. A property that blocks something tshark needs shows as an ingest warning on the first hourly run after a deploy, which is why the Pi validation in [the extractor](how-it-works/extractor.md#validating-on-the-pi) runs one file by hand. A host without systemd cannot ingest pcaps as root.

## 38. dumpcap writes the hourly ring

**Context.** tcpdump's `-G 3600` starts a new file 3600 seconds after its own start, and only when the next packet arrives, so files never began on the hour and an idle hour had no file at all (data-architecture finding 6). The October survey rates dumpcap in its place ADOPT NOW ([state of the art, section 1.2](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#12-verdicts)): rotation on the clock, the same BPF, no root. dumpcap ships in `wireshark-common`, which `tshark` already pulls in. The ingest decides which file is finished from `/proc` alone, and the extractor runs as `nobody:nogroup`, which must be able to read every file (the 2026-10-27 incident).

**Decision.** `tvgw-pcap.service` runs `dumpcap -i eth0 -q -P -g -s 0 -b interval:3600 -b nametimenum:2 -w /var/lib/tvgw/pcap/tv.pcap -f "<the same filter>"` as the same user, with the same two capabilities and sandbox. `interval:3600` rather than `duration:3600`: duration counts from the start, as tcpdump did, and only the clock-aligned interval makes each file one clock hour. `-P` keeps the pcap format (not pcapng), so the ingest's glob and header check, tshark and the hardware suite read the files unchanged; pcapng's drop records and comments are not worth that change now. `-g` is required: dumpcap otherwise creates ring files `0600`. `nametimenum:2` puts the time first, since dumpcap restarts its file number at every start. `pcaps.WRITERS` names `dumpcap` and still `tcpdump`, for the deploy that switches and for a rollback. A missing or non-executable dumpcap fails the start in `ExecStartPre` with a message naming the package; the unit does not add `SupplementaryGroups=wireshark`, which would fail every start on a system where the package never created the group.

**Consequences.** Files close on the hour with the TV off, so the hourly ingest and the daily reports see each hour's file closed within minutes of its end; an idle hour is a 24-byte file the ingest reads as empty. Names change from `tv-<stamp>Z.pcap` to `tv_<stamp>_<n>.pcap`; the store keys files by name only, so nothing migrates, and the old names sort first. The deploy restarts the capture once (its unit file changed). The one environmental dependency is dumpcap being executable by `tvgw-pcap`, which holds when `wireshark-common` was installed answering No to non-superuser capture (the default); [the capture page](how-it-works/capture.md#what-dumpcap-needs) gives both fixes otherwise.

## 39. The first packets of each connection, behind a switch

**Context.** The byte-pattern filter keeps only the segment that starts a ClientHello, so a hello split over segments (post-quantum key shares, randomized extension order) can lose its server name, and what the capture keeps per connection is bounded only by the patterns. nftables can hand the first N packets of each connection to NFLOG (`ct original packets`), which keeps every handshake whole and bounds the bytes by construction; the survey rates it PROTOTYPE, a firewall change to be run beside the current capture and measured ([state of the art, sections 1.1 and 1.2](research/state-of-the-art-2026-10.md#11-findings), top recommendation 5). The live gateway must not change until the owner chooses to.

**Decision.** `CAPTURE_MODE=full|headers`, default `full`. With `headers`, `tvgw-apply` loads its own table `inet tvgw_capture` after the filter: one prerouting chain at `mangle - 5` (after conntrack, before the DNAT and the rejects), policy accept, two rules that only count and log to NFLOG group 7: packets 1 to 8 of each connection the TV opens (`ct original packets != 0` and `<= 8`, so a connection without conntrack counters never matches everything), at most 4096 bytes each (a GRO-merged hello of two or three segments fits; 1600 would not), and every packet to TCP port 80 after them, as the full capture keeps plain HTTP whole. A failure to load it only warns. `tvgw-pcap-headers.service` reads `nflog:7` with dumpcap into the same directory as `tvhdr_<stamp>_<n>.pcap`. The two units carry mirrored `ExecCondition`s (run, skip, or fail on a value neither accepts), the full unit `Wants=` the headers unit and the headers unit is `PartOf=` it, so `install.sh`, which enables and restarts `tvgw-pcap` only, needs no change. `tvgw verify` expects the unit the mode names, fails when both run, and in headers mode checks the rule. There is no third mode that runs both captures into the store: every hello would be counted twice; the side-by-side measurement uses a transient shadow capture into a scratch directory instead ([measuring it on the Pi](how-it-works/capture.md#measuring-it-on-the-pi)).

**Consequences.** With the default, the firewall is unchanged and the only difference on the Pi is decision 38. With `headers`, split hellos are named and each connection's capture is bounded, at the cost of connection flags, DNS questions and other UDP after the eighth packet of a flow, of some TV-sent ciphertext in the first eight, and of drop records the pcap format cannot hold; [the capture page](how-it-works/capture.md#what-it-gains-and-what-it-loses) lists each. A headers file is not expected to be smaller than a full one. Whether the mode becomes the default rests on five measurements on the Pi: bytes per hour, hello recall against a shadow full capture, drops (the rule's counter against the frames kept), how often the snaplen cut a packet, and whether the frame times match the kernel's. A future extractor that replaces tshark must read link type NFLOG.

## 40. The TV's radios are checked, not assumed

**Context.** The gateway's premise is that the TV has one road to the internet, the cable into the Pi. The owner asked, after the radio research of 2026-11-01, whether the whole effort could be undone by a radio: a TV that rejoins a remembered Wi-Fi network after an update or a reset, Wi-Fi Direct, tethering, or Bluetooth. Measured that day, the CX reported over its LAN API: wired connected, Wi-Fi disconnected, Wi-Fi Direct disconnected ("P2P technology unavailable" when asked to start it), tethering off, no cellular; and it advertised itself continuously over Bluetooth Low Energy, which a nearby phone can see but which carries no data to anyone without a relaying app. Bluetooth is a presence leak; Wi-Fi coming back is a bypass. Nothing in the gateway was watching for the second.

**Decision.** The hourly reading of the TV (`tvgw tvcheck`, stored in `tvsettings`) asks `connectionmanager/getstatus` beside the settings and keeps five keys: the states of the wired port, Wi-Fi and Wi-Fi Direct, and whether Wi-Fi and Bluetooth tethering are on (`PATH_KEYS` in `src/tvgw/lgwebos.py`). A reading in which any path but the wired port is on is a `WARNING` and exit code 3 from `tvcheck`, a red line in the brief (`TV has a second network path:`), a `FAIL` of the new `tv_paths` check in `tvgw verify` (read from the last reading file, so verify stays off the TV), and, because the keys are in `PACK_SETTINGS`, a settings change the analyst is asked about. A reading that lacks the keys, from a TV that is off or a firmware that does not answer, raises nothing: the check reports what it knows.

**Consequences.** "Wi-Fi is off" becomes a fact checked every hour instead of a memory. The cost is one more request per reading. What this does not see: a radio turned on between readings and off again, and the Bluetooth advertisement itself, which webOS 5 offers no supported way to silence (the known method needs a rooted set, which decision 4 forbids). A passive listener on the uplink for the TV's Wi-Fi hardware announcing itself on the home network is the next step, so the two halves need not agree with each other.

*Amended by [decision 57](#57-consent-the-tv-will-not-report-is-the-owners-dated-word-including-unknown-asked-again-after-a-software-download-samsung-is-read-without-pairing) (2026-11-03): a TV that reports no network paths is "not checked", never OK: the brief says so until the owner's dated `yes` that the Wi-Fi is off, and asks again once the TV's software has changed since that answer.*

## 41. The autonomy refuses what it cannot see and keeps what it decided

**Context.** Decision 20's safety rests on code that reads bytes, not on the models: the realistic attack on the analyst is camouflage that earns a quiet verdict, and no prompt defence is proof against an adaptive attacker ([the November report, section 3](research/state-of-the-art-2026-11.md#3-the-analyst-under-autonomy)). The second research pass found the code failing open in three places, each of which let a block land, or graduate, on no evidence ([03 F2](research/deep-dive-2026-11/03-analyst-evaluation-and-safety.md#f2-the-code-gates-are-the-real-defense-and-they-fail-open-in-three-places)). A name with no named flow had a shape of zeros, and zeros passed the payload gate. The live canary needed the TV `Active` at apply time, and the daily pass applies at 03:50. A trial with fewer than two TV hours passed "too little to compare", and a passed rule is never judged again. The hourly judge reverted on the minimum of n hours, so at a 10% chance per hour that an innocent hour reads low, 4 TV hours reverted a good rule 34% of the time, and an HDMI evening read as a collapse ([16 F3](research/deep-dive-2026-11/16-gap-one-change-at-a-time-a-change-ledger-and.md#f3-the-judges-arithmetic-multiplies-confounders-min-of-n-hours-end-time-accounting-no-demand-model)). Two rules on trial at once shared every bad hour. Nothing could be replayed: the pack was written only on `--dry-run`, `judgments` and `auto_rules` keep one row per name and per rule, and the second model's usage was thrown away. `SYSTEM` still told the proposer "You never apply anything", which decision 20 made false, and the verifier read the proposer's own prose, so one injection could reach both models. `Screen Off`, the TV on with a dark panel, counted as off. As measured on the gateway on 2026-11-01, no machine rule has been applied yet (`auto_rules` is empty). What goes live at deploy depends on `ANALYST_APPLY` in the Pi's `/etc/tvgw/tvgw.conf`, which this record does not state; the owner asked for `auto` from decision 20's first deploy. With `off`, the brief's TV hours, the pack kept beside each request, the held verdicts and the prompt's wording change. With `auto`, so do the gates, the queue, the armed canary with its reads of the TV, and the judge.

**Decision.** Every change can only refuse or delay a block, or keep more evidence.

1. *Gates.* A block whose shape has no connection is refused, `no: no observed connections`, and asked about again, unless the name received at least one A answer in the window and no flow went to any of those addresses (`autoblock.answer_use`, the DNS gate's join of answers to flows). A name with a label holding `ignore`, `instruction`, `system`, `assistant`, `override`, `prompt`, `allowlist` or `tvgw`, or a label longer than 40 characters, never receives a quiet verdict. The tokens are matched as whole words of the label (split on hyphens, underscores and digits), so `adsystem` and `ecosystem` are hosts. Such a name is `held`: its verdict records its evidence (`analyst.evidence`: categories, unblocked DNS oddity kinds, payload), the daily pass treats it as judged while that evidence recurs, and a change of evidence or the weekly review opens it again, so a name the TV looks up every day does not buy a request every day (decision 22). Every free-text string that reaches a model (the pack, the vetted proposal, the verifier's message) passes `analyst.canonical` first, which drops Unicode categories Cf, Co, Cn and Cs, the tag block U+E0000 to U+E007F, the bidi controls and the C1 controls; it runs before the scrub's redaction, so a hidden character cannot split a secret.
2. *Watches.* `queries.AWAKE` is `("Active", "Screen Off", "Screen Saver")`, and the judge, the canary and the pack read `power IN AWAKE`. The judge measures streaming per TV hour with demand: an awake hour with a streaming app in front (not the launcher, not an input) or an expected-category connection opened in it. The hourly test reverts only on two consecutive demand hours each under a fifth of the baseline. At the end of a trial, nothing passes without two demand hours on both sides: too few in the trial is NODATA and the trial runs a day longer, up to 14 days (`TRIAL_CEILING`); at the ceiling, or with no baseline before the block to compare with, the rule stays in place as `untested`, listed in the brief every day, still judged for harm, never passed on its own. `MAX_CONCURRENT_TRIALS = 1`: a block that clears the gates during another's trial, or while older ones wait, waits in `auto_queue`; when its turn comes the vetting and the gates run again (on evidence read up to then) before the second model is asked, at the next analyst run, quiet or not; a wait over 14 days drops it. `RETRY_PREFIXES` gains `no: no observed connections`; a queued block is a verdict and is remembered, so a waiting block that fails its turn for a reason in `RETRY_PREFIXES` goes back into the queue in its place, with the start of its evidence unchanged. The hourly check and the analyst's autonomous steps share one lock (`autoblock.exclusive`, `/var/lib/tvgw/autoblock.lock`): a check that finds it held exits 0, and an analyst that finds it held queues its blocks. `tvgw autoblock check --no-watch` judges without the armed canary, for the hardware suite. A block applied while the TV is not awake with a watchable app in front arms its canary, and the hourly check runs the same 10-minute watch at the first reading that shows the TV so, one watch per check; the canary records `armed`, `passed` or `reverted` when watched, and `unobserved` when the rule leaves its trial unwatched. A rule is recorded armed before its watch starts, so a watch the process does not live to finish is the hourly check's to run.
3. *Evidence.* `<stem>.pack.json` is written for every request sent, not only on `--dry-run`, without the private `_judged` key. A `decisions` table (schema 9) gets one row per gate decision, queue event, second-model answer, apply, canary outcome, judge verdict and owner revert: the gate's margins (connections, bytes, days seen, answers used, the limits), the judge's (the demand rate and hours on each side of the block, the trial's share, the low hours running with their shares, and the four thresholds; `autoblock.judge_margins`), the verifier's model, effort and tokens, the trial id and the outcome. Rows are never updated; a trigger refuses it. A re-apply of AdGuard that fails after a revert is a row of its own (`apply`, `reapply_failed`). `judgments` and `auto_rules` keep their current roles.
4. *Models.* `SYSTEM` says what is true: a block both models agree on, with the code's checks, is applied at once on probation and watched, and the owner can undo it. The verifier receives the proposal's kind, name and rule, the name's category, the code's shape and limits and the context, never the proposer's reason, evidence or rollback. The second model's usage goes into `decisions`, the record and the `.md`.

**Consequences.** The autonomy blocks less and later. A name whose connections went unnamed waits for a day the capture names them; a rule applied on a night before days of no viewing stays untested instead of passing, and the owner sees it daily until deciding; a second good proposal waits up to a trial (a day, or up to 14 with no viewing) behind the first. The hourly check can now run for about 11 minutes and reads the TV over its LAN API while an armed canary waits (its unit's timeout rises from 10 to 25 minutes), and its revert restarts AdGuard while someone is watching, as the canary at apply time already did. An armed watch's baseline is the rate at the start of the watch, with the block in place, so a stream that never starts is left to the two-hour test. The demand rules name `com.webos.app.hdmi` and `com.webos.app.livetv` as inputs; those identifiers are the ones LG integrations use and have not been seen on this TV yet, and an input missing from the list only counts as demand. The judge's thresholds (two hours, a fifth, a half, two demand hours) are the old ones, not yet calibrated by placebo runs over the store ([03 F7](research/deep-dive-2026-11/03-analyst-evaluation-and-safety.md#f7-decision-quality-under-autonomy-counterfactuals-breakage-cost-cap-and-trial)), and the hour classifier and the change ledger of [16](research/deep-dive-2026-11/16-gap-one-change-at-a-time-a-change-ledger-and.md) are not here: a confounded hour can still revert, given two of them running with demand. An hour's streaming volume still credits a flow's bytes to the hour its conntrack entry expired (`t_end`), while demand hours are keyed by reading and `t_start`, so one long video connection over the first two hours of an evening reads as two low demand hours; the armed canary meets fresh evenings more often than the 03:50 apply did. This end-time accounting is 16 F3 item 2's confounder and is left to the ledger's judge v2. A false positive of the instruction test costs a request each time the name's evidence changes and a look at each weekly review. The stored packs are the corpus the eval of 03 needs.

**Owner decisions.** `DECISIONS_DAYS` (default `keep`): how long the decisions rows, which hold names and reasons like `auto_rules`, are kept; `keep` matches `auto_rules` and `judgments`, which are never pruned. The time a queued block is applied stays the analyst's schedule (`ANALYST_SCHEDULE`); applying a waiting block at the moment the TV is next watched, so the canary sees it at once, is 03's item 11 and is not built. Whether an untested rule is kept, adopted into the profile or undone is the owner's, case by case.

## 42. The Pi is closed to the TV whatever nftables holds

*Workstream guard, 2026-11-01, from [the November report](research/state-of-the-art-2026-11.md#9-the-pi-is-the-tvs-only-neighbour), section 9 and recommendation 2, and [section file 11](research/deep-dive-2026-11/11-gap-the-pi-is-the-tv-s-only-neighbour-the-tv.md) F1, F4 to F7.*

**Context.** One chain, `inet tvgw`'s `input`, stood between the TV and every socket on the Pi, and it is missing at boot until `tvgw-apply` runs, while `tvgw.service` is stopped, after an apply nft rejected, and whenever anything deletes the table. Forwarding is off in each of those windows (decision 5), so the TV is dark toward the internet, but the Pi itself was open to it. Measured on the gateway on 2026-11-01, the services listening on every interface are smbd, nmbd, xrdp, shairport-sync (Debian 4.3.7, AirPlay 1), avahi-daemon, sshd (not socket-activated) and JupyterHub's proxy on TCP 8000. Binding them to the LAN address would not help: Linux owns an address for the whole host, so only `ip daddr != GW_IP drop` keeps the TV off the Pi's other addresses. Two gaps existed even with the firewall up. The input chain never checked the source, and `rp_filter` on the TV port is 2 (loose), so a forged home-LAN source could make the resolver answer a LAN host. And the forward chain accepted any source in the TV subnet, so a second address on a rooted TV would be outside the flow log (`--orig-src TV_IP`) and the HTTPS/SVCB strip (`client=TV_IP`). dnsmasq, which parses every DHCP packet the TV sends, kept `CAP_NET_ADMIN`, which is enough to rewrite the firewall.

**Decision.**

- **The guard table.** `inet tvgw_guard`, from `deploy/nftables/guard.nft.template` (`__TV_IF__`, `__TV_NET__`, `__GW_IP__`, filled by `install.sh` into `/etc/tvgw/guard.nft`), with input, forward and output chains at priority `filter + 10`, after `inet tvgw`. Its input chain allows what `inet tvgw`'s does (established, DHCP, DNS, nginx's 80 and 443, pings at 5 a second, to `GW_IP` only, from `TV_NET` or `0.0.0.0` only) and drops the rest; its forward chain drops a foreign source, the home-LAN ranges and IPv6 from the TV port; its output chain lets only root open the TV's LAN API on TCP 3000 and 3001, the rule October's survey placed in `inet tvgw`, moved here so it holds in the windows it is for. It is one `nft -f` transaction of `add` and `flush chain`, so a reload never leaves a moment without the chains. A drop in any base chain is final and an accept is not, so the guard can only narrow. While `inet tvgw` is loaded its final input counter stays still; `tvgw verify`'s `guard_quiet` warns when it does not, by comparing it with `tvgw-apply`'s listing right after its last load.
- **Its unit.** `tvgw-guard.service`, ordered as Debian trixie's `nftables.service` (read from the package, not from memory: `DefaultDependencies=no`, `Wants=` and `Before=network-pre.target`, `Before=` and `Conflicts=shutdown.target`, `WantedBy=sysinit.target`), plus `Before=tvgw.service`. Oneshot with `RemainAfterExit=yes`, `RefuseManualStop=yes` (which systemd also applies to restarts), no `ExecStop`, `ExecReload` the same `nft -f`. `install.sh` reloads it only when the rendered file changed or the table is missing, with `systemctl reload-or-restart` (a reload when active, a start when not); the .deb's scripts never restart or stop it, and removal only disables it. The one way to lift it is `nft delete table inet tvgw_guard` with the unit disabled ([operations](use/troubleshooting.md#removing-the-guard-in-an-emergency)).
- **Source pinning in `inet tvgw`.** Input drops `ip saddr != { 0.0.0.0, TV_NET }` before anything answers (comment `TV source outside its subnet (input)`, counted under the brief's `foreign source`). `tvgw-apply` sets `net.ipv4.conf.TV_IF.rp_filter=1` beside its other sysctls. Forward drops `ip saddr != TV_IP` (comment `TV source is not the TV`, the brief's `second TV address`) once the TV is pinned. "Pinned" means `TV_MAC` is a real MAC, not that `TV_IP` is set: the shipped template sets `TV_IP` to a placeholder while the TV still holds a pool address, and the pin then would have darkened a fresh install. `tvgw discover --write` records `TV_MAC` and `TV_IP` together from the lease the TV holds. When `TV_IP` is edited while the TV still holds an unexpired lease for another address under `TV_MAC`, that address forwards too, with a warning, until an apply after the lease moved; otherwise the TV would be dark for up to half its 12-hour lease. `TV_IP` and `TV_MAC` join `settings.applied` and verify's `APPLY_KEYS`.
- **The co-tenants' own wall.** `CO_TENANT_UNITS` (default `smbd nmbd xrdp shairport-sync nqptp avahi-daemon ssh jupyterhub`, plus installed `qbittorrent-nox@` instances) names the units that get `/etc/systemd/system/<unit>.service.d/10-tvgw-not-from-tv.conf`: `IPAddressDeny=TV_NET` and `IPAddressAllow=GW_IP/32`, a cgroup filter on ingress by source and egress by destination that holds with every nftables table gone. `GW_IP` stays allowed because the kernel drops a packet arriving on the TV port with one of the Pi's own addresses as its source, so only the Pi uses it, and a co-tenant that resolves through the resolver there keeps working. A unit that is not installed is skipped; an active socket unit of a listed name gets the `[Socket]` variant. `tvgw guard cotenants` writes and removes them and `install.sh` runs `systemctl daemon-reload` without restarting the units. That this attaches a running unit's new filter at once rests on a reading of systemd, not a test here: on deserializing a unit whose cgroup is realized, systemd invalidates its cgroup and BPF state so the new settings are realized again after the reload (the end of `unit_deserialize_state` in `src/core/unit-serialize.c`; not re-read against the systemd release on the Pi). `systemctl show IPAddressDeny`, which `cotenants_denied` reads, reports only the loaded property, so the hardware test `test_cotenant_ip_filters_are_attached_to_their_running_cgroups` checks the kernel with `bpftool cgroup show` (it skips where bpftool is not installed). If the reading is wrong, a running co-tenant gets its filter at its next restart or the next boot, and the guard table holds meanwhile. avahi also gets `deny-interfaces=TV_IF` in `avahi-daemon.conf`, with a one-time backup and a restart only when the file changed. The line tvgw adds under its own comment is tvgw's: a `TV_IF` change replaces the port in it, and taking `avahi-daemon` out of `CO_TENANT_UNITS` (or `none`) removes it again. A name tvgw appended to a `deny-interfaces` list the owner wrote stays there (the `.tvgw-backup` is the file before tvgw touched it). dnsmasq, nginx, AdGuard Home and tvgw's own units are refused by the setting's check, since they serve the TV.
- **dnsmasq without the firewall's capability.** `dhcp-broadcast` and `no-ping` in the template; with both, dnsmasq 2.91 asks for neither `CAP_NET_ADMIN` nor `CAP_NET_RAW` (read in Debian's `src/dnsmasq.c`). The drop-in `20-tvgw-hardening.conf` bounds it to `CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE CAP_SETUID CAP_SETGID CAP_CHOWN CAP_DAC_OVERRIDE`. `CAP_DAC_OVERRIDE` is beside section file 11's set: dnsmasq writes its pid file as root into `/run/dnsmasq`, which Debian's tmpfiles line makes `dnsmasq`'s with mode 755, so root needs it there; dnsmasq keeps none of the five after it drops to its user. `IPAddressDeny=any` with DHCP's addresses allowed, and the usual protections.
- **nginx sandboxed.** Section file 11's lines in the drop-in `install.sh` already wrote, plus `CAP_KILL` (the root master signals `www-data` workers when its channel to them fails) and `AF_NETLINK` (glibc's `getaddrinfo` with `AI_ADDRCONFIG`); `AF_INET6` only when `nginx -T` shows a `listen [` address, `MemoryDenyWriteExecute=yes` only when it shows no `pcre_jit on`. `ReadWritePaths` entries are optional (`-`), so a missing directory does not stop nginx.
- **Health checks with a way back.** A sandbox reaches a daemon only at a start. `install.sh` restarts dnsmasq and checks it is active and holds UDP 67; it restarts nginx once when its drop-in changed and checks it listens on `GW_IP:80` and `:443`. On a failed check it prints a `WARNING`, takes the hardening out (dnsmasq's drop-in removed, nginx's written back without the sandbox), restarts again, and stamps the refused text in `/var/lib/tvgw/.unit-stamps/` so later deploys do not restart the daemon twice each time. Only a daemon that does not serve even without the hardening stops the install, as a failed restart did before.
- **`tvgw discover`** puts its SSDP answer hole at the head of the guard's input chain too, in each table that is loaded, and removes all of them in the same `finally`; on a first install the guard is the only input chain.
- **`tvgw verify`** gains `guard_present` (a FAIL: the table, its three hooks, its key rules, and the listing the unit left in `/run/tvgw/guard.applied`), and three checks that print `WARN` and pass: `guard_quiet`, `cotenants_denied` (from `systemctl show`, so a drop-in never reloaded counts as missing) and `dnsmasq_caps` (the running daemon's `CapEff`, and its bounding set `CapBnd`, which alone shows a drop-in that is missing: with `dhcp-broadcast` and `no-ping` dnsmasq drops every capability whether or not the drop-in is there). A WARN is new: none of the three is an outage, and a FAIL would stop every deploy with the TV in use. The guard's counters go to `fw_counters` beside the others.

**Consequences.**

- The Pi is closed to the TV at boot, during a stop, after a failed apply and during an upgrade, and the co-tenants stay closed to it even with every table flushed. The virtual CI proves the first with canary listeners in the namespace gateway ([the virtual gateway](../tests/virtual/README.md)); the Pi proves the rest after a deploy.
- The guard cannot stop root or a process holding `CAP_NET_ADMIN` from deleting it. nft's `owner` and `persist` flags would not either, so they are not used; the answer is fewer such processes, which is why dnsmasq loses the capability.
- On a stock system `rp_filter=1` on the port changes nothing: the kernel uses the larger of `conf.all` and the port's value, and systemd sets `all` to 2. The input chain's rule is the layer that holds. Lowering `conf.all` would touch every interface, the VPN's included, so it is left to the owner.
- A process under a listed unit can no longer reach anything in `TV_NET` but `GW_IP`. That is the point for the daemons; for JupyterHub it includes the notebooks its spawner runs inside its own unit. A non-root process anywhere can no longer open the TV's LAN API.
- The first deploy restarts nginx once (a new drop-in) and dnsmasq as every deploy does. dnsmasq now answers every DHCP request by broadcast; every RFC 2131 client accepts that, and the TV's next power cycle and lease renewal on the Pi are the check.
- New counters with comments of their own; the brief labels the pin (`second TV address`) and folds the input source rule into `foreign source`. The guard's own counters are stored but not yet given a brief line. <!-- (fin-release) --> Since 0.2.0 the guard's final input drop has the line `guard`, which stays at 0 while `inet tvgw` is loaded; its other counters still have none.
- **Owner decisions.** None of these changes what the TV may reach on the internet. Held behind settings or left as they are: `CO_TENANT_UNITS` (the default the brief named; `none` turns the drop-ins off, and dropping `jupyterhub` from it keeps notebooks able to reach the TV subnet); `net.ipv4.conf.all.rp_filter`, left at the system's value; dnsmasq's `static` range once the TV is pinned (section file 11 Fix B), the L2 pin of `netdev tvgw_l2` to `TV_MAC`, nginx's `preread_timeout` and `limit_conn`, and the co-tenant version and posture check, all not built here.

*Note, 2026-11-01 (the 0.2.0 integration).* `none` turns off the drop-ins of the units `CO_TENANT_UNITS` lists, and avahi's `deny-interfaces` line, but not the `qbittorrent-nox@` one. `config.co_tenants("none")` is the empty list, and `guard.wanted_units` adds `guard.TEMPLATES` (`qbittorrent-nox@`) to whatever the setting lists, so while the template is installed `tvgw guard cotenants` writes `qbittorrent-nox@.service.d/10-tvgw-not-from-tv.conf`, which systemd applies to every instance, and `cotenants_denied` checks every loaded instance, `none` included. The code keeps it on purpose (qBittorrent's WebUI listens on every address by default), and [the settings file](reference/settings.md) says so; the owner-decisions row for `CO_TENANT_UNITS` below reads the same.

## 43. The flow log hears the TV alone, and the gate's day starts at each answer

*2026-11-01, from the November research (sections 6 and 16 item 4; deep dive 06 F1, F2 and F8).*

**Context.** Three things the gateway believed about its own evidence were wrong.

- **The flow log's buffer.** `tvgw-flowlog` asked for a 32 MB netlink buffer (`conntrack -b 33554432`, 2026-10-31) after a torrent client on the Pi overflowed the 8 MB one twice in an evening. conntrack-tools 1.4.8 applies `-b` to its request socket and reads events from a second socket it opens for `-E` (Debian's 1:1.4.8-2, `conntrack.c` lines 3436, 3452, 3477 and 3824), so neither buffer ever reached the socket that overflowed. Measured on the gateway the same day: the logger holds two `NETLINK_NETFILTER` sockets, and the one subscribed to events is not the one `-b` sized.
- **What reaches that socket.** `nf_conntrack_events` was 2, the kernel's default: every connection on the box gets events while anyone listens, and conntrack filters out the non-TV ones in userspace after the kernel has queued them. The torrent client's churn, the VPN's and the resolver's own connections all compete with the TV for one small buffer. Under 2 a connection gets events only if a listener exists when it is created, so a TV connection opened while the logger restarts (every deploy, every crash) or before it starts at boot is never logged. Only the flow logger listens for conntrack events on the gateway.
- **The DNS gate's set.** From kernel 6.12 an `add element` that meets an element already in a timeout set moves its expiry only when the add carries one. The feeder sent `timeout 86400s` alone, so an address the TV resolved every hour still left the set a day after its first answer, and the next connection to it counted as one no lookup returned: half of [decision 22](#22-a-pass-that-proposes-nothing-is-remembered)'s gate noise. The gateway runs 6.18.

**Decision.**

1. **Events come from the firewall.** `tvgw-apply` loads a chain `ct_events` in `inet tvgw` (prerouting, priority -150, after conntrack and before the connection is confirmed) with one rule, `iifname TV_IF ip saddr TV_NET ct state new ct event set destroy`, in both uplink modes, and then sets `net.netfilter.nf_conntrack_events=0`: no connection makes events unless a rule asks, and the rule asks for the TV's. The order matters: switched first, TV connections opened between the two steps would have no events. `/etc/sysctl.d/60-tvgw.conf` ([deploy/sysctl](../deploy/sysctl/README.md)) holds the switch with `nf_conntrack_acct` and `nf_conntrack_timestamp`, and `install.sh` installs it. A kernel built without conntrack events has neither the switch nor the statement; there the apply leaves the rule out and warns, because nft would refuse the whole table and keep the TV dark for a flow log that cannot work anyway. `-b` leaves the unit; `--orig-src` stays as the second filter.
2. **The drops are a number in the store.** `tvgw verify --record` finds the logger's event socket through `/proc` (the socket inodes in `/proc/<pid>/fd` of the unit's main process and its children, matched against the `Inode` column of `/proc/net/netlink`, protocol 12, non-zero `Groups`; `src/tvgw/flowlog.py`) and stores its `Drops` in `fw_counters` as the series `flowlog: event socket drops`. The ingest warns when it grew since its last run; a lower count is a restarted logger and all of it is new. The brief keeps the series out of the firewall's evidence. A new check, `flowlog_events`, fails when the switch is 0 and the rule is missing (a flow log that records nothing), when the logger has no process, or when it holds no event socket; it warns when the switch is not 0.
3. **The gate refreshes.** `dnsgate.batch` sends `{ip} timeout {ttl}s expires {ttl}s`. `tvgw verify` warns when `resolved4` holds more than half its size. A warning is a new level, `WARN`, printed by `verify` and carried in `--json`: it fails no check, no unit and no deploy.

**Consequences.** The logger's socket hears the TV and nothing else, so the torrent client can no longer cost the TV its evidence. A TV connection opened while the logger is down is logged when it ends, as long as the logger is back by then, because its event extension now comes from the firewall rather than from a listener being present. The drop count survives a reboot, which the journal's `ENOBUFS` lines may not. The gate holds every address the TV was handed in the last day; the expected effect is a lower gate count on viewing days with `dns_gate.unexplained` unchanged, judged against the two weeks of counts before the deploy. The costs:

- The switch applies to the whole network namespace: any other program that listened for conntrack events would see only the TV's connections. None did on 2026-11-01; `awk '$2==12 && $4!="00000000"' /proc/net/netlink` lists the listeners.
- While `inet tvgw` is absent (stopped, a failed apply) new TV connections make no events. Forwarding is off then, so this costs only the TV's connections to the Pi itself.
- A rollback to a release older than this one needs `sudo sysctl -w net.netfilter.nf_conntrack_events=2` and `sudo rm /etc/sysctl.d/60-tvgw.conf`: the old script leaves the switch at 0 and has no rule, and its flow log would record nothing ([rolling back the code](project/deploying.md#rolling-back-the-code)).
- On a kernel older than 6.12 `expires` changes nothing; the first expiry is kept, as before.
- A `WARN` is seen in `tvgw verify`'s output and its journal only; the brief does not carry it.

Not done here: the lossless listener of deep dive 06 F1c (a Python reader with `NETLINK_BROADCAST_ERROR` and its own 8 MB buffer, exact kernel timestamps), which is a new root service with `CAP_NET_ADMIN` and needs the owner's approval; and the one-line upstream fix to conntrack-tools (`event_sock` for `sock` in the event path), which is for the owner to send.

**Owner decisions.** None held behind a setting: the change is to what the gateway records, not to what the TV may reach.

## 44. The tunnel is a setting, and the Pi's own leaks are counted

*2026-11-01, from the November research (sections 10 and 16 item 10; deep dive 12).*

**Context.** `tvgw-apply` named the VPN's interface `tun0` in two places, the vpn mode's uplink and the input chain's "nothing unsolicited from the VPN side", and the NetworkManager dispatcher named it a third time. PIA's desktop client 3.8.0 (2026-10-16) makes WireGuard, whose interface is `wgpia0`, the default for new installations, so a reinstall or a settings reset would silently end the drop and leave vpn mode dark. Measured on the gateway on 2026-11-01: PIA runs OpenVPN on `tun0`, and `piactl` exists. Read from PIA's source (deep dive 12): its kill switch filters only the Pi's own traffic, in iptables' `OUTPUT`, and tvgw's rule 60 routes the TV before PIA's forwarded-packet rule 70, so the TV's data path never depends on the tunnel. The Pi's own traffic does: under `killswitch=auto` AdGuard's DoH is rejected while the tunnel reconnects, but leaves by the uplink after a daemon start before the first connection, after `piactl disconnect` and while the daemon is not active. Nothing counted that. Decisions 1 and 9 credited the tunnel with hiding the TV's DNS from the ISP, which DoH by address does.

**Decision.**

1. **`VPN_IF`**, a setting in `config.Settings` and the template: `auto` (the default) takes `tun0` if it exists at the apply, else `wgpia0`; `none` says the Pi runs no VPN client; any other value is the tunnel's interface name, and may not be `lo`, `TV_IF` or `WAN_IF`. `UPLINK=vpn` sends the TV out the tunnel it resolves to, or `tun0` before one exists (the boot apply runs before the VPN connects, as before), and refuses `VPN_IF=none`. The dispatcher re-applies when the tunnel comes up: both PIA names under `auto`, the named one otherwise, none under `none`. `tvgw verify` expects the route that `tvgw-apply` would build (`config.uplink_interface`). The input chain's drop covers `tun0` and `wgpia0` whatever the setting, and a third named tunnel beside them, so no value can open the Pi to the tunnel side.
2. **The Pi's leaks are counted, never dropped.** A chain `post_leaks` in `inet tvgw` (postrouting, priority `srcnat - 20`) counts new connections from a source outside `TV_NET` leaving by `WAN_IF` to `@doh4` on TCP 443 (`pi leak: DoH left by the uplink`) and to port 53 (`pi leak: DNS left by the uplink`). Postrouting sees a packet after every output-hook verdict, PIA's included, so the counters count what really left; before the masquerade, so the TV's own packets are never counted. With `VPN_IF=none` the chain is empty.
3. **The brief says so.** `tvgw verify --record` stores the VPN boundary (`VPN_IF`, the tunnel it resolved to, and PIA's own `piactl get connectionstate`, run under a 5-second timeout and kept only when its first line is one of piactl's connection states) in `meta`, and, when the run saw a tunnel or a state, the time in `vpn_seen_t`. The brief shows `PIA <state>` on the Coverage line, keeps the leak counters out of Firewall held and out of the analyst's pack, and raises the red line `the Pi's own lookups left outside the VPN: ...` when either counter rose in the window on a gateway that has a VPN: the last run found a tunnel or PIA's state, `VPN_IF` names one, or a run in the 30 days before the window's end saw one (`vpn_seen_t`), and `VPN_IF` is not `none`. The last clause is the one the leaks need: while the PIA daemon is not active there is no tunnel and `piactl` answers nothing, so the last run alone would read as a gateway without a VPN exactly while DoH leaves by the uplink. The line is about the gateway's health (`analyst.HEALTH_RED_LINES`), so it buys no analyst request.
4. The texts of [decision 1](#1-the-tv-uses-the-direct-uplink-not-the-vpn) and [decision 9](#9-doh-upstreams-by-ip-address) are corrected, and the settings template says that `URL_FILTER_RESOLVER=1.1.1.1` cannot work behind PIA's kill switch.

**Consequences.** Nothing the TV may reach changes: the counters only count, and the drop only widens. A PIA client reinstalled with WireGuard keeps vpn mode and the drop working at the next apply. A leak that used to be a guess about PIA's state machine becomes a number in the brief, with PIA's state on the Coverage line. The red line itself names no VPN client and no state, since red lines travel in the analyst's pack. The costs:

- The port-53 counter counts every new connection from the Pi to port 53 leaving by the uplink. That includes the Pi's lookups to the home router while PIA is disconnected, which is a leak, and the VPN client's own transport if it ever uses remote port 53 (PIA's OpenVPN offers it), which is not; set PIA's remote port to another one if the line fires with the tunnel up.
- A gateway with `VPN_IF=auto` and no VPN client counts its own lookups every day but raises no red line, because no run ever saw a VPN; such a gateway should say `VPN_IF=none`. A gateway whose VPN client was removed keeps the line for up to 30 days after the last run that saw it, unless it says `VPN_IF=none`.
- PIA's state is the one the last `verify` run saw, as the AdGuard version is, not a history over the window.

Not done here, each a separate decision: `DNS_FALLBACK` (the lists workstream); a guard that defers AdGuard restarts while the upstream fails (12's first build item); the `tvgw-egress` namespace that would let a resolver and the GFTS fetches survive a tunnel outage (an owner decision; it changes decisions 1 and 9 again); and the hardware test that rule 60 sits below the VPN client's forwarded-packet rule.

**Owner decisions.** `VPN_IF`, default `auto`, which on this gateway resolves to `tun0`, exactly what was hardcoded. A gateway without a VPN client sets `none`. `killswitch=on` stays off until a forwarded fallback resolver exists (section 17 of the November research).

## 45. The ACR red line is an upload test and a port wall

**Context.** The brief's ACR red line (red line 7 in [the November report](research/state-of-the-art-2026-11.md#5-lgs-acr-on-the-wire-and-one-detector), 8 in [the brief's order](reference/brief-sections.md#red-lines)) fired on a 10 to 20 s timer with under 4 KB per connection (`ACR_PERIOD`, `ACR_PAYLOAD`). Rebuilt as conntrack sees it, the released *Watching the Watchers* LG trace trips that test on 0 of 17 days: LG's client opens a connection about every 60 s and uploads a fingerprint of about 22 KB every 14 to 15 s inside it, on TCP 4431, for 10 to 39 MB up a day at 7 to 17 times the bytes down ([deep dive 13, sections 1.1 to 1.4](research/deep-dive-2026-11/13-gap-reconcile-the-two-incompatible-acr-detec.md)). The 15 s timer is invisible between connection starts, and a 10 to 200 s timer also marks captive-portal probes, Hue polling and QoE beacons (15 other domains in the lab set). The same lab set used TCP 4001, 4431, 4445, 4447, 8180 and 8883 for ACR, ads and an IoT hub, and nothing legitimate; the CX has never sent a flow to any of them (measured on the gateway on 2026-11-01).

**Decision.**

- `queries.acr_uploader` replaces the cadence test. A cell is the registrable domain of the connection's TLS name, or the server's /24 when no hello named it, and the server port: the port keeps Alphonso's port-443 control plane from diluting the fingerprint cell's ratio. A cell fires on a local day with at least 1 MB of payload up, at least 4 times the payload down and at least 24 connections (`ACR_DAILY`), or on a clock hour with 500 KB, 4 times and 5 connections (`ACR_HOURLY`), which catches a partial day. Flows count by `t_start`, only to public servers, and only when their name is outside the profile's expected categories or there is no name. One line per cell and day: the day when it passes, else its busiest hour, with the count of hours that passed. The line reads `ACR-shaped upload: <cell>:<port> sent <X> MB up (<R>x down) in <N> connections <on day | in hour HH of day>; Live Plus reads <state>`.
- `ACR_PERIOD` and `ACR_PAYLOAD` are deleted, the report key `acr_cadence` is now `acr_upload`, and `beacons()` and `PERIODIC_JITTER` stay. A periodic row is an attribute: it is marked `ACR-like` only when its name and port are an ACR-shaped cell, it never raises a red line, and `analyst.novelty` no longer counts it ([decision 22](#22-a-pass-that-proposes-nothing-is-remembered): a mark that recurs by construction must not buy a request).
- `tvgw-apply` resets TCP 4001, 4431, 4445, 4447, 8180 and 8883 from the TV in `tv_out`, after the manual IP blocks and before the LG 8080 line and the odd-port rules, with a log rule of its own (`limit rate 6/minute`, prefix `tvgw-adport: `) and `counter reject with tcp reset comment "LG adtech port"`, in both uplink modes, behind `ADTECH_PORT_WALL=yes|no` (default `yes`). Its counter's increase in the window, floored at the samples logged, is the line's second form: `ACR port attempts: <N> connections to TCP <ports> rejected (<names>)`, each sampled address named from the A answers of the 7 days before. The samples land in a table of their own, `adport` (schema 9), not in `oddport`, so "Unexpected ports" stays the list of unknown ports [decision 17](#17-strict_ports-stays-off-until-two-clean-weeks) is measured by.
- Fixtures for all of it come from published summary statistics only (`tests/unit/acr_synth.py`), never from the CC BY-NC-ND trace.

**Consequences.**

- The wall defends little: on the CX, DNS already blocks every host behind these ports, AWS IoT and Azure IoT Hub fall back to 443, and Alphonso's provisioning host already uses 443 for two connections in three. Its value is naming an attempt DNS missed (a hardcoded address, an unseen resolver, a new host on an old port). A rejected SYN never confirms its conntrack entry, so the counter and the samples are the only record. A firmware whose ACR client falls back to 443 after a reset would show only as the upload line.
- The counter counts packets. The reset ends an attempt at its first SYN, so a packet is about one attempt, and the line says "connections".
- Thresholds are the lab set's: 15 of 17 ACR days and 0 of 447 other day cells for the daily test, and 16 of 17 days with 8 out-of-scope streaming hours for the hourly one. YouTube reached 77% of the ratio threshold; the expected categories keep it out of scope, and an unnamed streaming cell stays under the 4x ratio. An upload-heavy telemetry hour on a name outside the expected categories fires the hourly test, and the line says what it saw, not that it is ACR.
- `adport` rows are pruned with `DB_DAYS` like `oddport` and `gate`, and keep the kernel's raw line as those tables do.
- With `STRICT_PORTS=yes` these ports would be rejected anyway; the wall stays a rule of its own so "the TV tried an ad-tech port" (a red line) is kept apart from "the TV tried an unknown port" (a breakage risk).
- The ports come from a newer US LG on a newer webOS. They are strong support for decision 17's end state and are not the CX's own evidence: the two weeks of the CX's briefs still decide `STRICT_PORTS`.

**Owner decisions.**

- `ADTECH_PORT_WALL`, default `yes`: the CX has never used these ports, so the wall costs nothing today and makes any attempt visible. `no` returns the ports to the odd-port rules. A firmware that needs one of them shows as the ACR port line and a broken feature; the runbook says to turn the wall off and tell the project.
- The FAST category is expected: LG Channels' delivery names (Amagi, Wurl's LG host, NBCU and Stingray) no longer reach the brief's allowed-and-unexpected list or the upload test, and `amagi.tv` is in `never_block_apex`. Amagi's names carry channel and programme slugs, so they are a viewing record in the store; how long the store keeps them is the retention work's question, not this one's.

## 46. The blocklists are watched, and AdGuard changes only on purpose

*Decided 2026-11-01, with the lists workstream (state-of-the-art report 2026-11, sections 4, 6 and 16 items 5 and 6).*

**Context.** The blocklists in `[adguard] filters` were the one part of the gateway's policy that changed without a review: AdGuard Home re-downloads each one every 24 hours from its last attempt and, at the v0.107.79 tag, swaps in whatever answers `200 OK`, an empty body included. HaGeZi's 2026-09-11 `nextlgsdp.com` push broke clocks and the Content Store on newer webOS sets for about a day. tvgw did not own `filters_update_interval`, so a `0` in the web UI would have frozen every list silently. Separately, AdGuard Home bumped its YAML schema five times in eight months and opened a 1.x line whose minor releases may break compatibility; `tvgw adguard apply` validated its own candidate with the installed binary but nothing checked a new binary against the live YAML, and the web UI could replace the binary in place. And every AdGuard restart empties the cache the TV lives on during an upstream outage, while the analyst, the hourly check, `tvgw undo` and a deploy all restart it.

**Decision.**

- **Watch the update channel.** The plan owns `filters_update_interval: 24`. `tvgw verify` asks the resolver for each subscribed HaGeZi list's sentinel and expects `0.0.0.0` (`list_sentinels`). The brief's List changes names the names a list blocked for the first time that the TV had resolved in the 30 days before, with their 14-day shape, and a red line when the name carried content by decision 20's payload limits read in reverse (over 2 MB down, or a median over 16 KB per connection); and the names a list stopped blocking. All of it is read from the store; no list text is kept.
- **Own the evidence and cache keys** a UI edit or a schema bump could change: `querylog.ignored_enabled` and `statistics.ignored_enabled` (`false`), `dns.cache_enabled` (`true`), the optimistic cache's `cache_optimistic_answer_ttl` (`30s`) and `cache_optimistic_max_age` (`12h`), and `filtering.rewrites_enabled` (`true`). `blocking_mode: default` becomes a tested invariant: a unit test on the plan, and the real v0.107.79 binary in the namespace CI must answer a blocked HTTPS query with an empty NOERROR without an SOA, a blocked SVCB query with NODATA, and a blocked name with `0.0.0.0`.
- **A schema ceiling and an upgrade path.** `apply` and `upgrade` refuse a YAML above schema 34. The floor rises to v0.107.79 in `verify.ADGUARD_MIN`, `install.sh` and the preinst. The service runs with `--no-check-update` through a drop-in `install.sh` writes with a health check and a restore. `tvgw adguard upgrade NEWBIN` is the one way the binary changes: the new binary's own `--check-config` on a copy of the live YAML, then the swap with backups, the readiness wait and `tvgw verify`, and a rollback of binary and YAML on failure.
- **A restart guard.** Before any restart, the code asks the resolver for a fresh random label under a must-allow name, so no cache can answer for the upstreams. The first try listens past AdGuard's own `dns.upstream_timeout` plus 2 seconds, because an upstream that drops packets makes AdGuard wait that long before its SERVFAIL. SERVFAIL, or silence on every try while AdGuard answers a name the profile's own rules block, raises `adguardcfg.Deferred` (`deferred: upstream down`), a `RuntimeError`, before anything is staged; every caller reports it and none retries. AdGuard silent on both does not defer, because an apply may be what repairs AdGuard. `apply`, `upgrade` and the service drop-in hold one lock.
- **Settings, defaults unchanged.** `QUERYLOG_DAYS` and `STATS_DAYS` (90, the value both intervals had) feed AdGuard's two intervals; `DNS_FALLBACK` (empty) feeds `fallback_dns` with a second operator, encrypted and by address.

**Owner decisions** (held behind a setting or not built):

- `DNS_FALLBACK`, default empty. Setting it lets a second operator see the TV's new lookups while the upstreams fail; it does not help during a VPN tunnel outage.
- `QUERYLOG_DAYS` and `STATS_DAYS`, default 90. The retention report proposes 72 hours and 168 hours; that is the owner's call with the rest of the retention inventory.
- Stage A (snapshots of each list's file and an effective diff on the names that matter, two new tables, about 50 MB of disk) and Stage B (fetching, holding and promoting list versions to local files AdGuard reads) are designed in [AdGuard Home](how-it-works/adguard.md#not-built-yet-snapshots-and-staged-promotion) and not built. Stage B changes where AdGuard reads its lists, and waits for the owner's approval and the namespace test that proves an empty `200` empties a list.

**Consequences.** A list update that takes away something the TV used shows up in the next brief, and one that took away content is a red line; a list that went empty fails `tvgw verify` within ten minutes. A newer AdGuard Home than tvgw knows is refused, not half-configured, and the binary no longer changes behind the gateway's back. An apply during an upstream outage no longer costs the TV its cache: autonomous blocks, reverts and undos wait instead: a deferred block waits in the queue, and a deferred revert or undo stays in AdGuard until the next hourly check re-applies it (`autoblock.push_owed`). The guard's probe sends a made-up name to the upstream at each apply. A renamed sentinel upstream is a false `FAIL` until `verify.SENTINELS` follows it. The first deploy restarts AdGuard once more for the drop-in.

*Note (2026-11-05), from research pass 4: a released AdGuard Home bug, and a TV that never asks.* AdGuard's changelog lists, for its next release and in no released one, the fix of an invalid SVCB record in its answer to a resolver-discovery (DDR) query when no DoH routes are configured, which is the gateway's case, since `dns.handle_ddr` is on and AdGuard serves no encrypted DNS ([AdGuard Home's CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/AdguardTeam/AdGuardHome/refs/heads/master/CHANGELOG.md), read 2026-11-05; v0.107.80, the pin since this date, does not carry it). In the reference gateway's whole store, read the same day (2026-10-25 to 2026-11-05), the CX never looked up `resolver.arpa`, `use-application-dns.net` or the Private Relay names (the November research's test T8, [decision 6](#6-adguard-home-is-configured-as-code)'s note of the same day), so that answer has not reached it. The test as written, asking the gateway's own resolver for `_dns.resolver.arpa` type SVCB, has still not run; a rule for those names is worth writing only if its answer is not empty. Test T5, whether any of the TV's UDP 443 is the old Google QUIC the capture filter does not keep, is still owed.*

## 47. The gateway keeps the least of its own record that the detectors need

**Context.** The gateway records the TV to protect the household, and that record is itself the most detailed account of the household's viewing anywhere: a year of minute-level facts by default, up to eight full copies of it on the card, twelve weeks of flow logs, an hourly app log that was never rotated, and a journal whose span was whatever the Pi's other drop-ins said. The research on the gateway's own record (deep dive 15, report section 12) found that no detector on this version reads raw rows more than two weeks old, that the kernel log lines kept the Pi's host name and the frames' MAC headers for no reader, that query strings are the part of a request closest to the contents of a communication, and that FAST channels put the show's title in the name the TV looks up. On the gateway, the journal is persistent with a three-month span from the owner's own `50-persistent.conf`.

**Decision.** Keep less wherever no evidence is lost, and build a lever with today's default wherever something is traded.

- On now, losing nothing: logrotate keeps 2 weekly copies of the flow and URL-filter logs, 1 of the TLS-passthrough log, which is never ingested, and 35 daily copies of `tvcheck-history.log`; the stanzas name each log, since a glob would name `gfts-tls.log` twice; `install.sh` removes the copies past each new count once, which logrotate never does. Ingest stores kernel log lines without the host name and with `MAC=` emptied, and rewrites the stored rows once.
- The journal: `install.sh` writes `/etc/systemd/journald.conf.d/60-tvgw.conf` with `Storage=persistent`, `MaxRetentionSec=` `JOURNAL_DAYS` days (14) and `MaxFileSec=1day`, since journald deletes only whole archived files and its default month per file would keep an entry up to a month past the span. It restarts journald only when the file changed, which keeps every service's log stream, and prints a note naming any other file that sets another span and which one is in effect.
- `tvgw forget` (`src/tvgw/forget.py`) deletes every viewing-derived row and file and keeps the gateway. Every table and file tree is classified as data, a CI test fails when a new one is not, and an unknown entry is kept and reported at run time. Rows are deleted with `secure_delete` and the file vacuumed; a floor in `meta` makes every ingest of the next 7 days drop late evidence of the forgotten time, which covers conntrack's 5-day timeout for an established connection, and then goes.
- Levers, off by default: `URI_VALUES=lengths` stores query values as their lengths, keeping `biz_code`; `NAME_HMAC=on` stores names under the profile's title-bearing zones as keyed tags. The zones are the plain patterns of the profile's `FAST` category, read if it exists, so the zone list is profile data and not code. The keys live in `/var/lib/tvgw/name-keys/` rather than `/etc/tvgw/`, because the ingest unit runs under `ProtectSystem=strict` and may write only the state and log directories; `tvgw backup` copies only the store file and `make backup` pulls only `backup/`, so the keys are outside every backup either way.

**Owner decisions**, each held behind a setting at today's value:

1. `DB_DAYS`, 365. 90 keeps a quarter of weekly reports reproducible at a quarter of the store and backup size (estimate); 35 covers every detector the research proposes once its nameless rollups exist, and this version does not build them.
2. `QUERYLOG_DAYS`, which the AdGuard workstream adds. tvgw reads the query log every hour and needs only a few days of slack, and the log can hold twice its interval.
3. `URI_VALUES`, `keep`. `lengths` loses only what a request asked for, which no detector and no pack reads.
4. `NAME_HMAC`, `off`. `on` loses show titles in the brief; detectors keep equality within a 90-day epoch. The analyst's pack, the judgments and the proposals then name tags, and the autonomy's gate refuses a block of a tagged name, since a rule for a tag never matches a real lookup: a title zone is blocked whole, by the owner.
5. `ANALYST_WEEKLY_MODEL`, `claude-fable-5-1`. Fable 5.1 requires 30-day retention and is not eligible for zero data retention without Anthropic's authorization; `claude-opus-5-5` is on standard retention, deleted within 30 days and eligible for zero data retention by agreement; the Batch API keeps 29 days. The verifier is on Fable too.
6. `JOURNAL_DAYS`, 14, which shortens the owner's own three-month journal for every service on the Pi. 92 keeps the span the owner chose; `off` leaves the journal to the owner's drop-ins.

**Consequences.** Up to ten weeks of flow and filter logs go at the first deploy, and the journal past `JOURNAL_DAYS` at journald's next rotation. A Pi off for longer than a rotation loses those lines, as it did before. After a forget the brief, the analyst and the judge start from nothing, and the hardware suite's data checks need an hour of the TV in use. The rollups that would let `DB_DAYS` fall to 35 are not built, and `tvgw forget` has no per-day or per-name form: the research's tombstone design (deep dive 15, F6) is the next step if the household asks for one.

## 48. One ledger of changes, one hour classifier, and a second judge that only watches

*Proposed by the November research (sections 11 and 13, recommendations 8 and 9; section files 14 and 16), built 2026-11-01. Judge v2 decides nothing until the owner adopts it.*

**Context.** The hourly judge of [decision 20](#20-the-gateway-blocks-on-its-own-under-three-watches) asks whether streaming per TV hour fell and blames the newest machine rule for any yes. On this gateway a 24-hour trial always holds other changes: every machine-rule apply, revert and undo restarts AdGuard Home; the NetworkManager dispatcher re-runs `tvgw-apply` on every uplink, TV-port and VPN event and on each DHCP renewal; deploys arrive through CI; PIA reconnects; the household watches HDMI inputs, which are `Active` hours with no streaming at all. Nothing recorded these, so the judge could not tell "the network got worse" or "the TV was on an input" from "the block broke it". Two flaws in its arithmetic make that matter: a revert on any one low hour is a minimum over n hours (at a 10% chance per innocent hour, 4 TV hours give a 34% false-revert rate), and a trial with too few TV hours passes. The TV's state itself is read once an hour, so a short session falls between two readings, and the reading never asked for the consent record.

**Decision.**

- **A change ledger** (`changes`, schema 9): one append-only row per change, class A when it can move what the judge measures, class B when it cannot, with no address in it. Three shell writers append one line each to `/var/lib/tvgw/changes.spool` and never fail for it: `tvgw-apply` (its caller, and whether it touched routing), the dispatcher (every event on `WAN_IF`, `TV_IF` and the VPN interface) and `install.sh` (`tvgw changes --note-deploy`: the version and fingerprints of the code and of the modules that define the metric). The hourly ingest loads the spool exactly once, copies the machine rules' adds and verdicts from `auto_rules`, and derives events from the gateway samples.
- **A sampler** in `tvgw verify --record` (`gw_samples`, every 10 minutes): boot id, carrier changes, interface bytes, the VPN interface, PIA's state, Wi-Fi station fields, conntrack's count, maximum and drops, AdGuard's `InvocationID` and `NRestarts`, list file times. Each source optional, each command bounded by 5 seconds, and a failure never fails `verify`. Events derived from two samples carry `t_precision` 600.
- **An hour classifier**, `ledger.classify_hour()`: `off`, `no_demand`, `confounded` or `clean` over [h - 10 min, h + 60 min), from the readings, the expected-category starts and lookups, the class-A changes, the samples and the measurement's own faults. One function feeds judge v2, the calibration, the brief and the analyst.
- **"Changes in effect"** in the brief, after the red lines and before the tables and the machine rules: class-A changes with their times, class B as counts, the trials with both judges' verdicts, and the hour classes. A red line when a trial has spent half of v2's ceiling short of its clean hours.
- **Judge v2 in shadow** (`judge2.py`, `judge_shadow`): strict pass (K clean demand hours, `JUDGE2_CLEAN_HOURS`), soft revert (two consecutive low demand hours revert when both are clean and suspend when one is confounded), NODATA and extension to a 14-day ceiling instead of a blind pass, hard red lines in any hour. `tvgw autoblock check` writes both judges' verdicts every hour, after the shipped judge has decided and within a 120 s budget; nothing reads them to decide. `tvgw autoblock calibrate --arms A,B,C,D --by-class` (C with prorated bytes, D with the shadow's end-time bytes) replays the store's history as placebo trials and measures each design's false-revert rate and power.
- **The TV's state.** The hourly reading also stores the consent record, every `eulaStatus` flag as `eula.<flag>` (`unknown` when the TV leaves it out, never off), and `bleAdvertisingOnOff`, recorded only. A flag going from false to true is a red line; BLE advertising never is. `tvgw tvprobe --hours N` holds one read-only session over the existing pairing, subscribed to the power state and the foreground app, with tvgw's own WebSocket client capped at 1 MiB a frame, at least 5 s between attempts, nothing tried without carrier, and no registration retry after a pairing error.

**Consequences.** The judge's specificity becomes measurable before anything about the judge changes: two weeks of shadow verdicts and one calibration say how often the shipped judge reverts a rule that did no harm (arm A's alpha), and how often a single-hour rule would revert on an input hour (arm B's `revert_hour_no_demand`) or a VPN outage. <!-- (fin-release) --> The gateway runs four more read-only commands every 10 minutes (`piactl`, `iw`, `conntrack -S`, `systemctl show`) and keeps about 144 sample rows a day under the store's retention. Every shell writer gains one line whose failure is ignored, so the deploy path, the apply and the dispatcher behave as before. A 10-minute sample dates an event only to its interval, and the classifier counts such an event in every hour it may have touched, so it errs toward `confounded`. The classifier's thresholds and the input app ids (`com.webos.app.hdmi*`, `livetv`) are starting values to be checked on this TV. The probe's file is a viewing record at app granularity: it stays root-only and is deleted after its report.

## 49. The gateway answers the TV through the TV port: table 100 carries the TV subnet

*Built 2026-11-03, closing the gap [decision 27](#27-a-namespace-copy-of-the-gateway-in-ci) recorded. Its owner decision (deploy in the gateway's quiet window, say in the v0.2.0 release notes what that release needs, and cut the patch release from v0.2.0 with this change alone) is answered yes by the owner's standing instruction of 2026-11-03 not to stop for questions.*

**Context.** Rule 60 (`from <TV_NET> lookup 100`, [decision 1](#1-the-tv-uses-the-direct-uplink-not-the-vpn)) matches by source address, so it catches what the gateway itself sends to the TV from `GW_IP` as well as what the TV sends through it: AdGuard Home's DNS answers, which leave from the address the TV asked, nginx's half of every connection to the URL filter, and the ICMP of a reject. Table 100 held only `default via <WAN_GW> dev <WAN_IF>`, so on a gateway with nothing else those packets left by the uplink and never reached the TV: the TV had an address and no DNS, and `tvgw verify` reported OK, since no check asked where the gateway's own packets to the TV go. The reference gateway answered the TV only because its VPN client's rule at priority 50 looks up the main table, where the kernel's connected route for the TV subnet lives, before rule 60 is reached. The virtual gateway found the gap and recorded it as a strict xfail with a helper route, without changing the production routing ([decision 27](#27-a-namespace-copy-of-the-gateway-in-ci)). v0.2.0 shipped with it.

**Decision.**

- **The route.** In direct mode `tvgw-apply` keeps `<TV_NET> dev <TV_IF> scope link src <GW_IP>` in table 100 beside the default route, compared before it is written like the rule and the default route, because rewriting an unchanged route destroyed live connections ([the 2026-10-30 incident](incidents.md#2026-10-30-live-connections-lost-their-conntrack-entry-at-every-dhcp-renewal)). A route on a port without carrier prints with a trailing `linkdown`, and still matches. While `GW_IP` is not on `TV_IF` (the boot apply before `tv-lan` is up) the write fails; the apply prints `tvgw: <TV_IF> not up yet; the dispatcher adds the TV subnet to table 100` and goes on, and the dispatcher's apply on `TV_IF` up writes it. In vpn mode rule 60 is removed and the main table answers the TV, so nothing is added. The stop path is unchanged and leaves table 100 as it is.
- **The check.** `tvgw verify` gains `gw_reply_route`: `ip -j route get <TV_IP> from <GW_IP>` must name `TV_IF`, in both modes. It is a FAIL from the start, where other new checks begin as warnings, because it fails only where the TV has no DNS, and it lands with the route that makes it pass. Its detail names interfaces only, never the route's addresses.
- **The proof.** The unit suite runs `tvgw-apply` against a stubbed `ip`: one write when the route is absent or differs, none when it is there, with or without `linkdown`, and an apply that finishes when the write fails. The virtual suite runs with no helper route: each scenario starts with the TV subnet taken out of table 100, so the gateway's own answers reach the TV by the apply alone. The hardware suite checks the verdict on the reference, and that rule 60 and both table-100 routes already read as the next apply wants them.

**Consequences.** A gateway with no VPN client answers the TV. On the reference one route is added to table 100 once; the VPN client's rule 50 still matches first, so the TV's packets and the gateway's answers take the same path as before, and every later apply changes no routing (the change ledger's `unchanged`). The route survives the TV being off, because `tv-lan` keeps its address without carrier; re-activating `tv-lan` takes `GW_IP` off the port, the kernel removes the route with it, and the dispatcher's apply puts it back. It is a routing change on the live gateway, so its commit carries `Deploy-window: tv-off` and deploys with the TV off. To undo it, revert the change, or run `sudo ip route del <TV_NET> table 100` until the next apply. v0.2.0 answers the TV only on a gateway whose VPN client adds its own routing rule, and its release notes say so.

*Note (2026-11-04), from the virtual suite's QUIC row: a reject's refusal never took table 100, and the kernel's ICMP budget is no route's.* The context above counts the ICMP of a reject among what rule 60 caught. It did not: with `net.ipv4.icmp_errors_use_inbound_ifaddr` at its default, the kernel routes the refusal of a forwarded packet before it has a source address, so a rule that matches by source never sees it, and the main table's route to the TV subnet takes it. What table 100 lost were the packets that leave from `GW_IP`, the resolver's answers and nginx's replies. `tests/virtual/test_virtual_gateway.py` shows both on a kernel: with the TV subnet out of table 100, the QUIC probe is still refused at once, while nginx's half of the handshake is lost and `gw_reply_route` fails. The check reads the stricter of the two paths and stands as decided; with the sysctl set, the refusal takes the very lookup it reads. What no route check can see is the kernel's per-destination ICMP limit (`net.ipv4.icmp_ratelimit`, one a second with a burst of six). A TV refused more than six times within a few seconds by rules that answer with ICMP (DoT and DoQ, QUIC, the manual blocks, `STRICT_PORTS`) gets silence for the rest, a drop in effect, until the budget refills; the resets (DoH, the adtech port wall) are not limited. The matrix met it once pytest 9.2's order put nine such refusals within about two seconds, and the kernel's own counter (`OutRateLimitHost`) named it. The suite's gateway namespace now has the limit off, so each row is judged by its own probe. tvgw leaves the gateway's limit at the kernel's default: the TV is what the gateway contains, and the limit keeps it from making the gateway send ICMP without bound.

## 52. Setup fetches AdGuard Home's own release, pinned and hash-checked, and seeds it without the wizard

*Decided 2026-11-03 under the owner's standing instruction of that day (the November improvement design's owner decisions D2 and D3, yes when he is away). The module, the setting and the commands were built the same day; setup's own AdGuard step comes separately.*

**Context.** [Decision 25](#25-adguard-home-is-a-checked-dependency-never-bundled) made AdGuard Home a checked dependency that the owner installs with AdGuard's own script and finishes in its first-run web wizard, which [decision 6](#6-adguard-home-is-configured-as-code) called the one manual step. The wizard is the step a typical user cannot do: it needs an ssh tunnel and a browser, and on a fresh computer it listens on `0.0.0.0:3000`, with no password, until someone answers it. Its usual answer, every interface, also makes AdGuard answer the whole home network, and on Ubuntu its wildcard collides with systemd-resolved's stub on `127.0.0.53`. The wizard is an HTTP API (`/control/install/check_config`, `/control/install/configure`), and `--web-addr` decides where AdGuard listens on every start, the first one included. Every release publishes a SHA-256 for each asset in its `checksums.txt`, and GitHub's API reports the same digest.

**Decision.**

- **One release, pinned.** `src/tvgw/aghinstall.py` names one AdGuard Home release (`PIN`, v0.107.79) with the SHA-256 of its `linux_arm64` and `linux_amd64` assets. `tvgw adguard install` downloads the asset for the computer and keeps it only on that hash, unpacks only plain files and directories under `AdGuardHome/`, and requires the binary to report at least the floor. The package still carries none of AdGuard: tvgw fetches AdGuard's own release and bundles nothing. `tvgw adguard upgrade --fetch` reaches the same release through the upgrade path of [decision 46](#46-the-blocklists-are-watched-and-adguard-changes-only-on-purpose). CI's namespace job reads the pin from the module.
- **No wizard, nothing exposed.** The first run starts in a transient unit with `--web-addr 127.0.0.1:<random port>`, and tvgw answers the wizard itself: the page on `127.0.0.1:3000`, DNS on `127.0.0.1:53`, one user with a random password kept in `/etc/tvgw/adguard-admin`, root only. The YAML must then hold exactly that user, and the password must open the page, because the wizard asks for no password and a local process could answer it first. AdGuard's own `-s install --no-check-update` registers the service. A failure undoes only what that run created and keeps the binary. `AdGuardHome.yaml` is written by AdGuard's wizard API once and by `tvgw adguard apply` afterwards, never by hand.
- **tvgw's AdGuard is known by its content.** `aghinstall.state()` calls an AdGuard tvgw's when tvgw's marker is there, or when its YAML holds what `tvgw adguard apply` writes: the TV's client entry, or the profile's must-allow exceptions. Every gateway set up by hand under decision 25 matches, so none is mistaken for someone else's. An AdGuard with none of these is `foreign`. tvgw refuses to install over it, since taking it over replaces filtering for every device that uses it; running `tvgw adguard apply` on it is the explicit yes.
- **`ADGUARD_LISTEN`.** `keep`, which is what an absent key means, leaves `dns.bind_hosts` and `dns.port` to AdGuard, and `install.sh` writes AdGuard's drop-in as before. `gateway`, which the template sets for new installs, makes `tvgw adguard apply` own them as `GW_IP` and `127.0.0.1` on port 53, and orders AdGuard after the network with a restart every 5 seconds, without a limit, until `GW_IP` exists. The drop-in follows the setting, never what the YAML holds.

**Consequences.** A new gateway gets a resolver that answers the TV and the gateway itself and nothing else, with no browser step and no moment in which an open wizard faces the home network. A tvgw release now names an AdGuard release: moving the pin is a reviewed change with its two hashes, and `SCHEMA_CEILING` is looked at with it. The download needs GitHub and the system's certificates; without them the install stops before anything is written. The password file is one more secret on the gateway, readable by root only, and no other module reads it, so it reaches no backup, report or pack. AdGuard's documentation says systemd-resolved prevents a bind to `127.0.0.1:53`; `tests/virtual/test_virtual_adguard_listen.py` checks the two-address plan on a CI runner where resolved runs, and if the documentation is right there, the plan must own `GW_IP` alone. The owner's gateway does not change: its settings have no `ADGUARD_LISTEN`, so `keep`; its AdGuard reads as tvgw's by content; its drop-in and its plan stay byte for byte what they were. Hardware tests check the first two and `tvgw adguard diff` the third.

## 53. Releases carry a hash-checked bootstrap for two architectures, with provenance, under immutable releases; updates are checked weekly, installed only by the user, judged by verify and rolled back; a new settings key never changes the meaning of an old key's value

*Decided 2026-11-03, from the improvement pass's research on the install and the bootstrap's trust chain. The release half was built on 2026-11-03; `tvgw upgrade` and the weekly update check are not built.*

**Context.** The 0.2.0 release carried one arm64 .deb and its `SHA256SUMS`, attached by the job that built them, which held `contents: write` beside a build that runs pip and a source archive's setup code. Installing it meant reading [packaging](project/packaging.md), answering debconf by hand and installing AdGuard Home first; a mini PC had no package, although `build-deb.sh` could already make an amd64 one. Nothing tied a downloaded file to the workflow that built it, and a release's files could be replaced after it was published, so an installer that carried a package's hash could be replaced together with the package. On a fresh system, apt starts the nginx and dnsmasq it installs during the transaction, which can fail the install where something already holds port 53 or 80, and a first boot's unattended upgrades can hold apt's lock for minutes. The postinst cannot run `install.sh` without systemd, so no CI job had ever installed the package.

**Decision.**

1. Every release builds the .deb for arm64 and amd64 from the same steps, installs each in clean Debian 13 and Ubuntu 24.04 containers of its own architecture (images named by digest), and publishes nothing unless all four installs pass.
2. A release carries `get.sh`, rendered from `packaging/get.sh.in` with the version and both packages' SHA-256 written in, and listed in `SHA256SUMS`. It installs only a package whose hash equals the one it carries and whose `SHA256SUMS` line matches. Before it downloads anything it checks root, systemd, the system (Raspberry Pi OS 64-bit, Debian 12 or 13, Ubuntu 24.04 or 26.04) and the architecture, and refuses a tvgw installed from a checkout. While apt runs, a `policy-rc.d` of its own keeps the dependencies' services from starting, and an existing one is left alone; both apt calls wait up to 5 minutes for the lock. It asks nothing, hands over to `tvgw setup` on the terminal, and runs nothing when the download is cut short, because every step is inside `main`, called on its last line. Its address is the latest release's: `releases/latest/download/get.sh`.
3. A release is created as a draft, given every file, then published, marked latest only when no newer version is published. Immutable releases, a repository setting, are turned on before the first release that carries `get.sh`, so that neither the script nor a package can be replaced after publication. The packages and `get.sh` carry build provenance attestations, made by a job that holds `id-token: write` and `attestations: write` and runs only the repository's scripts and pinned actions; the job that writes the release holds `contents: write` alone and runs on a tag only.
4. A version's section of `CHANGELOG.md` starts with `### Highlights`, three to six bullets in plain words, and `### Status`, one paragraph on what was tested; they are the release page's text, and a tag whose section lacks either is refused before anything is built. Sections released before this decision keep their shape.
5. Updates, not built: `tvgw upgrade` installs a newer release only when the user runs it, judges the result with `tvgw verify` after the settling checks have had their time, and reinstalls the kept package on a `FAIL`. A weekly check, on new installs only (absent, the setting means off), stores the latest version for the brief and downloads nothing. Automatic installation is never a default.
6. Settings across versions: a new key's absent value means the behaviour from before the key existed, setup writes new values only on new installs, and a new key never changes the meaning of an old key's value. An older release reads a newer settings file as before, and the reference gateway keeps its behaviour through an upgrade and a rollback.

**Consequences.** One command installs tvgw on either architecture, and a tampered, truncated or wrong-architecture download installs nothing. The trust root is still GitHub: the account that can push a tag, and immutable releases once they are on. A release takes longer: two builds and four container installs come before it is published. The image digests are refreshed by hand, since dependabot does not watch them.

## 54. A TV's LAN API is a module the profile names; a reading never pairs; a profile declares what it can check

*2026-11-03, from the improvement design of that day (its sections 5.2 and 5.3, and finding F2 of its engineering review). The reading that never pairs is built; the reader named by the profile is not built yet.*

**Context.** The LAN-API library registers with the TV on every connection, and a registration without a stored key is the pairing prompt on the TV's screen. `lgwebos.read()` connected whatever the key file held. So the hourly `tvgw tvcheck` put the prompt on the screen every hour the TV was on, for an owner who had declined pairing and on a new gateway before setup had said what the prompt was, and the analyst's canary did the same. A key accepted from that prompt was written under `tvgw-analyze`'s `umask 027`, readable by the `adm` group until the next deploy's `chmod`, and with the library's manifest the key is a full remote-control credential. Setup's own pairing was the same `read()`. The reader is also LG's by name in three places: setup's `PAIRABLE_PROFILES`, `tvgw-analyze`'s `PROFILE = lg-webos` test and the Live Plus key in the queries, so another vendor's reader has nowhere to go.

**Decision.**

- **A reading never pairs.** `lgwebos.read()` first asks the key file, through the library's own storage, whether it holds a key for the TV's address (`lgwebos.has_key()`; a missing file is never opened, since opening creates it). With none it connects to nothing and returns `not paired`. `tvgw tvcheck` exits 4 for that, a new code. `tvgw-analyze` treats 4 as it treats 1, the TV not answering: it writes no `tvcheck.txt`, and its hourly history line records the power as `unknown`, because nothing was asked.
- **One call pairs, from two commands.** `lgwebos.pair()` is the only path that may register, and the reading it then takes is the same as `read()`'s. It runs under `umask 077`, and afterwards sets the key file to 0600 whatever its mode was. Setup's pairing step and `tvgw tv pair` call it, and nothing else does; `tvgw tv pair` says what the prompt is, as setup does, before the TV shows it. The `chmod 600` of the key in `install.sh` stays as a second guard.
- **The reader is the profile's.** A TV's LAN API is a module the profile names, with a read-only reading and, where the TV has one, a pairing, and the profile declares what that reader can check, the TV's network paths among them; a profile with no reading of the paths is "not checked", never OK. Not built yet: until it is, `PAIRABLE_PROFILES` and `tvgw-analyze`'s profile test choose the reader, as before.

**Consequences.** On a gateway that holds a key for its TV, the reference among them, the hourly reading is the reading it was. Declining pairing now costs nothing: no prompt comes back by itself, and the owner pairs later with `sudo tvgw tv pair`. A gateway that never paired records an `unknown` power line every hour; the brief counts it as off, as it counted the `unreachable` lines the old prompting reading left. What this does not prevent: a key the TV itself has forgotten still brings the prompt up, because the TV decides that only after the registration is sent; the long-running probe of [decision 48](#48-one-ledger-of-changes-one-hour-classifier-and-a-second-judge-that-only-watches) stops for good on it, while the hourly reading tries again the next hour. A key made by hand with the library's own command still carries that command's umask until `install.sh` or the next pairing closes it.

*Amended by [decision 62](#62-a-tv-gets-a-vendor-profile-only-on-evidence-or-its-owners-answer-otherwise-the-general-profile-which-isolates-records-briefs-and-blocks-only-shared-lists) (2026-11-03): setup's pairing step follows the profile the settings will name, not the one discovery gave the device picked, so an LG its owner confirmed at the brand question is offered pairing, and a TV on the general profile, which has no LAN-API reader, never is.*

*Note (2026-11-04, workstream W12): the reading still never pairs, and now it also never sends a key the TV has refused. Before the library's reading, `lgwebos.handshake()` opens one short connection of tvgw's own: the TV's `hello` reply gives its webOS release, and the registration with the stored key says whether the TV still takes it. A refusal (LG's `403 Pairing rejected: blacklisted certificate detected`, which newer firmware answers the library's signed manifest with and shows no prompt for, or a pairing prompt in place of `registered`) is recorded with its time and reason in tvgw's own table of the key file (`lgkeys.py`), `tvgw tvcheck` exits 5, and every later reading connects to nothing until `sudo tvgw tv pair`, which alone clears the record: a refused key sent every hour is a prompt on the screen every hour, and LG's "too many pairing requests" lock clears only by unplugging the TV. The consequence above, "the hourly reading tries again the next hour", no longer holds. The manifest each key was made with is recorded with it and sent on every connection, since LG checks it per connection: a new pairing uses tvgw's unsigned read-only manifest, the reference CX's key keeps the library's signed one (a key file from before the table migrates to `signed`), and `tvgw tv pair --manifest signed` is the owner's explicit fallback. No code path switches a stored key's manifest or pairs on its own.*

*Note (2026-11-04), from the Samsung research of that day (W11): a second reader, chosen the interim way.* `tvgw tvcheck` runs src/tvgw/tizen.py when `PROFILE` is `samsung-tizen` and LG's reader otherwise, and `tvgw-analyze` takes the hourly reading for both profiles; setup's `READ_WITHOUT_PAIRING` beside `PAIRABLE_PROFILES` says the Samsung is read with nothing to pair. The profile still does not name its module. What the reading can check is the profile's `[support] can_check` and `cannot_check`, in plain words.

*2026-11-03, from the improvement design of that day (its sections 2.4, 4.4 and 6.1 item 5, and the owner's addendum on assumptions). The on-screen record, its store side, `tvgw tv confirm` and the brief's Wi-Fi line are built; the Samsung reading without pairing, the privacy table that shows each unreadable setting with its date, and the software-download detection from the firmware hosts are not built yet.*

**Context.** The gateway's premise is that the TV's only road is the cable ([decision 40](#40-the-tvs-radios-are-checked-not-assumed)). The LG CX reports its Wi-Fi, Wi-Fi Direct and tethering every hour, so that premise is checked there. Asking the owner is the only source left, and an owner who cannot find a menu must not have to answer yes to finish.

- **Samsung without pairing** (not built): a Samsung TV is read over its no-pairing REST interface, which puts nothing on its screen, and its unreadable settings are the owner's word as above.

The answer is the owner's, not a measurement: a `yes` given falsely silences the line, and the brief cannot tell.

*Note (2026-11-04, workstream W12): "asked again after a software download" now also follows the TV's release. ## 58. The brief has a plain form with three tiers, the login message shows its status and action, and `tvgw doctor` explains every check

*2026-11-03, from the improvement design of that day (its sections 2.5, 4.3 and 7.2, and finding F29 of its engineering review), under the owner's standing instruction of that day (its owner decisions D13 and D14, yes when he is away). Built the same day: verify's plain texts, its groups and the status file; `tvgw doctor`, `tvgw status`, the new checks and the must-allow retry; the plain brief, its tiers and the action file. Not built yet: the login message.*

**Context.** `tvgw verify` and the brief are written for an engineer: a `FAIL` line names a check and a detail, and the fix is in a table on another page. A typical user cannot act on them, and asking "is it working?" took reading a whole brief. Some checks never existed: a full disk, an unsynchronized clock, a backup that stopped, an ingest that stopped, an uplink without IPv4, a router that changed its address, another firewall on the same computer that drops the TV's traffic, other devices on the TV's port. A check added at FAIL on the reference could put a red line in the owner's brief the day it lands, and one SERVFAIL on 2026-11-01 did exactly that for 24 hours through `resolver_must_allow`.

The brief is about 70 lines of engineering words, and its red lines are one tier ([decision 14](#14-red-lines-are-the-alarm-model)): "ingest warnings: 2", which needs nothing, "firewall checks failed 1 of 144", a transient that cleared itself, and "Live Plus (ACR) is on", which needs someone at the TV, look alike. A typical owner cannot tell them apart, and reads the brief only by logging in to the gateway.

**Decision.**

- **Every check in plain words** (`verify.PLAIN`, `GROUPS`, `SETTLING`): what it means, what you notice on the TV, the fix, naming only commands this version has.
- **`tvgw doctor`** (root) runs the checks, prints each one that failed or warned in those words and offers its fix: `[y/N]` per fix, `--fix` for one question, `--yes` for none, and without a terminal only the explanation. It takes the install lock before any fix and checks again under it, so it never runs during a deploy. Each unit gets the fix it tolerates: `tvgw-apply` or starting a stopped `tvgw.service`, never a restart of an active one (its stop darkens the TV); a reload of the guard, which refuses a stop; `tvgw adguard service` for AdGuard Home, which waits during an upstream outage, then a start, which leaves a running AdGuard alone, or a restart of one that runs but answers nothing while the probes reach the internet, since it then serves no cache; restarts with the journal shown for dnsmasq, nginx and the recording units. Before a lookup fix it probes the uplink, the router, an upstream and the resolver and says which is down. It never changes another firewall, the uplink or the TV. Every run ends with the one step that always gets the TV online: move its cable to the router.
- **`tvgw status`** reads only `/run/tvgw/status.txt`, so any account can ask; three lines about now, and an exit status by tier: 0 nothing, 1 the gateway (run the doctor), 2 the TV.
- **New checks are warnings first.** Each new check is a WARN at most, whatever it finds, until a later change promotes it to the level `verify.FAIL_AFTER` names, one change per check after 7 days of OK in the hardware job's log, listed in the changelog. Their details carry no address, MAC or host name. `resolver_must_allow` asks a failing name once more after 3 seconds, and an answer then is a WARN, `transient`.
- **The plain brief.** `tvgw brief --plain` prints one day in seven lines: the title, Protection (the recorded checks), the TV (its hours on and its lookups of known tracking services, and how many were refused), Blocked most (three categories in plain words), New (a firmware update, new registrable domains only, a list update that took away a name the TV downloaded from, a newer tvgw), Action, and a footer naming `tvgw brief --full`. A store younger than a day prints "Your first brief is ready tomorrow morning." instead of a day that is mostly not assessed. `plain_brief()` in src/tvgw/plain.py writes it from the same report as the engineering brief.
- **Three tiers.** `RED_LINE_PLAIN` gives each red line a tier: `act` needs a person at the TV or the router, and the Action line says what to do there, with the menu path from the profile's `[[privacy_setting]]` record for its ACR switch; `doctor` is the gateway's own trouble and sends the reader to `sudo tvgw doctor`; `detail` stays in the engineering brief (ingest warnings, Encrypted ClientHello, the gateway's own lookups outside the VPN, a stalled trial). A red line no row names is `doctor`, never `detail`, and a test fails when `queries.red_lines()` can write a line without a row. Transients, a failed check or ingest warnings, are never an action at the TV.
- **`BRIEF_STYLE`.** `full`, which an absent key means, is the engineering brief from `tvgw brief` and in each daily file, as before; `plain` makes `tvgw brief` print the plain brief and starts each daily file with it. The settings template sets `plain`, so new installs get it; a gateway set up before keeps its brief and its daily files until its owner sets the key ([decision 53](#53-releases-carry-a-hash-checked-bootstrap-for-two-architectures-with-provenance-under-immutable-releases-updates-are-checked-weekly-installed-only-by-the-user-judged-by-verify-and-rolled-back-a-new-settings-key-never-changes-the-meaning-of-an-old-keys-value)'s rule for new keys). A day's JSON carries the plain lines as `plain` either way.
- **The status and the action, for the login message.** Each `tvgw verify` run leaves its verdict by check name in `/run/tvgw/status.txt`; `tvgw-analyze` leaves the newest finished day's Action in `/var/lib/tvgw/reports/action.txt` as a tier and a sentence, or empty. Neither holds a name, an address or a time from the record, so a login message built on them shows nothing an account outside `adm` could not otherwise see. The login message itself is later.

**Consequences.** "Is it working, and what do I do?" has a three-line answer and a command that does the fix, and the fixes are the ones `install.sh` already makes. The doctor's output may be pasted into an issue, so the journal lines it shows have their MACs and outside addresses replaced. A new check reaches FAIL only after a week of evidence on the reference, so a regression it would catch is a warning for that week. A new gateway has had no daily copy and no hourly ingest due in its first hours, so `store_backup` and `ingest_fresh` date them from its install rather than warn about them. The retry makes a failing run up to 3 seconds and one lookup longer. `tvgw backup` now refuses a copy without the store's size plus 512 MiB free (twice the size plus 1 GiB until 2026-11-04, which refused every copy on a small card: `VACUUM INTO` writes one copy before the rotation), so a gateway short of space keeps its older copies and `store_backup` says why.

A typical owner reads the day in five seconds and sees at most one thing to do, while the engineering brief is unchanged, one flag away. The tiers can hide a line from the Action slot only by a row that says `detail`, so a new red line is at worst a "run the doctor" too many. The action's menu path is the profile's first matching record, which on LG is the reference generation's: a newer set's menu may differ until its generation's record comes first. Still nothing pages anyone or writes to the TV's screen ([decision 14](#14-red-lines-are-the-alarm-model)).

|---|---|---|

| Whether transients are an action at the TV (D14) | none: `RED_LINE_PLAIN` | no: a failed check is `doctor`, ingest warnings `detail` |

## 59. Site rules: `/etc/tvgw/local.rules`, written by `tvgw allow` and `tvgw block`, applied after the profile and before machine rules, under the same lint

*Decided 2026-11-03 under the owner's standing instruction of that day (the November improvement design's owner decision D12, yes when he is away). Built the same day; nothing changes on a gateway until its first `tvgw allow` or `tvgw block`.*

**Context.** Policy changed only through the profile in the repository and a deploy ([decision 6](#6-adguard-home-is-configured-as-code)). On a gateway installed from the package there is no checkout, so when an app stopped working its owner had no supported way to let one name through, and no way to block a name the lists miss. Every non-LG profile subscribes to shared lists that may break an app (design D37), and LG's billing host stays blocked with `tvgw allow ibs.lgappstv.com` named as its undo (D28): both assume such a command.

**Decision.**

- **One file the owner's commands write.** `LOCAL_RULES` (default `/etc/tvgw/local.rules`, 0640 root:adm) holds two forms only: `@@||<host>^$important`, an allow, and `||<host>^`, a block, each under a dated comment line the command writes with its reason. No regex, no wildcard, no other modifier. `sudo tvgw allow NAME`, `sudo tvgw block NAME`, `sudo tvgw unallow NAME` and `sudo tvgw unblock NAME` edit it atomically under the autonomy's lock and re-apply AdGuard Home; `tvgw rules --local` lists it. An allow replaces the owner's own block of the same name and the ones under it, and a block replaces the owner's allow of the name.
- **Its place in the plan.** `tvgw adguard apply` lists the profile's rules (the generated ones, then the `.rules` file), then the site rules, then the machine's, each group linted beside the profile and none repeating a rule an earlier group holds. An allow carries `$important`, so it outranks every list's block and the machine's, whatever the order. `tvgw adguard diff` shows the site rules as their own group. Without the file the plan is the one from before it existed, byte for byte; an unreadable file stops the apply.
- **The same lint, in the owner's mode.** A block of a must-allow host or of a name above one, of a clock host, of a host the URL filter answers, at the apex of a zone in `never_block_apex`, or under an allow (the profile's or the owner's) is refused, with the reason in plain words. An allow may sit at or under a blocked name, which is what it is for, so the profile lint's exception-over-block refusal does not apply to the owner's allows; every other check does. An allow of a whole registered domain that the profile files under tracking (ACR, ads, telemetry, fingerprinting) needs `--force`, after the command says what it unblocks; an allow of one host under such a domain is said and taken.
- **All or nothing.** When AdGuard holds its restart back because the upstreams are down, the command says so, exits 3 and leaves the file as it was; when the apply fails, both files go back and it exits 1. Each change that took is one `owner_rule_change` line in the change ledger (class A), and the brief's Rule changes section lists the rule once AdGuard holds it.
- **The machine defers to the owner.** The analyst's vetting and the autonomy's gate refuse a block of a name an owner's allow covers ("no: allowed by the owner (local.rules)"), and `tvgw allow` takes a machine block under the name out of `AUTO_RULES` in the same step, which the next hourly check records as the owner's revert. A site block the machine also holds stays when the machine reverts its copy.
- **Kept.** `tvgw forget` keeps the file wherever `LOCAL_RULES` puts it: it is policy, not a viewing record.

**Consequences.** A package user can undo a block that broke an app in one command, and the commands cannot block anything the TV needs. The price is a second policy file beside the profile: a rule written on the gateway has no review and no history in the repository, only its dated comment, the ledger line and the brief's Rule changes. Every change restarts AdGuard Home once, which the restart guard holds back while the upstreams are down. A hand edit of the file bypasses the `--force` question, though never the lint, which the plan applies to every line.

*Note (2026-11-05, research pass 4, build item 7): `sudo tvgw refused` finds the name to allow. It reads what the filter refused the TV in the last minutes from AdGuard Home's own query log (its file and its API, never the store's late copy), ranks a name the profile says the TV needs first, then a refusal the record has not seen (by its rule and its registrable domain, so a rotating tracker name is no news), then by retries, with every known tracker last and never on its own the likely cause, and prints for the likely cause the fix `tvgw check` gives, so the one command this decision added is one step from a broken app. A name refused on purpose before too (a tracker, or a rule of the profile or a list) gets no fix: the allow stays one `tvgw check` away, for whoever means it. It blocks nothing, changes nothing and writes nothing, and when AdGuard cannot be asked it reports from the file and says so ([what the TV was just refused](how-it-works/adguard.md#what-the-tv-was-just-refused)). It is pass 2's `tvgw broke` triage without the draft breakage record, which stays unbuilt.*

It deploys a release's tag on every release, and a push to `main` only while the user pages name no command the latest release lacks. Every page stays plain Markdown with relative links, so GitHub stays a complete reading.

**Consequences.** A reader finds the task before the mechanism, and an engineer still finds every fact where its owner page was, one section further down. Owners moved: the settings, the commands and the profile format have reference pages of their own, and the folder READMEs link them. A link, anchor or printed path that leads nowhere fails the gate, so a later move cannot break one silently. User pages may repeat a stable fact their task needs, linking its owner, which A2 alone forbade. Until the plain rewrite lands, the moved user pages read as the engineering text they came from, and the test exempts them by name. Until the site exists, GitHub is the only reading; once it does, a page that names an unreleased command holds the site at its last good state until the next release.

*Note, 2026-11-03 (the site's build).* A release publishes its tag's pages only when it is the latest release, so a pre-release or a patch to an older line never replaces newer pages. Nothing publishes until the owner sets Pages to deploy from GitHub Actions and the repository variable `TVGW_PAGES` to `on` ([the documentation site](site/README.md#when-it-publishes)).

## 62. A TV gets a vendor profile only on evidence or its owner's answer; otherwise the general profile, which isolates, records, briefs and blocks only shared lists

*2026-11-03, from the improvement design of that day (its section 5.2, and finding F1 of its user review). Its owner decision, whether the general profile subscribes to shared lists or blocks nothing by name, is answered "the two shared lists" by the owner's standing instruction of 2026-11-03 not to stop for questions.*

**Context.** The settings template said `PROFILE=lg-webos`, and setup and `tvgw discover --write` changed it only for a device a discovery row named a profile for, and only LG's rows did: ports 3000 and 3001, or a host name holding `lgwebostv`, either one alone. Every other TV kept the template's LG profile, and setup said `PROFILE stays as it is`: a Google TV, a Sony, a Fire TV, and a Samsung, Roku or Vizio set too, since no row names their DNS-only profiles ([decision 32](#32-a-vendor-profile-without-hardware-is-dns-only-cited-and-subscribed)). Such a TV got LG's port wall on TCP 4001, 4431, 4445, 4447, 8180 and 8883, whose counter drives the ACR red line ([decision 45](#45-the-acr-red-line-is-an-upload-test-and-a-port-wall)), LG's must-allow and URL-filter hosts, and the clock red line, which on a TV that never asks LG's SDP hosts for the time fires every day it is on. And one LG signal was enough to name LG, although a host name is only what the device says about itself.

**Decision.**

- **A profile on evidence.** `discover.assign()` gives each device found a profile, a vendor and the evidence. A bundled profile is assigned on two signals that agree, every port of a vendor's row open and a host-name hint of the same vendor, or on one that only that vendor's TV gives: an answer to its own SSDP search target, LG's second-screen service. One signal alone (the ports, the host name or the UPnP manufacturer) names a vendor, never a profile. The discovery rows are v0.2.0's, and only LG's name a profile.
- **One question when the evidence is thin.** When the vendor has a bundled profile and none was assigned, setup asks "This looks like a <vendor> TV. Is it? [Y/n]": yes writes that profile, no writes the general one. A vendor with no profile, or no vendor at all, gets the general profile, and setup says what it does. Unattended, `SETUP_PROFILE=auto`, the default, never asks and writes the general profile wherever it would have asked; `SETUP_PROFILE=<name>` writes that profile, as `PROFILE=<name>` in the answers does, and under `auto` a copy of the template's `PROFILE=generic-tv` in the answers leaves the choice to the evidence, as its `TV_MAC` placeholder leaves the TV. A re-run for the same TV keeps the profile its settings file gives, as before, `lg-webos` for a file without the key included: setup writes no `PROFILE` that would change it.
- **The general profile.** `generic-tv` is the profile for any TV tvgw has no profile for. The TV is isolated, recorded and briefed as any TV is. The profile subscribes the two vendor-neutral lists every DNS-only profile already ships, HaGeZi Pro and HaGeZi's DoH-only bypass list, each naming its sentinel, and blocks nothing else by name. It has no must-allow hosts, no URL filter, no LAN-API reader, no generation rows and no firewall rules of its own. Its `[clock] model = "none"` says the gateway does not know how the TV sets its clock, so the brief raises no clock red line for it. Its support status is Not yet tested, and its Wi-Fi step is the plain one any brand needs.
- **LG's wall stands only in front of an LG.** `tvgw-apply` builds the port wall only when `PROFILE` is `lg-webos` or absent, whatever `ADTECH_PORT_WALL` says, and records `PROFILE` in `/run/tvgw/settings.applied`. `PROFILE` is one of `verify.APPLY_KEYS`, so `applied_current` shows a profile change no apply has taken up.
- **The template.** `deploy/tvgw.conf.example` says `PROFILE=generic-tv`. A settings file without `PROFILE` still means `lg-webos`.

**Consequences.** A TV of a brand tvgw has no profile for gets isolation, the record, the daily brief and the shared lists, with no LG wall, no LG hosts and no false clock alarm. A Samsung, Roku or Vizio set gets its DNS-only profile when its owner says yes, and an LG that gives one signal gets the LG profile the same way. HaGeZi Pro may break an app on a TV whose needs nobody has recorded, and lifting one of its blocks means a profile of your own. A profile given by path (`PROFILE=/etc/tvgw/my-tv.toml`) no longer gets LG's wall either. On the reference gateway nothing changes: its settings file names `PROFILE=lg-webos`, the template is copied only when no settings file exists, a setup re-run keeps the stored profile, and the next apply adds the one line `PROFILE=lg-webos` to `settings.applied`. Not built: the brand question for a TV that gives no signal at all (it gets the general profile), a record of how the profile was chosen, and a watch mode for profiles that are not tested.

*Note (2026-11-04), from the Samsung research of that day (W11): a Samsung is assigned on its own answer.* `assign()` gives `samsung-tizen` on one signal only, the device's REST reply on port 8001 saying `type` "Samsung SmartTV", asked once of a device another signal names Samsung and nothing names LG. Ports, the SSDP server header in both of its spellings, a `urn:samsung.com:` search target, a `tizen` or `samsung` host name and the DHCP vendor class name the vendor only, because Samsung soundbars answer the same discovery; a reply that is not a SmartTV's gives no profile, whatever else agrees. A device is its MAC, never its UPnP UDN, which some sets change at every boot. The `localhost` many Samsungs announce over DHCP is no name, so setup proposes `TV_NAME=tv`, not a name that would point the AdGuard client and the scrubber at this computer itself. On the reference gateway nothing changes: its settings file names `lg-webos`, and an LG is never asked the Samsung question.

## 63. Behavior-changing deploys to the reference wait for a quiet window decided on the gateway; nothing the gateway prints where others can read it carries a home identifier; acceptance that needs systemd runs in a container tier

*Decided 2026-11-03 under the owner's standing instruction of that day (the November improvement design's owner decision D38: the 03:30 to 05:30 window when he is away). The quiet window and the scrubber were built the same day; the container tier comes separately.*

**Context.** A push to `main` deploys to the reference gateway ([decision 26](#26-the-workflows-are-the-most-privileged-code-in-the-repository)), and a deploy restarts what any deploy restarts: nginx, dnsmasq, the firewall's counters. A deploy in the middle of an evening has cut the TV off before. The owner asked not to be asked, and nothing in CI can see whether the TV is on, so "deploy when the TV is off" needed a mechanism instead of a person. A job that waited for the TV to go quiet would publish, through its own timing, when the household watches TV. The repository is public, and so is the hardware job's log: the install's output and `tvgw verify`'s details could carry the router's and the uplink's addresses, MACs, a USB adapter whose name embeds its MAC, the host name and the Wi-Fi name, against the house rule on site identifiers. Acceptance that needs systemd, NetworkManager and the real installer runs only on the reference gateway.

**Decision.**

- **A trailer marks the commits that wait.** A commit that changes `deploy/nftables/`, `deploy/networkmanager/`, `deploy/dnsmasq/`, `deploy/nginx/`, `src/tvgw/tvapi/`, a profile's `[firewall]` or `[url_filter]` table, or the routing and dnsmasq parts of `install.sh` ends its message with `Deploy-window: tv-off`. `tools/check_deploy_window.py` fails `make gate` for such a commit without it, and CI's hardware job holds a push with such a change for the window even when the trailer is missing.
- **The window is fixed and read on the gateway.** `tvgw tv quiet` says `deploy: now` only inside `DEPLOY_WINDOW` (absent: `03:30-05:30`, the gateway's local time), with the TV's last hourly reading not `Active` and the TV port under 1 MB over the last 20 minutes, and otherwise `deploy: deferred`: one line that never says why. The hardware job runs it from its checkout, as root, before any such deploy.
- **A deferred deploy is retried, never dropped.** The deferred run writes its commit to `/var/lib/tvgw/deploy-deferred`. A 30-minute schedule runs the hardware job alone; it stops at once without the file, and with it deploys `main`'s head in the window, never an older commit, then removes the file. A push while the file exists waits as well. The retry waits in its own concurrency group, so it never takes a waiting push's place.
- **What the gateway prints is scrubbed twice.** `src/tvgw/scrub.py` replaces every address outside `TV_NET` but the ones tvgw ships, every MAC, a USB interface named after its MAC, and the host, TV, Wi-Fi and home-directory user names. A `verify.Check` scrubs its detail when it is made and keeps the original in `raw`, which is never printed, stored or serialized. Every step of the hardware job runs under `bash -o pipefail` with its output piped through `python3 -m tvgw.scrub --stdin`.
- **Acceptance that needs systemd** runs in a container tier on GitHub's runners, beside the namespace copy of [decision 27](#27-a-namespace-copy-of-the-gateway-in-ci).

**Consequences.** A commit that changes how the gateway treats the TV can wait up to a day, and no second such commit goes into a window before the first has had its hardware run. `make deploy` by hand ignores the window: the owner chooses that moment. The log shows a short green run every 30 minutes, and a deferred deploy's time always falls inside the fixed window, which says no more than that the TV was quiet then. The scrubber can replace too much (a four-part version number reads as an address, a Wi-Fi name that is a common word goes wherever it appears) and can miss an identifier of a kind it does not know; the hardware suite counts private addresses, MACs and the host name in `tvgw verify`'s details, and the acceptance counts them in a downloaded log. A check's detail on the gateway's own screen and in the store shows the placeholders too; the command the check names shows the value. GitHub's runner prints its own name and the computer's before the first step, and the checkout step the workspace's path with the runner user's name in it, both outside any pipe, so those names should say nothing about the household. The reference gateway gains a read-only command and one marker file, and nothing else changes on it.

**Owner decisions, held behind settings.**

| Decision | Setting | Default |
|---|---|---|
| The hours a deploy that waits may run in | `DEPLOY_WINDOW` | `03:30-05:30` when absent |

*Note (2026-11-05): the run that defers waits on the gateway, and the schedule is the backstop.* The 30-minute schedule was what brought a deferred deploy into the window, and GitHub's scheduler did not hold it: it started four runs in 21 hours (18:13, 23:54, 06:35 and 14:56 UTC on 2026-11-04 and 2026-11-05), up to 22 minutes after their minute and none inside the window, so the deploy deferred on 2026-11-04 missed the window of 2026-11-05. A deploy that must happen within two hours cannot hang on a best-effort scheduler; the gateway's own clock is reliable, and the job already runs on the gateway. So a run that defers now stays on the gateway's runner. After the marker step, `tools/wait_deploy_window.py --wait` asks `tvgw tv quiet --wait 0` on the gateway's local half hours only, :00 and :30, the same times every day, never between them, and prints nothing but its last line. The time of a deploy the wait lets through is a half hour inside the fixed window. The log shows that half hour, and with it that the half hours before it, since the wait began, did not let the deploy through, and a run still waiting after a window shows that none of its half hours did; the 30-minute retry showed as much, and this decision accepted it. The wait gives up after 22 hours 15 minutes, so that the job, with a deploy of up to 90 minutes after it, ends inside the 24 hours its token lives; the job's timeout is 1440 minutes, the wait step's 1345 and the deploy step's own 90. Every 5 minutes the wait reads `main`'s head from GitHub, without credentials. A head that differs from the commit that waits in a path the workflow does not ignore, which a newer push with a CI run of its own makes it, or a head that does not descend from that commit, ends the wait, so that the gateway's one runner is free for that push's run, which takes the wait; the two commits are compared, not the pushes between them. A push that changes only paths the workflow ignores has no run, so the wait goes on and the deploy takes `main`'s head. The marker now names the newest commit a tested push deferred: only a push writes it, never over a newer commit that descends from its own, and it goes only after a deploy step that ran. A scheduled run no longer asks or deploys in the window step. With a marker whose commit vouches for its checkout, as the same commit or one ahead of it only by ignored paths, it takes the wait up, and the wait reads `main` from GitHub, asks on the half hours and makes the checkout `main`'s head before a deploy. ## 64. The gate's second set is filled in the path of the answer, beside the passive feeder, and still only counts

*2026-11-05, from research pass 4 (its section "Where the ways round a filter stand in late 2026" and build item 3). Built behind a setting; the reference gateway runs it only once the owner sets `DNSGATE_FILL=inpath`.*

**Context.** The DNS gate counts connections the TV opens to an address its resolver never handed it ([decision 18](#18-dns-gated-egress-starts-as-a-count-in-its-own-table)). Its feeder is passive: tcpdump sees an answer as it leaves the gateway and the address is added a few milliseconds later, while the TV can connect within one. Measured on the reference gateway over five days, 322 connections fell outside the set and 320 of them came within two seconds of the answer that named the address; one evening of a single streaming app took the 24-hour count from 13 to 80, every sampled row marked `just looked up`. A count that is 99% its own race cannot be enforced, and decision 18 says enforcement needs a filler "proven to see every answer". Every working design for this rule, in the research's reading, updates the list in the path of the DNS answer, before the device can act on it.

Two builds do that. In the first, dnsmasq answers the TV, adds each address to the set itself (`nftset=`) and forwards to AdGuard Home. The research names four traps in it, which it cites to dnsmasq's changelog and mailing list and which this project has not tested: AdGuard would see dnsmasq, not the TV, and the client-keyed rule that withholds HTTPS records, the lookup log and `tvgw check` all recognise the TV by the address AdGuard sees; an answer served from dnsmasq's cache never reaches the set; dnsmasq's adds carry no lifetime, so on the gateway's kernel a repeated answer would not renew an entry ([decision 43](#43-the-flow-log-hears-the-tv-alone-and-the-gates-day-starts-at-each-answer)); and Debian 13's dnsmasq 2.91 predates a 2.92 fix for answers over TCP and a 2.93 fix for a memory overwrite on long names, which matters more once every lookup of the TV passes through it. In the second build AdGuard Home stays the TV's resolver and each answer is held on its way out until its addresses are in the set.

**Decision.** The second build, beside the passive feeder and not instead of it, counting only.

1. **A setting.** `DNSGATE_FILL=passive` (the default, and what a settings file without the key means) is the gateway as it was: no new rule, no new set, no new unit running, and `tvgw-apply --check` renders byte for byte what it rendered before. `DNSGATE_FILL=inpath`, read only with `DNSGATE=count`, adds everything below. `DNSGATE` keeps its two values; nothing here refuses a packet.
2. **The rules.** In `inet tvgw_gate`, in a load and in chains of their own ([the in-path filler's half](../deploy/nftables/README.md#the-in-path-fillers-half)): the chain `answers` in the output hook hands the gateway's own DNS answers to the TV (every UDP datagram from port 53, and every TCP segment from it that neither opens nor resets the connection) to netfilter queue 53053 with `bypass`; the set `resolved4_inpath` is the second list; the chain `forward_inpath` is the gate's rule again against that list, with its own counter and its own log prefix, `tvgw-inpath`. The gate's own rule, set, counter and samples are untouched.
3. **The filler.** `tvgw-dnsgate-inpath` (`src/tvgw/inpath.py`) binds the queue. For each answer it reads the A records, adds the ones it does not already know to `resolved4_inpath` with a one-day timeout and the same expiry, waits for the kernel's acknowledgement that the transaction was committed, and then gives the packet its verdict. The only verdict it can give is accept, with no payload and no mark: it cannot drop an answer and cannot change one. It is pure Python on the standard library's netlink socket (`src/tvgw/nfnl.py`), with no new dependency. The add is a netlink transaction, not a spawned `nft`: on the development machine the Python side of a hold (decode, read, encode, the hand-over to the adding thread and back) has a median of 18 microseconds, against several milliseconds for starting `nft`. The hold on the gateway is measured by the filler itself, kept in its status file, and held under 10 ms at the median by the hardware suite.
4. **What it remembers.** An address whose add was acknowledged in the last 60 seconds is known: an answer naming only known addresses is released at once. Every other answer renews its addresses before it leaves, so an element's expiry is never more than a minute short of a day after the last answer that named it. The memory is bounded (4,096 addresses) and is emptied whenever an add finds no table or no set.
5. **What never enters.** `0.0.0.0` (the blocked answer) and its network, loopback, the private, shared and link-local ranges the firewall's `lan4` set already refuses the TV, multicast and the reserved block; AAAA records, since the TV side has no IPv6. Every A record of the answer section is taken, so a CNAME chain adds all its addresses. This is stricter than the passive feeder, which lets private addresses in; the documentation ranges of RFC 5737 are not excluded, since nothing routes them on a real uplink and the namespace tier's internet is built from them.
6. **A second count, recorded on its own.** The second rule's counter, the two queue rules' counters and the filler's own counts (answers held, released at once, late, unadded, not parsed, passed by the kernel) are stored by `tvgw verify --record` as series under the prefix `in-path:` and kept out of the brief's Firewall held line. The second rule's samples go to a table of their own, `gate_inpath` (schema 11). The brief's DNS-gated egress section shows both counts on one line when the window holds the second.
7. **A check.** `dnsgate_inpath` in `tvgw verify`: OK, saying `not in use`, on a passive gateway; with `inpath`, whether the rules are loaded, a process is bound to the queue, its status is fresh and it is live, with its counts as the detail. A WARN at most while it is new ([decision 58](#58-the-brief-has-a-plain-form-with-three-tiers-the-login-message-shows-its-status-and-action-and-tvgw-doctor-explains-every-check)). The unit is not among `verify.SERVICES`: a filler that is down is not an outage.

**What happens when the filler fails.** The rule this pass set for anything new: it must not be able to take the TV's lookups away by failing. Each case was read in the kernel's source (Linux 6.18, `net/netfilter/nfnetlink_queue.c`, `nf_queue.c` and `core.c`) and is tested, the kernel's half in the namespace tier. The watchdog case runs there under a transient unit that carries the shipped `Type`, `NotifyAccess`, `WatchdogSec` and `WatchdogSignal` and none of the shipped sandbox, so the real unit's first start, with its capability bound, address families and read-only system, is proven only on the gateway. The verdicts the kernel refuses (answers it had already dropped) are stored as a series and the last hour's are a warning of the check; the kernel's own queue counters never show that class of drop.

| The filler is | What the kernel does | What the TV sees |
|---|---|---|
| Not running, or not bound | The queue rule's `bypass`: with no process bound, the packet is accepted | Every answer, unheld. The in-path count rises; `dnsgate_inpath` warns |
| Starting | The queue rules match only while the set `inpath_live` holds port 53. The filler empties it, binds and configures the queue in one message, then puts the port in | Every answer. No answer can reach a queue that is bound but not yet configured, which the kernel would drop |
| Slow to add (another transaction holds nf_tables' lock) | Nothing: the filler releases the answer after 20 ms and counts it `late`. Until that add returns, later answers are not held at all | An answer at most 20 ms later than it would have been, a fiftieth of the shortest resolver retry timer |
| Reading hostile bytes | Nothing: the answer comes from upstream servers through AdGuard, so it is read with work linear in its length, never past 4,096 bytes, and the verdict is given in a `finally` | Every answer. On the development machine, a crafted answer that costs the store's decoder 12 ms costs this reader 0.1 ms |
| Without its table or set (`DNSGATE=off` applied under it) | Nothing: the add fails, the answer is released and counted | Every answer. Every 2 seconds the filler puts its port into `inpath_live` again, as a create, on its second thread: when the port turns out to have gone, the set was made anew, so the filler forgets every address it knew and adds again from the next answer |
| Running while `tvgw-apply` runs | The kernel drops every packet waiting in any queue the moment any netfilter hook is removed (`nf_queue_nf_hook_drop`), and every apply removes hooks, each `delete table` does. So the apply takes the switch away first: one transaction removes the queue rules and deletes the set `inpath_live`, after which no answer is offered to the queue and the filler cannot put its port back; the apply waits up to a tenth of a second for the filler to release what it held (ten looks, 10 ms apart), and then removes the first hook, which drops whatever a stalled filler still holds; its last load makes the rules and the set anew | Every answer, unheld for the second or two the apply takes and the up to 2 seconds until the filler switches itself on again. Answers in that time can show as misses in the second count. A filler that is stalled through the tenth of a second loses what it held, at most 128 answers |
| Hung or stopped | The queue is configured fail-open and holds at most 128 packets: once that many wait, the kernel accepts the rest itself. systemd's watchdog (`WatchdogSec=2`, `WatchdogSignal=SIGKILL`) kills a loop that stops pinging, and the socket's close drops what still waits in the queue (`NETLINK_URELEASE`, `instance_destroy`, `nfqnl_flush`) | At most 128 answers held back for at most about two seconds and then dropped, the TV asking again for each; every later answer passes. While they wait, each queued packet stays charged to the resolver's UDP socket (the queue entry keeps `skb->sk`), roughly 100 to 300 KB against a 212 KB default send buffer, so the resolver's own writes can stall for the same two seconds |
| Killed, or crashed | The socket closes and the queue is gone: `bypass` again | Every later answer |
| Stopping cleanly | The filler empties `inpath_live`, sets the queue's length to 0, which makes the kernel accept each new packet, releases what waits and only then closes | Every answer |

Three things remain that no program outside the kernel can prevent, and they are stated here because the claim must be exact. A queue whose socket closes while packets wait drops those packets: `nfqnl_flush` reinjects them with `NF_DROP` in every kernel up to 6.18. So a kill, a crash or the watchdog loses the answers in the queue at that instant, normally none or one and at most 128. The same flush runs for every queue when any hook is removed by a program that is not `tvgw-apply`: a VPN client rebuilding its tables, a container runtime, the guard table's own reload during an install, a hand-typed `nft delete table`; an answer is lost then only if one is waiting at that instant, and an answer waits for a fraction of a millisecond. And a packet the kernel cannot allocate a queue entry for, under memory exhaustion, is dropped. In each case the TV's resolver asks again, and none outlasts the instant. The passive feeder is not in the path and is unaffected by all of it: it keeps filling `resolved4`, the gate's own rule keeps counting, and the brief's first count is what it was.

**Consequences.** One more root service, with `CAP_NET_ADMIN` only, no packet socket and no network socket, running only where the setting asks for it. Each held answer is one nf_tables transaction, as each answer already is one for the passive feeder; `nft` commands running at the same moment see the ruleset's generation move and retry, as they do today. A TCP answer is read only when it sits whole in one or more length-prefixed messages inside one segment; a length prefix and its message in separate writes, an answer over one segment (the kernel queues GSO segments one by one), or a coalesced segment pass unread, are counted `unparsed_tcp`, and their addresses can show as misses in the second count; this is untested against a real resolver over TCP, so `unparsed_tcp` is one of the numbers to watch. An add the kernel refuses is never retried: during an apply's gap (ENOENT), at a full set (ENFILE at 65,536 elements, for which the check warns from half), after a transaction that got no answer (EIO), or when the adder is 64 adds behind. An address is known for 10 seconds after its add, so a set emptied by hand leaves up to 10 seconds of answers with no add; an apply that makes the set anew is noticed by the port being gone. Answers the kernel passed at a full queue take no id, so `kernel_passed` does not see them; answers passed while an add was out are added only if that add returns. A UDP answer with the truncation bit passes untouched, since the TV asks again over TCP. An answer over 4,096 bytes passes unread. The filler sees only the gateway's own answers, so a lookup that never reaches the gateway's resolver adds nothing, which is the gate's purpose. The first day after a reboot overstates both counts, as before: the TV's cache outlives both sets. The store's schema is 11, for `gate_inpath`. What a passive gateway gains from this change, the default: one `nft list chain` per apply, `DNSGATE_FILL=passive` in `settings.applied`, one more OK check, the schema bump, an hourly empty `journalctl -k -g tvgw-inpath`, the unit file copied and disabled, and a fifth rendering in the platform check.

How the comparison is read: with both fillers running, the brief's line `passive feeder N misses, in-path M misses` should show M at a handful a week at most, each explained by a reboot or an app's cache, against the passive count's race. `answers offered` against what the filler received says how many answers the kernel passed on its own; that number at 0, over two weeks, is the evidence decision 18 asks for.

**Not in this change.** Enforcement. It would still need: decision 18's condition met by the two weeks above; the list kept across a reboot, so the TV's cache does not outlive it; a refusal by reset and not by silent drop, so an app retries at once; exceptions for LG's fixed firmware fallback address and for the gateway itself; a way to refuse without re-checking a connection that is already open; and the automatic undo of a bad change that research pass 4 ranks beside it. Reading a TCP answer that spans segments, and running the dnsmasq build in CI for comparison, are also left out: the first needs stream reassembly in the answer's path, and the second would change who AdGuard sees.

## 65. A test client stands where the TV stands; an apply is probed before and after, and only reports what it would undo

*2026-11-05, from research pass 4 (its reliability section and build item 5). Built the same day: the probe, its check and its series, and the confirmation in `tvgw-apply` with `report` and `revert`. Nothing is on by default, and `revert` is a default nowhere.*

**Context.** All three entries of [the incident log](incidents.md) are changes the gateway made to itself that were right in testing and wrong on the gateway. Since [decision 63](#63-behavior-changing-deploys-to-the-reference-wait-for-a-quiet-window-decided-on-the-gateway-nothing-the-gateway-prints-where-others-can-read-it-carries-a-home-identifier-acceptance-that-needs-systemd-runs-in-a-container-tier) a change that touches the TV's path is deployed while the TV is off, so nothing real judges it until the evening, when the household is the test. `tvgw verify` runs from the gateway itself: its lookups and its route question never cross the forward chain, the address translation or the routing rule a packet of the TV's crosses. Every tool that undoes a bad network change (a router's confirmed commit, `netplan try`, `iptables-apply`) waits for the operator's own session to survive. Here the device the change is for cannot confirm anything, and is off.

The TV is cabled straight to `TV_IF`. A second device cannot be put on that wire in software: a macvlan child cannot reach its parent's own address, where the resolver and the URL filter listen. Making `TV_IF` a bridge would put a real second port there, and would change the port every rule, route and capture names on a working gateway. Injecting a test client's packets into `TV_IF`'s receive path with a traffic-control redirect was read and set aside: the kernel refuses the redirect while the port has no carrier, which is the TV-off case the probe exists for, it puts a filter of tvgw's on the TV's own port, and packets that arrive there are counted, logged and captured as the TV's.

**Decision.**

- **The probe is a client on a port of its own, behind a twin of the TV's rules.** With `TV_PROBE=on`, `sudo tvgw probe` makes a network namespace and a veth pair for a few seconds. The pair's gateway end is `tvgw-probe`; the client has `TV_PROBE_IP`, an address of its own in `TV_NET` (default `10.10.10.30`: outside the DHCP pool, never `GW_IP` or `TV_IP`). `tvgw-apply` gives that port a twin of every rule the TV's packets cross, made at each apply from the text of the TV's own chains as that apply rendered them: the input rules after "not the TV's port", the forward rules after the same, all of `tv_out`, and the TV-port rules of the mark, redirect and masquerade chains, each with the interface renamed and without its counter, its log and its comment. A twin that cannot be made whole, or that nft refuses, is not loaded, and the TV's table loads as it does without the probe.
- **It never contacts the TV's vendor.** Measured on the reference gateway, the TV opens no connection and makes no lookup while it is off (29 hours off, 2026-10-26 to 2026-11-05). A probe that completed a handshake with a must-allow host every ten minutes would make the home louder toward the vendor than the TV is. So the allowed half is two steps. `lookup`: a must-allow name of the loaded profile is looked up at the gateway's resolver and must get an address; nothing ever connects to that address. `path`: a TLS handshake, with no request and no identifier, goes through the forward path, the address translation and the uplink to a neutral server and back. The server is `TV_PROBE_TARGET`, or, when that is empty, the host one of the profile's blocklists is fetched from (`adguardteam.github.io`, then `cdn.jsdelivr.net`, for the shipped profiles): content networks the gateway's own software already asks every day. A must-allow host is refused as a target whoever names it. A target the filter blocks, or whose address the TV's rules refuse (a DoH resolver's, a manual block's), reads `unknown` with the advice to name another, never `fail`.
- **The connection out is rare.** The ten-minute check runs the port, the lookups, the walls and the redirect every time; none of them leaves the gateway but the lookups, which go to the resolver. It makes the connection out only when none was made for six hours, or the last one did not pass and an hour has gone by: four a day. What the connection adds to the other steps is the address translation, the uplink and the way back; a fault there comes from an apply, which has a connection of its own, or from outside the gateway. `sudo tvgw probe` always makes it.
- **The walls, in a few seconds.** A name the profile blocks is refused; DNS over TLS (TCP 853) is refused; QUIC (UDP 443) is refused with `BLOCK_QUIC=yes`; a lookup of the blocked name aimed at a public resolver (the first address of `DNS_BOOTSTRAP`) is still refused, which shows the gateway's filter answered it. Beside its packets it reads `TV_IF`'s own forwarding switch and asks the kernel for the route of a packet from `TV_IP` arriving on `TV_IF`.
- **What the name steps test.** AdGuard Home sees the probe as a client of its own, not as the TV. `tvgw adguard apply` writes the TV's client entry with the global settings and the global blocked services, and every name rule tvgw writes (the profile's, the owner's, the machine's, the must-allow exceptions) has no client scope. The one rule scoped to the TV's address withholds the HTTPS and SVCB record types, which the probe never asks for. So for a name, the resolver's answer to the probe is its answer to the TV, and `lookup`, `blocked` and `redirect` test the policy the TV gets. Two things would break that and are not the case today: a profile rule scoped with `client=` or `ctag=` (a unit test fails when one appears), and a subscribed list that scopes a rule to the `device_tv` tag the TV's entry carries. The probe is not made a second AdGuard client: that would restart AdGuard Home at each change of the setting and put the probe into AdGuard's own statistics under the TV's entry.
- **The live gateway is judged by the settings it was applied with.** The probe reads `/run/tvgw/settings.applied` for the uplink, the ports, the addresses and `BLOCK_QUIC`, not the settings file: the file may hold an edit no apply has taken up, and before an apply that moves the TV to another uplink it already names the new one. The verdict before an apply is of the gateway as it is, and the verdict after of the gateway as that apply made it. The namespace tier found this: judged by the file, the verdict before such an apply was a failure, so nothing was kept and nothing armed.
- **Three verdicts, by one table.** `pass`, `fail`, `unknown`. A step is `fail` only when its failure is one an apply of the firewall can cause (the port's switches and routes, the filter chains, the redirect, the address translation), and for the connection out only when the gateway itself, from its own network stack with the socket bound to the uplink the TV uses (and not by the tunnel the gateway's own traffic may take), completed what the probe could not. What the filter answers for a name is its lists and its upstream, which no apply sets: it is never a `fail` here, and `tvgw verify`'s own resolver checks carry it. The table, as `probe.judge` holds it and `tests/unit/test_probe.py` runs every row of it:

| Step | Seen | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| `port` | forwarding off for `TV_IF` | fail |
| `port` | the TV's route leaves by another interface | fail; unknown with the uplink down |
| `port` | the gateway's answers to the TV leave by another interface (table 100's TV-subnet route) | fail |
| `lookup` | every must-allow name got an address | pass |
| `lookup` | the resolver answers nothing at the TV's position, and answers the gateway | fail |
| `lookup` | the resolver answers nothing at the TV's position, nor the gateway | unknown |
| `lookup` | a name blocked, or without an address | unknown |
| `path` | a handshake completed | pass |
| `path` | not due in this run, or no neutral server known | skipped |
| `path` | the uplink is down | unknown |
| `path` | the target is blocked by the filter, or its address is one the TV's rules refuse | unknown |
| `path` | failed, and the gateway by the same uplink completed it | fail |
| `path` | failed, and the gateway by the same uplink did not, or could not try | unknown |
| `blocked` | the filter's own blocked answer (`0.0.0.0`) | pass |
| `blocked` | anything else, an empty or failed answer included | unknown |
| `dot`, `quic` | refused | pass |
| `dot`, `quic` | anything else | fail; unknown with the uplink down |

| `redirect` | none, and the resolver answers nothing there | unknown |

The verdict is the worst step. A failed verdict is taken once more two seconds later before it stands.
- **The walls contact nobody.** DNS over TLS, QUIC and the redirected lookup are aimed at `192.0.2.53`, an address of a documentation range (RFC 5737) that is routed nowhere and is in none of the firewall's sets: a refusal or an answer can only be the gateway's, a far end cannot stand in for the gateway's reject, and a packet a missing wall let out reaches nobody. Together with the neutral target this leaves one kind of packet that leaves the home because of the probe: the handshake with that target.
- **What a pass means, and no more.** Forwarding is on for `TV_IF`; the kernel routes a packet from `TV_IP` out by the uplink and the gateway's answers back by `TV_IF`; and a client with another address, on another port, got every must-allow name from the resolver's policy for any client, was refused at the walls, and (when due) completed one handshake through copies of the TV's chains. It says nothing about the cable, the TV's lease, the pin matching the TV's real address, the guard table (a stale `guard.nft` after a `GW_IP` change drops the TV's DNS while the probe passes), the IPv6 guard, other firewalls on the computer, the URL filter, or any second name or port; and a rule put into the TV's chains by hand after the apply is not in the copies (`ruleset_unchanged` sees that). The probe prints that sentence on a pass: `every step passed for a test client beside the TV's port (the TV itself is not tested)`, and the check `tv_probe` carries it.
- **The narrow exception, and a copy that fails loudly.** Only `TV_PROBE_IP` is taken from the probe's port; any other source there is dropped first, in the two chains its packets enter by. The TV's own source pin is not copied (matched whole, as the script rendered it, not by its words) and not widened. Every probe chain ends in the drop the TV's chain ends in. The pair exists only while a probe runs, its gateway end never has an address, and the probe sends nothing until it has seen its chains in the loaded firewall. `tv_port_neighbours` needs no exception: nothing of the probe's is ever on `TV_IF`. The copy is made only of what can be copied faithfully: a rule of the TV's standing before "not the TV's port", a rule that names the TV's port as the way out, a jump to a chain with no twin, or a verdict the copy does not know (`return`, `queue`, `redirect`, `goto`, `synproxy`, and the rest) stops the derivation with a line naming the rule; no probe rule is loaded for that apply, the probe says it has none, and the check shows a warning. Never a copy that silently differs. Rules copied to the head of a chain are written last first, so they keep the TV's order.
- **Out of the record.** The twin has no counters, no logs and no comments, so no number of the brief moves and no sample reaches the journal. The flow log's event rule, the DNS gate and the capture are not twinned. Every source the ingest reads keeps only `TV_IP`'s rows. The probe's lookups do appear in AdGuard Home's own query log, under its address.
- **The series.** With `TV_PROBE=on`, `tvgw verify --record` runs the probe as the check `tv_probe`, a WARN at most while it is new ([decision 58](#58-the-brief-has-a-plain-form-with-three-tiers-the-login-message-shows-its-status-and-action-and-tvgw-doctor-explains-every-check)), and stores its verdict with the gateway sample of the same run (`gw_samples.tv_probe`, schema 13): one reading every ten minutes of "the TV would have worked".
- **An apply that confirms itself, and never waits to repair.** With `APPLY_CONFIRM=report` or `revert`, `tvgw-apply` first supersedes whatever an earlier apply armed (under the lock, the earlier timer is defused), then asks for a verdict before it changes anything, by `tvgw probe --across`: a verdict before, the one connection out held open, a verdict after. It asks only when what costs nothing says the path is whole: the last probe, at most 15 minutes old, passed (`/run/tvgw/probe.last`, which every probe writes, the verdict after a confirmed apply included, so an apply that follows a failed one is not confirmed until the 10-minute check passes again); `TV_IF` forwards; and where rule 60 stands, table 100 has its default route. Then the probe needs about two seconds, and is given eight. Otherwise (the uplink came back and the apply's job is to put the route back; a fresh boot; a probe that never passed) the verdict before is `unknown`, nothing is kept or armed, and the apply starts at once: an apply whose job is to repair never waits on a probe of what is broken. When the verdict before is `pass` it keeps the live state it manages as last-good under `/run/tvgw/last-good` (the four tables that can refuse or steer the TV's traffic as nft lists them without counters, with a note of which stood; the NTP count table is left alone, since it refuses nothing and is written to keep its counts across applies; the kernel switches it writes; rule 60 and table 100; the two files `tvgw verify` compares with), refuses to keep anything if one listing failed, and arms a restore 75 seconds ahead: a transient systemd timer, which outlives the script and the unit that ran it. Then it applies as before, and waits up to 60 seconds for the verdict after, which is made with a new connection (the held one was made before the apply, and an established connection passes rules a new one meets). `pass` or `unknown` after: the timer is cancelled and the apply stands. `fail` after a `pass` before: `report` changes nothing and writes one line and one `changes` row saying it would have reverted, and why; `revert` puts last-good back (the filter first, in one transaction; then the switches; then the routes, a missing one written before a stray one is removed; the gate's table left as it stands where it stood before, sets and all; each only where it differs; never through the stop path), probes once more, records both verdicts, exits 3 and tries nothing else. A probe that did not pass before the apply gives nothing to go back to, whatever it says after. The restore the timer runs takes the apply lock and does nothing without it.
- **Report before revert, and `revert` refused on a live gateway for now.** The reference gateway runs `report` for two weeks. Every confirmed apply writes its outcome to the change ledger (`apply_confirm`, `apply_would_revert`, `apply_restored`), so the rate of "would have reverted" over applies that were in fact harmless is measured before anything is undone on a verdict. The script ships with `REVERT_SUPPORTED=no`: `APPLY_CONFIRM=revert` says so in its output and acts as `report`. What a revert still lacks before that line may say yes, each a change of its own: (1) a revert `tvgw verify` shows and a person can read: the restore copies `ruleset.applied` and `settings.applied` back, so `applied_current` reads the conf as applied while the old rules are loaded; it needs a check of its own that fails with a line naming what was reverted; (2) a latch: the next apply, two hours later, would apply the same settings, fail and revert again, an outage of 20 to 50 seconds each cycle, so the same settings must not be applied again until something changes; (3) `install.sh` calls `tvgw-apply` bare under `set -e`, so exit 3 would stop the install before `dnsmasq`'s restart and the final verify; (4) last-good and the timer live under `/run`, so after a reboot or a service restart a bad configuration stands unconfirmed; (5) the probe exercises one lookup, one handshake and three walls, so `STRICT_PORTS`, `block4`, the adtech wall, a DoH-list refresh, the gate table and every AdGuard change pass unseen, and a revert would guard none of them; (6) the gate's table is left as it stands at a restore, which is right while it only counts and wrong once a rule in it can hold a packet. And two things in `report` itself, to do before `APPLY_CONFIRM=report` goes on at the reference (review of 2026-11-07): (7) a confirmed apply holds the apply lock while it waits for the verdict after, up to 60 seconds past its last change, so a dispatcher's repair waits behind it exactly when the apply broke the path; the wait should run outside the lock, or the lock be released before it; (8) the eight-second wait for the verdict before is also paid by a repair whose table-100 default route is present but stale (`dhcp4-change` with `WAN_GW_MODE=auto`, the router's address changed), which `confirm_whole` cannot tell from a whole path. The namespace tier runs copies of the script with the line set to yes, so what is built stays tested.

**Consequences.**

- What the probe shares with the TV for real: the resolver and its lists, the URL filter's listeners, rule 60 and table 100 (both chosen by source address), connection tracking, the uplink, the home router. What it crosses as a twin: the filter, the DNS redirect, the masquerade, the mark. What it reads instead of crossing: `TV_IF`'s forwarding switch and the kernel's route for the TV. What it cannot see: the cable, the TV's DHCP lease, `TV_IF`'s other kernel switches, the IPv6 guard on `TV_IF`, a rule put into the TV's chains by hand after an apply (`ruleset_unchanged` sees that), anything keyed on the TV's own address (AdGuard's record-type strip, a per-client rule), and everything that records.
- Against the incident log: the 2026-10-26 fault (forwarding off on `TV_IF`) is caught, by the switch it reads, not by a packet. The 2026-10-30 fault (live connections cut at each apply) is caught only by the connection held across an apply, and only in part. The mechanism, as read from the kernel's address translation and as the namespace tier bears out: a packet of a translated connection that is routed out by another interface ends the connection's entry. With rule 60 out, the held connection's next packet goes that way; in the namespace tier, with the rule held out for two seconds, the probe catches the cut three applies out of three, on amd64 and arm64. It did not at first: the entry was known by its conntrack id alone, and that id is a hash of the entry's address in memory, which the next entry of the cut connection (picked up again at its next keep-alive) often inherits, so about half the cuts read as the same entry (CI, 2026-11-07, 7 of 12 caught over three systems). The entry is now known by its id, its start time and its packet count together (`probe.Entry`), which the apply's `nf_conntrack_timestamp` and `nf_conntrack_acct` settings make available. The held connection carries one keep-alive a second, so a window of milliseconds, as that day's was, is met only when a keep-alive falls in it: a window of ten milliseconds against one packet a second is one apply in a hundred. A stream's traffic met it every time. The 2026-10-27 fault (the capture unreadable) is not caught at all: it lost the record, not the path.
- A false revert is kept away by five things: `unknown` never reverts; the uplink is read before any blame; a failure needs the gateway's own success beside it; it must fail twice; and only a `pass` before the apply makes the apply the cause. The cost is that a fault which also breaks the gateway's own reach reads `unknown`.
- What the probe can cost the TV, and what it cannot. Missing, crashed or hung, its verdict is `unknown`; an apply waits for it only when the path is known whole, at most 8 seconds for the verdict before and 60 for the one after, and then stands; so an apply with `APPLY_CONFIRM` set can take up to 70 seconds longer when a probe hangs, during which the TV's path is whatever the apply has already made of it. A probe's settings that are wrong turn the probe off for that apply and never stop it. A probe ended by a signal removes its namespace, pair, routes and neighbour entry on its way out; the next probe, and an apply with the probe off, remove what one that was killed outright left. Each probe adds and removes a /32 route in the main table and in table 100 (the gateway's answers to the probe come back by rule 60, as the TV's do, and table 100 holds the TV subnet on `TV_IF`): the namespace tier holds a translated TV connection with data in flight across twenty probes and a restore, and it keeps its entry. What is not shown anywhere: the probe beside a VPN client's own rules on the reference, until it runs there.
- A bad apply with the script alive is undone when the second failed verdict is in: about 20 seconds, 50 at most. An apply that was killed is undone when the timer fires: 75 seconds after it was armed, if the lock is free; a timer that finds the lock held does nothing and says so.
- What leaves the home because of the probe, per day. To the TV's vendor: nothing, with any setting; the walls' packets reach nobody. With `TV_PROBE=off`: nothing at all. With `TV_PROBE=on`: 4 TLS handshakes with the neutral server, from the home's address, the TV on or off, and one more for each `sudo tvgw probe`; the server and the networks between see that the address is online, and nothing that says a TV is there. On a gateway with a VPN client the gateway's own list fetches leave by the tunnel, so the handshakes are the first time that server sees the home's own address from the gateway. Also with `TV_PROBE=on`: 144 runs a day, each looking up every must-allow name of the profile (nine for the LG profile), the blocked name and, 4 times a day, the target's name, at the gateway's resolver, which answers from its cache and asks its upstream (the DoH operator, never the vendor) when an answer has expired. AdGuard Home's own query log holds those lookups under `TV_PROBE_IP`, for `QUERYLOG_DAYS`; tvgw's ingest never reads them. With `APPLY_CONFIRM=report` or `revert`: two to six handshakes more per confirmed apply: one before (held open), one after, one more when a failure is taken again, and each of those doubled when the first neutral server does not answer and the second is tried; about 13 applies a day on the reference (the uplink's renewals and the daily list refresh), so about 26 when each is confirmed and every first handshake completes, 78 at the most.
- A profile with no must-allow host (the general profile) has no `lookup` step, which is said; its `path`, its walls and its port are probed.
- A control that will refuse traffic in a later pass is applied through this: its rule goes into the TV's chains, so the twin carries it with no further work, and its promotion from counting is an apply the probe judges. A control that lives outside those chains (a table of its own, as the DNS gate) needs its own rule for the probe's port before the probe can judge it.

| Decision | Setting | Default |
|---|---|---|

| The probe's address | `TV_PROBE_IP` | `10.10.10.30` |

## 66. An upload budget with a ceiling set once, count-only, and the names of a TV that goes online while off

*2026-11-05, from research pass 4 (its invention table's upload budget row, "Three of these give the most protection for the least risk", and build item 4). Built the same day, count-only: nothing is capped, and nothing new can refuse the TV anything. The measure and the ceiling rule were redesigned before any deploy, after an evaluation on the reference gateway's own record, and an independent review on 2026-11-06 tightened the rest.*

**Context.** Tracking that survives a name filter rides on names nobody has classified and on shared cloud hosts. What such an upload cannot hide is its size. IMC 2024 measured LG's screen-recognition host at 4,125 to 4,948 KB in a one-hour run while it fingerprinted (HDMI, antenna and FAST), against 214.9 to 240.4 KB in every other scenario: Ethernet frames, both ways ([deep dive 13, section 1.3](research/deep-dive-2026-11/13-gap-reconcile-the-two-incompatible-acr-detec.md#13-imc-2024s-units-and-whether-07s-17-19-kb-is-05s-24-kb-settled-from-the-authors-code-cross-checked-on-the-trace)). [Decision 45](#45-the-acr-red-line-is-an-upload-test-and-a-port-wall)'s upload test recognizes that shape after the fact, by its ratio of up to down and its connection count; an upload of another shape had no bound at all, and on the measured day of 2026-11-01, 28 names no category covers carried 2.7 GB. Research pass 3 rejected learned "abnormal upload" alarms of Firewalla's kind, which come late and raise false alarms ([R1](research/deep-dive-2026-11-04/R1-adjacent-repos.md)): a threshold that follows the data makes a slow rise normal and a quiet week's normal tomorrow's alarm, and it pages, which [decision 14](#14-red-lines-are-the-alarm-model) does not. Separately, the brief's "While off or asleep" line counted the TV's lookups and connections between two readings that found it off, but named nothing, never reached the plain brief, and counted `unknown`, the reading of a TV nothing could be asked of, as off.

The first build measured each destination-hour by the brief's gross payload bound up, `queries.payload_bound()`, and set the ceiling at ten times the largest clean hour. Run read-only over the reference gateway's 12-day record on 2026-11-05 (576 counted flows, 205 destination-hours), it could not work. The gross bound takes 40 bytes a packet off conntrack's bytes, but a bare acknowledgement carries 12 to 40 bytes more of TCP options (timestamps, and SACK blocks on a lossy Wi-Fi uplink), so the largest destination-hour of the record was a live-video delivery host: one connection, a very large download, and 55.9 MB "up" that was its acknowledgements. Ten times that is a ceiling of 559 MB, 191 times `ACR_HOUR_NET` below: the budget would never have fired. Netted against what came back on each connection, the same record held only upload-heavy hours: a telemetry host's 2.51 MB on the record's first day (a tracking category), 1.01 MB to a shopping and video company's tracker, 0.86 MB in a sports app's own busiest hour, 0.59 MB to an unnamed /24, and less everywhere else; the live-video host and a CDN's downloads netted nothing. On this TV ordinary upload-heavy hours and an hour of screen recognition are about three times apart, not a hundred, so ten times any of them would let screen recognition through ([what an LG CX does, measured](tv/lg-cx-measured.md#the-upload-budget-measured-on-its-own-record)).

**Decision.**

- **What the budget bounds** (`src/tvgw/budget.py`). Per local clock hour, what the TV sent each destination outside the profile's expected categories beyond what came back. "Expected" is the profile's own `expected_categories`, the set the ACR test and "Allowed, outside the expected categories" already use (on `lg-webos`: Streaming, Infra, LG core and FAST), because those are the TV doing its job: video, its apps' own interfaces, CDNs, and the maker's clock, login, store and firmware, where a large upload is the household's own use. Everything else is bounded: `Other`, the tracking categories a lookup should never have reached, and connections no hello named. A destination is `queries.acr_cell()`, the registrable domain of the connection's TLS name or its server's /24, so rotating names and the addresses behind one name are one destination; a flow counts in the hour it opened in, as the ACR test's do. A must-allow host is never counted: by name, the host or a name under it, as its `$important` exception covers them; with no name, an address an A answer for one returned in the window or the day before.
- **The measure is net, per connection** (`budget.net_up()`): the brief's payload bound up less the same bound down, never below zero, summed over the destination-hour's connections. The same estimator on both sides nets content against content; a download's acknowledgements, however many options they carry, are far fewer bytes than the segments they acknowledge, so a download nets nothing. Netting per connection, never per destination, means a download on one connection never hides an upload on another, and also that a destination's hour can net more than the destination as a whole sent beyond what it returned; every line says "beyond what came back on the same connections". The gross bound up stays beside the net in the brief. The gross bound cannot be the measure: on the reference's record it made a live-video download the largest upload of 12 days.
- **What it cannot see.** An upload inside a connection that returns at least as many bytes, such as telemetry riding a connection that also downloads; and an upload spread so that no destination-hour passes the ceiling: across clock hours, across destinations, across registrable domains that rotate, or, unnamed, across several /24s. It bounds volume to one destination in one hour, not identity.
- **A fixed ceiling, bounded above.** The ceiling is `ceiling_for()` the largest clean destination-hour of the `BUDGET_DAYS = 14` whole local days before the day it is set: `BUDGET_TIMES = 2` times it, never under `BUDGET_FLOOR`, 512 KiB, and never over `BUDGET_CAP`, half of `ACR_HOUR_NET`. `ACR_HOUR_NET`, 2,930,000 bytes, is an estimate of the net of IMC's quietest fingerprinting hour, 4,125 KB of frames, on two assumptions: a ratio of 7 up to 1 down, the lowest daily ratio of the fingerprint port in the *Watching the Watchers* trace, applied to IMC's domain-wide frame totals; and every upload frame full-size, with the whole down side taken as payload (computed, rounded down). At the trace's lowest domain-wide ratio, 4.5, the same hour nets about 2.48 MB. So no ceiling the rule sets lets that hour under it: at the assumed ratio it is twice over the cap, at 4.5 about 1.7 times; only `UPLOAD_CEILING`, the owner's, goes higher. The multiplier is 2: on the reference's record the largest ordinary hour netted 864,544 bytes, ten times would be 8.6 MB, three times the ACR hour, and twice lets an ordinary hour double without an event; there, twice is over the cap, so the cap decides. The floor is about twice IMC's busiest hour without fingerprinting, 240.4 KB of frames both ways, which bounds that hour's net from above, and under a fifth of `ACR_HOUR_NET`: a new install catches an hour of LG's screen recognition from its first day, and that background level never reaches the floor. A largest clean hour at or above the cap does not raise the ceiling: it is fixed at the cap (`at_cap`), and both briefs say plainly that this TV's own uploads reach the size the budget watches for, the engineering brief naming the destination-hour, so the owner can look at it. A destination-hour is clean unless the ACR test flagged that destination on that day, and a connection under a known tracking category adds nothing to it, so neither can raise the ceiling.
- **Set once, for this rule, profile and TV, from covered days.** The hourly ingest's `budget` step sets the ceiling at its first run 3 hours (`SETTLE`) after a midnight that ends 14 whole days the record covers, and only when each of those days is covered: it has flows to public servers, and at least `CAPTURE_NAMED`, 90%, of its TCP 443 connections carry a TLS name. A day the capture missed would leave the TV's streaming unnamed, counted under its /24 outside the expected categories, where it could lift the ceiling to the cap for good; until 14 covered days in a row exist the row stays pending and says so. The row records the rule's version (`RULE = 2`; 1 was the gross bound at ten times, never deployed), its measure, the profile and the TV's model where the readings name one. The brief honours no row of another rule, profile or TV, and no stored ceiling above the current cap. The ingest computes a row of another rule again from the 14 days before; for another profile or TV it waits for 14 days after the change, since the old history describes another TV. A fixed row of this rule, profile and TV is never computed again. `UPLOAD_CEILING` replaces it with the owner's own size. `sudo tvgw budget --rederive` sets it again from the 14 days before today, keeping the record: the store's rows are untouched, the new row keeps the old ceiling as `previous`, the daily files already written keep the ceiling they were written with, and a brief computed again for an earlier day uses the new one. `tvgw forget` removes the row with the record, and the next covered history sets it again. Until it is set the brief judges against the floor and says why and when; on the reference gateway, whose record starts on 2026-10-27, that is the first run after 03:00 on 2026-11-11 once those days are covered. A store that keeps 14 days or fewer (`DB_DAYS`) never holds the history whole, and keeps the floor.
- **Late connections.** A flow reaches the store only when conntrack destroys it, and each daily file is written once, 3 hours after its day ends; a connection opened on a day and still open then (a long-lived upload channel, or one opened late in the evening) is in no daily file, and its bytes later land in that day's opening hour, which no daily file reads again. So each brief also takes every connection that ended in its window after its own day's file was written, however long ago it opened, and lists that connection's destination-hour under `late` when the hour's whole net, now known, is over the ceiling, with the part that came late. The derivation cannot see a connection still open when it runs.
- **Count-only first.** A destination-hour over the ceiling is a "would have capped" row of the engineering brief's "Upload budget" section, with its destination, hour, net, gross bound and the ceiling, and the plain brief carries one `Uploads:` line on a day with one, counting clock hours, never destination-hours. It is not a red line in this pass and never an action, so it never reaches `action.txt`; it is not in the analyst's pack; nothing is capped. The count first shows what a cap would cost: a hit on a destination the household wants is a cap that would have broken it.
- **Enforcement later, as a design only.** In its own table, a dynamic set keyed by the TV's destination address whose elements carry a `quota` and a timeout of an hour, filled from the TV's forward path, and over the quota `reject with tcp reset`, so the app sees a refusal at once and not a stall, with a counter and a sampled log as the port wall has. The kernel cannot net a connection's replies against it, so the quota counts only packets that carry data, an `ip length` above that of a bare acknowledgement with its options, which leaves a download's acknowledgements out. The must-allow hosts' and the expected categories' addresses are exempt before it, from a set the resolver's answers fill as the DNS gate's feeder fills `resolved4`, and so is the gateway. The kernel counts by address and from an element's first packet, not by name and clock hour, so its ceiling is the brief's plus the header allowance of the busiest clean hour, and a destination spread over many addresses has one quota each. It goes on probation with automatic undo: the probe from the TV's position (research pass 4, item 5) and a revert on any hour in which a must-allow or clock lookup fails or the judge's drop-in-viewing rule fires. It waits for two weeks of this count with no hit on a destination the owner recognizes as wanted, for that undo, and for a check that the gateway's kernel takes a quota per set element. None of it is built.
- **The tripwire for a TV that speaks while off.** Measured on the reference gateway on 2026-11-05 over 2026-10-26 to 2026-11-05: in the 29 hours bounded by two hourly readings that found the TV unreachable, it opened 0 connections and made 0 lookups. Cutting the TV's path while it is off would gain nothing on this TV today, so that control is not built. The notice when it changes is: the brief's "While off or asleep" section, the one the brief already had, names up to `STANDBY_NAMES = 5` names behind its counts (`queries.standby_names()`, by lookup, by the connection's TLS name, or by its server's address). Its hours and the both-ends rule agree: an off stretch is the time between two consecutive readings that both say off and are at most `STANDBY_GAP` (90 minutes) apart, so an hour with a missed reading is never off, and a screen saver is the TV on. One difference is corrected: a reading of `unknown` or no power at all (`NO_POWER`) is no reading, a TV that answered without a power state or one nothing could be asked of, and an hour with one at either end is no longer off. Before, a TV whose key the TV had refused read every hour as off, and every evening's traffic as traffic while off. So that the correction does not make the tripwire silently blind, those hours are listed where the brief lists what it could not check.
- **Its evidence, and its tier.** The readings alone cannot say the TV was off: they miss a short session two times in three, and a TV whose LAN API stops answering while it is on reads unreachable all evening. So the plain brief's `While off:` line needs the TV port's own byte counters to agree where the gateway samples them (`queries.standby_port()`): when a sample interval overlapping the stretches the TV talked in carried at least `activity.AWAKE_BYTES` in 10 minutes, someone was using the TV, the plain brief says nothing and the engineering line says the counters show it in use. With quiet counters the line says the TV goes online while it is off; with no samples it gives both explanations. By the rules of [the red lines](use/daily-brief.md#red-lines) it is not one: "the TV went online while off" is not a sentence that must never be true while the readings can miss a session, and an act-tier line would send the household to the TV for a session it had itself. It stays among the lines worth an answer.
- **No line the owner learns to ignore.** A plain line, `While off:` or `Uploads:`, that has come `REPEAT_DAYS = 3` calendar days running says so once, then stays out of the plain brief until it changes (a day without it, or a name or destination the earlier days did not have); the engineering brief keeps it, with the days running. The at-cap case says in the plain line why such hours are listed.

**Consequences.**

- What the budget is not: it bounds what a destination nobody classified receives in an hour beyond what it sends back on the same connections, not who receives it, and it cannot see tracking inside a host the profile expects, such as a streaming service's own interface (Disney+'s edge interface sent 33.9 MB up on the measured day, in Streaming), nor an upload inside a connection that returns as much, nor one spread below the ceiling. A long connection's whole net lands in the hour it opened, which overstates that hour; one still open when its day is filed counts late.
- The ceiling is only as clean as the 14 days it came from, and the cap bounds the damage: a tracker whose upload outside the expected categories was the largest of them, and that the ACR test did not flag, can raise the ceiling to the cap and no further, and the brief names the destination-hour the ceiling came from so the owner can judge it. Under the cap an hour of LG's screen recognition at IMC's quietest is at least about 1.7 times over the ceiling, and twice at the assumed ratio; that margin is an estimate on the two assumptions above, held by a positive control at each corner in the tests. An owner's `UPLOAD_CEILING` at or above `ACR_HOUR_NET` makes the brief say it would not count one.
- On the reference's record the rule gives the cap, 1,465,000 bytes (1.4M), since twice its sports app's busiest hour is more; that hour is under it, so the ceiling is not `at_cap`. Before the window, the telemetry host's 2.51 MB hour would have been an event.
- Coverage can hold the ceiling at the floor: one day in 14 without flows or with its capture missing restarts the wait, so a gateway that is often off for whole days fixes its ceiling late.
- The derivation names a destination and an hour, a small viewing record in `meta`, which no pruning reaches: the ingest drops the name and hour once they are older than `DB_DAYS`, and `tvgw forget` removes the whole row (`forget.META_FORGET`).
- The brief and the hourly ingest each name the window's flows once more, as the ACR test does; a calendar-day brief with an event names up to three days before it again, for its days running; the ingest's derivation names 14 days of them once. A failure of either is a report warning or an ingest warning, and a row the code did not write prints as a section that could not be shown; nothing else changes: the budget touches neither the firewall nor the TV's path.
- On a gateway whose TV reports `unknown` (not paired, a refused key), the While off line now reads no off hours where it read every hour, and a `Not checked:` line names those hours. The reference reports `Active`, `Screen Saver` or `unreachable` and reads as before.

**Owner decisions, held behind settings.**

|---|---|---|
| Whether the in-path filler runs beside the passive feeder on this gateway | `DNSGATE_FILL` | `passive` |
| The upload budget's ceiling | `UPLOAD_CEILING` | `auto`: the gateway's own, set once from its 14 covered days, between the floor and the cap (the floor until then) |

## 67. The TV's own account of itself is the ground: sessions, a receipt and a replay keyed to the app in front; a live reading that never knocks blind; no rule yet

*2026-11-05, from research pass 4 (its section "Invention: no other system keys its rules to the TV's own state", the candidate "Foreground-app least privilege") and [deep dive 14](research/deep-dive-2026-11/14-gap-live-tv-state-through-ssap-subscriptions.md). Built: the sessions, the attribution, the pictures, `tvgw receipt` with its four views, the brief's section, the evaluation, and the live reading as a service that is off by default. Not built, and not tried in this pass: any rule that refuses traffic by the app in front. The live reading has not run on the reference gateway yet.*

**Context.** Every other tool that watches a home network keys what it reports to the device as a whole. A TV says more about itself than that: whether it is on, and which app is in front. tvgw read that once an hour (`tvstate`), and a reading once an hour cannot say which app a connection belongs to: on the reference gateway's first 28 days of record, 12,165 connections, the hourly readings place 61.3% of the connections and 64.4% of the bytes in a session, and 38.7% of the connections start in an hour whose two readings disagree or are missing. Research pass 4 found no product and no paper that joins a TV's own state with its traffic at a gateway. The rule that would follow, "while an app is in front, allow only what that app needs", has more ways to spoil an evening than anything tvgw has built: a late signal fails an app's start, the signal comes from the device being policed, and a content network an app has never used looks exactly like a stranger. So this decision builds the ground and the measuring instrument, and writes down what would have to be true before a rule is tried.

**Decision.**

- **Sessions.** `src/tvgw/appfront.py` cuts any window into sessions (one app in front from start to end), gaps (the TV may have been on and nothing says which app was in front) and none (the TV said it was off at both ends). Inside the time a live session to the TV was up, only its events count; outside it, only the hourly readings: two readings at most `STANDBY_GAP` apart, both awake, naming the same app. Nothing is guessed: a lost session leaves a gap even when the same app is in front before and after it, and a clock step leaves a gap as long as the step. The home screen, inputs (HDMI, live TV) and cast receivers are sessions of their own kind. The screen saver keeps the app in front, and its time is counted apart.
- **Attribution is a match in time, and says so.** A connection belongs to the session that holds the time of its own TLS hello ([decision 21](#21-a-continuation-inherits-its-name)'s continuation inherits its hello, and so its session), or conntrack's start where no hello was captured. A connection in the `LEAD_SECONDS` (3) before an app change the TV reported belongs to the new app; the number is deep dive 14's guess until the evaluation has measured it on a live record. No report says an app "sent" anything. `receipt.FORBIDDEN` lists the words no line may use, and a test holds every line the receipt can write to it.
- **A name is sorted before any app is considered.** `appfront.classify` gives each name one class from the profile: `platform` (the must-allow hosts, the clock hosts, the vendor's expected category), `vendor` (the TV maker's other categories), `tracking` (ACR and third-party advertising and measurement), `content` (the other expected categories), `shared` (Infra), `other`, and `unclassified` (no category in the profile). Platform and vendor names are never an app's. A tracking name can be usual company for an app and is never called the app's own. An unclassified name is "not classified yet", never somebody else's: on the reference one app's whole video came from a delivery host the profile did not know.
- **The unit of a picture is the registrable domain with its category.** Leaving one viewing day out and testing it against the picture from the other days, the full host name kept 19.1% of one app's bytes and 0.1% of another's inside the picture, because delivery hosts are numbered per session; the registrable domain kept 100.0% of both, and 99.3 to 99.9% of every app's connections. The category is in the unit because one registrable domain can hold an app's video and its measurement host, and the two must not be one unit.
- **One earlier day puts a unit in a picture; its days are always shown.** A rule of three earlier days put 49.3% of one app's connections outside its own picture on the same record, and 6.5% of another's content bytes: an app's less common names show up on few days, and the hourly readings miss the hour an app starts in. So `PICTURE_MIN_DAYS = 1`, every report says how many days a unit was seen on (one is "thin", `PICTURE_FIRM_DAYS = 2` or more "firm"), a name is "new" under an app when no earlier viewing day has it, and nothing is called new until the app has `PICTURE_READY_DAYS = 3` earlier days. A viewing day runs from 05:00 to 05:00. A picture is learned from the last 28 days.
- **The TLS stack is a second witness.** An app that brings its own TLS library sends hellos with its own JA4. `appfront.Stacks` holds deep dive 14's rule with a lower floor: a fingerprint with 0.9 of at least 100 hellos under one app names that app, and one seen under three apps or more with none above 0.5 is the system's. On the reference's record two stacks named one app at purity 1.0 and 0.955, three leaned to another without the hellos to name it, and one TLS 1.2 stack appeared under four apps. The stack names the software that opened a connection whichever app was in front, so it can say whose connection a gap held, and when another app's software talked during a session. The receipt uses it only as a remark on a new name; the evaluation measures how often it is right on a day left out.
- **The receipt, the picture, the replay and why** are `tvgw receipt` and its `--picture`, `--replay` and `--why NAME` ([the commands](reference/commands.md)). The brief gains a short section, "App in front", only on a gateway whose store holds a live record, so every other gateway's brief and its JSON are what they were.
- **The replay is a report.** For the imagined rule "while an app with three earlier days is in front, allow what was seen with it before and the platform's essentials, refuse what is new", it prints per app what would have been refused, what was allowed on one day's evidence, and the two numbers that decide: the share of streaming bytes outside the picture, and the count of must-allow names outside it. The rule carries the must-allow list, so that count can only be other than 0 through a bug; the replay also names any must-allow host that only the list protects.
- **The live reading** is `tvgw-tvlive.service`, off unless `LIVE_READING=on`, for the LG profile only. It holds at most one read-only session (register with the stored key, subscribe to the power state and the foreground app, ping) with the WebSocket client `tvgw tvprobe` already had, and writes each change to `tvlive-events.log`, which the hourly ingest loads into `tv_events` (schema 12) beside the hourly readings, never among them: everything that counts `tvstate` rows counts what it did.
- **It never knocks blind.** LG locks a TV out after too many pairing requests, and the lock clears only by unplugging the TV. Before every connection attempt all of this holds, and when any of it does not, no packet is sent (`tvlive.Discipline`):
  - the key file holds a key and the key store records no refusal, read again just before the attempt. Only an explicit denial from the TV (a `type: error`, a 403) is written where the hourly reading obeys it, so one denial stops both until `tvgw tv pair` ([decision 54](#54-a-tvs-lan-api-is-a-module-the-profile-names-a-reading-never-pairs-a-profile-declares-what-it-can-check)). A prompt that the TV does not follow with a denial (a slow `registered`, a reset, a close) is never written to the shared key store, so a transient at a wake moment never stops the hourly reading; the daemon counts it a failed attempt and stands itself down after `PROMPT_LIMIT` (3) of them. When the TV has itself forgotten the key, one prompt can appear, once: the TV decides that only after the registration is sent, which is the hourly reading's exposure today;
  - there is evidence that the TV is on, newer than the last attempt: at least `EVIDENCE_BYTES` (8,000) from the TV on its port inside `EVIDENCE_SECONDS` (30), counted from after the last attempt ended, or an hourly reading that found the TV awake. After `TRAFFIC_TRUSTED` (3) failures in a row only a reading counts, which is one attempt an hour at most, until the port has been silent for 10 minutes: the TV went off, and its next evening is tried on the first traffic again. There is no timer that tries without evidence, and the first attempt after a wake waits `BOOT_GUARD` (75 seconds) while webOS finishes booting;
  - the back-off has run: 15, 30, 60, 120, 300, 600, then 900 seconds after each failure, never under 15 seconds, and 120 seconds after the TV said it was switching off. The counts return to zero only after a session that stayed up 10 minutes: a TV that takes the connection and says off at once, or holds a minute and drops, is a failure, so the real ceiling is `SERVICE_LIMIT` an hour (6, or 144 a day), not a rate that a quick standby makes unbounded;
  - the hour has room: every connection of this service and of every reading (`lgwebos.read`: the hourly one, the canary's, `tvgw tvcheck` by hand) is written to `tvlive-attempts` before the connect, and the service attempts only while the last hour holds fewer than `SERVICE_LIMIT` (6) by anyone. With the hourly reading's two that is `CEILING` (8). The reading is counted and never refused: it carries the Live Plus and Wi-Fi red lines. The file outlives the process (and is cut to the last hour on every write, so it is no record of when the TV was on), the process also counts by the monotonic clock, and a file that cannot be read or written means no attempt. `tvgw forget` empties the file in place, and the service makes a missing one again, so the reading never stops counting;
  - the hourly reading is not at work (120 seconds after one of its attempts);
  - the service has not stood down. It stands down, leaving a marker only `sudo tvgw tvlive --resume` removes, when an hourly reading fails to reach a TV a minute or more into a held session twice (`CONFLICT_LIMIT`: one reading ends the session and backs off, two mean two sessions on one key do not get along), when the TV refuses a subscription, and after `GIVE_UP` (12) failed attempts with no session between them that held 10 minutes, whatever silence lay between. A session in which the TV floods the events (`FLOOD_PER_MINUTE`, 120 messages a minute) is ended, and is one of those failures. The give-up counts are kept in `tvlive-progress` and read again at start, so a crash loop or a forward clock step does not buy a fresh first knock.
- **It lets go when the TV says it is switching off.** Nobody has measured whether a held session delays this TV's standby (deep dive 14, T-P2), so the service closes its session itself, with a close frame, at the first off message. A session that is lost is recorded as lost and is never read as "the TV went off".
- **If it dies, hangs or is absent, the TV does not notice.** Nothing the TV needs depends on it. Every wait has a limit; the loop reports to systemd's watchdog; a crash comes back after a minute and finds the hour's count and the give-up count in its files; a write to the key store or a marker that fails (a locked or read-only disk) ends the attempt and stands the daemon down rather than crashing it. The receipt uses the hourly readings for the time the service did not cover and says so on each session.
- **The finer record is kept short and stays on the gateway.** `tv_events` holds the app in front to the second, which is more than the hourly readings say. Its rows sit in the store (`root:adm 640`, so the admin group reads them as it reads the TV's whole DNS history), and the live reading's own log, `tvlive-events.log`, is root's alone (`0600`). It is kept `TV_EVENTS_DAYS` (14) days (pruned by that span at the hourly ingest, whether or not the log is still there), removed by `tvgw forget`, never part of the analyst's pack, and never written into a persisted report: the brief's "App in front" section and `tvgw receipt` are computed only for an interactive run (`app_in_front` is False for `tvgw-analyze`), so the minute-level record does not outlive `TV_EVENTS_DAYS` in `reports/`. The evaluation prints app identifiers, registrable domains, categories and counts only, never a time.

**The road to a rule (not built).** What a rule keyed to the app in front would be, so that the next pass argues with a design and not with an idea:

- **States.** *Open*: no rule applies. *App X*: the rule for X applies. The rule is open while the TV is off, on the home screen, on an input or a cast receiver, under an app that has fewer than three earlier days, for a grace period after every app change, and whenever the signal is not live.
- **What it refuses under app X.** A name that is new under X and is either tracking or `other`, or is firm in another app's picture and not in X's (another app talking in the background; the TLS stack corroborates). It lets through, and reports, a new name that is content, shared or unclassified: a content network an app has not used before looks like this (the 6.5% above), and the three options were to allow such categories by class, to let the name through on first sight and take it into the picture the next day, or to refuse it. The second is chosen: the first makes the rule blind to a new delivery name, and the third breaks the evening the picture exists to protect.
- **At an app change.** Connections that are open stay open: the rule acts on new lookups and new connections only. For the grace period the rule is open, so the old app winds down and the new one starts. An app that switches in directly from another app opens connections before the TV reports the change; whether the grace period can cover that is the evaluation's `lead` table, which has no data until a live record exists.
- **How it would be enforced.** Through the in-path filler of the address gate that this pass builds count-only: it sees every answer before the TV does, so a name outside the allowed set can be answered as refused, and only the addresses of allowed names enter the gate's set. One set per state, swapped whole at a change.
- **When the signal is lost the rule opens, by the kernel's clock.** The state "app X" would be an element with a timeout of about 90 seconds that the live reading renews while its session answers pings. A service that died, hung, stood down or lost the TV stops renewing, and the kernel itself returns to open. The rule can fail to protect; it cannot fail closed.
- **The numbers that must hold first**, from `tvgw receipt --replay` and `--evaluate` on the gateway where it would run: at least 28 app-days replayed, nine in ten of them timed by the live reading; streaming bytes outside the picture at most 1%; must-allow names outside it 0; at most 5% of bytes on connections with no name; the hourly readings agreeing with the live record on power and app at 99% of the readings taken while it was up; the live reading's busiest hour at or under its limit and no stand-down in 14 days. `receipt.meets_the_bar` holds the first four.
- **The undo it needs.** The probe from the TV's position and the confirm-and-revert that this pass builds for the firewall. On probation the rule returns itself to open, and records why, when a must-allow or clock lookup fails, when an app goes back to the home screen within a minute of starting twice in an evening, or when the judge's drop-in-viewing rule fires.

**Consequences.** With the default settings nothing changes on any install but one line of `tvgw size`: the service exits at once, the attempts file is never made, the hourly reading writes what it wrote, and the brief is byte for byte what it was. `tvgw receipt` works on any gateway with hourly readings, at their precision: the hour an app starts in and the hour it ends in are gaps. A session's bytes keep arriving for up to 3 hours after it ends, because conntrack logs a connection when it lets it go; the receipt marks such a session as settling. A connection's bytes are counted where it started, also when it ran on after a switch. What the receipt calls new is new under that app in the last 28 days, nothing more: an app's own rare name shows up as new once, and the next day it is in the picture. The service runs as root, as the hourly reading does, because the pairing key is root's; its unit drops every capability and may reach the TV's address alone. The picture of a household's apps by day is a viewing record of its own kind, and it is kept no longer than the events it is made from.

**Owner decisions, held behind settings.**

| Decision | Setting | Default |
|---|---|---|
| Whether the gateway holds a live session to the TV | `LIVE_READING` | `off` |
| How long the record of the app in front, to the second, is kept | `TV_EVENTS_DAYS` | `14` |
| Whether to pair again with a read-only key of the service's own, so that it need not run as root (needs the owner at the TV) | no setting | the hourly reading's key, as root |

*Note (2026-11-05): deep dive 14's carrier inference does not hold on the reference TV.* Its F4 reads `ignore-carrier=yes` in the NetworkManager file as a sign that the TV port loses its link when the TV powers off, and builds its reconnect rule on that: try while the carrier is up, stop when it is down. On the reference gateway the kernel counted no carrier loss on the TV port between 2026-11-01 and 2026-11-05 while 35 hourly readings found the TV unreachable: this CX keeps its link up while it is off. A loop that waits for the carrier would have tried the TV every 60 seconds all night, which is what `tvgw tvprobe` does there. The live reading therefore takes "the TV is on" from the port's traffic and the hourly reading, and uses the carrier only to forbid an attempt when it is down. In the 29 hours the TV was off at both ends of an hour it opened no connection and made no lookup, and the hourly reading's attempts did not wake it.

## 73. The store refuses a newer version and commits durably; the gateway samples its own health; the QUIC refusal is watched; an encrypted copy leaves the box

*2026-11-08, from research pass 5 (internal note sections 3.4, 5.2 and 7 item 10; external note B6 and C), the reliability floor, and from the coordinator's reading of the reference gateway that morning (the gateway's own lookups at a reboot). Nothing here refuses the TV anything or changes `tvgw-apply`'s firewall; the one new table, behind `VPN_DNS_SEAL`, which is off, refuses only the gateway's own plain lookups. The schema number, 14, is provisional until the pass lands.*

**Context.** Several gaps could cost the record or the evening without a line saying so. `db.connect()` wrote `SCHEMA_VERSION` into `meta` on every writer's open, so an older tvgw installed over a newer one, which is how a bad release is rolled back today ([decision 53](#53-releases-carry-a-hash-checked-bootstrap-for-two-architectures-with-provenance-under-immutable-releases-updates-are-checked-weekly-installed-only-by-the-user-judged-by-verify-and-rolled-back-a-new-settings-key-never-changes-the-meaning-of-an-old-keys-value)), relabelled the store with its own lower number and left the newer tables and rows behind with nobody responsible for them. Nothing sampled the gateway's temperature, its throttling, its memory, the card's writes or the kernel's disk errors and USB resets, on an overclocked Pi that had one watchdog reset (2026-10-31) beside a USB disk failing its SMART checks since 2026-10-25. The store ran `synchronous=FULL` with its rollback journal ([decision 10](#10-a-rollback-journal-not-wal)), which makes a commit atomic but not durable: the commit is the unlink of the journal, FULL does not sync the directory after it, and a cut before the filesystem journals that unlink brings the journal back, so the next open rolls the committed transaction back. Nothing had ever cut the power under the store, the settings file or the machine's rules file, and the rules file and the live reading's give-up counts were renamed into place without an fsync.

**Decision.**

- **The version only goes up.** A writer reads `meta.schema` before it writes anything, the journal mode included, and raises `db.StoreTooNew`, a subclass of `sqlite3.Error`, having written nothing, when the version is higher than `SCHEMA_VERSION` or is not a whole number. Every command that writes the store says so in one line and exits 1 (`cli.main()`); `tvgw verify --record` prints the line as the run it could not record; the analyst's two writer connections now go through `db.connect()` too. A reader still opens such a store: reading changes nothing and the schema only adds. The version row is written only when it rises, so a writer's open of an up-to-date store writes nothing, where every open used to replace the row, a journal and three fsyncs on the card each time. `tvgw verify` gains `store_version`, which FAILs at once, not held at WARN as the new checks are, when the store is newer than the code, naming both versions and the two ways out: a rollback deploy ends with a plain `tvgw verify`, which reads the store and would otherwise pass while every writer refused it.
- **`synchronous=EXTRA`.** NORMAL is out with a rollback journal: SQLite documents a window for corruption on power loss there, and only WAL makes it safe. WAL stays out for decision 10's reason, unprivileged readers. Between FULL and EXTRA the difference is one fsync of the directory per commit, after the journal's unlink, and it is what makes an acknowledged commit survive a cut; every writer now opens with EXTRA (`db.SYNCHRONOUS`). Its cost is measured by `tools/sync_bench.py`, which commits one small row per transaction in each mode and, on Linux, reads the device's own counters of sectors written and cache flushes per commit. On the development machine (macOS, APFS, whose fsync does not flush the drive's cache, 2026-11-08, 300 commits a mode) FULL and EXTRA both took a median of about 0.3 ms a commit, which says only that the order costs nothing there; the card's number is the gateway's to give, by the bench run on a directory of the card. The store commits a few dozen times an hour (an ingest per batch and per pcap, a verify run, an hourly check), so EXTRA adds a few dozen directory fsyncs an hour; the up-to-date open above takes away the commit every writer's open used to make, about ten an hour with two or three fsyncs each, so the total stays about where it was.
- **A file replaced whole, by one order.** `durable.replace()` writes a new file beside the old one, fsyncs it, sets its mode and owner, renames it over the old name and fsyncs the directory. The machine's rules file (`autoblock`), its restore after a failed site-rule change (`localrules.write_auto`) and the live reading's give-up counts (`tvlive.write_progress`, whose loss would buy the TV fresh knocks on its pairing) go through it now; the settings file (`discover.write_conf`), the site rules and AdGuard Home's configuration already kept that order.
- **The power cut, tested.** LazyFS, which the research proposed, is a research prototype tested on Ubuntu 20.04 that CI would have to build from a third party's source, and its README does not say whether a cut loses a rename or an unlink, which is exactly the loss in question; it was not used. The namespace tier cuts at the block device instead, with the kernel's own device-mapper target and no new code from outside: ext4 on a loop device behind device-mapper, and the cut is dm-flakey's `drop_writes`, the way the kernel's filesystem tests cut power (`tests/virtual/test_virtual_powercut.py`). Every writer is the gateway's code in its own process. With EXTRA the store keeps every acknowledged commit, whole, also when cut in the middle of a transaction; with FULL, the control, the last acknowledged commit rolls back. The settings file, the rules file and the give-up counts come back as the acknowledged version; a rename without an fsync, the second control, loses it. The unit tier kills each writer with SIGKILL at fixed random instants (`tests/unit/test_killed_writers.py`), which proves the order and the transactions but not a cut, since a kill keeps the page cache; a file written in place is its control. Neither models a card that acknowledges a flush it did not do: no software order survives that, and only a better card or a battery-backed one does. The gate's reboot list that the research named does not exist yet; when it is built it is written with `durable.replace()`, and its writer joins the cut's cases.
- **The gateway's own health, sampled** (`src/tvgw/health.py`, schema 14, provisional). `tvgw verify --record`'s sample gains nine columns: the hottest thermal zone, the Raspberry Pi firmware's throttle flags (`vcgencmd get_throttled`), `MemAvailable`'s share and the kernel's cumulative memory-pressure stall, the disk under `STATE_DIR` and its sectors written since boot, and the kernel's lines since the previous sample, across boots, that report a disk error (on any disk, and on the record's) and a USB reset (`journalctl _TRANSPORT=kernel`; `-k` would read this boot alone and miss the minutes before a crash). Each source is optional, as the sampler's others are, and a source that cannot say is NULL, never zero. `health.findings()` reads a window against the one as long before it and names only what needs the owner: the firmware slowing the gateway (a condition holding at a sample, or newly happened since boot, so an old event does not come back every day), any disk error on the record's disk, and disk errors elsewhere or USB resets that more than doubled against the window before (`health.rising()`), so the reference's USB disk, which fails at its own pace, is counted and shown every day and raised only when it starts or gets markedly worse. `tvgw verify` gains `gateway_health`, a WARN at most and never promoted, over the last 24 hours; the brief gains a `Gateway health` line with the numbers and the red line `gateway hardware:` in the doctor's tier, its one plain line; the analyst's pack carries it as a health line that buys no request. Temperature, memory and the card's writes are shown and are never findings: no threshold for them was measured on this hardware, and a number the owner learns to ignore is worse than none.
- **The QUIC refusal, watched** (research pass 5, external note B6). UDP 443 stays refused, so apps fall back to TCP where the server name shows; an app that never falls back would fail silently one evening, and Media over QUIC, piloted in 2026, may not fall back. The note proposed a sampled log rule before the reject; none is needed, because the capture sees the TV's QUIC Initial before the firewall refuses it and the extractor already stores its hello (`tls.transport` `quic`). `queries.quic_fallback()` judges each attempt (one address and source port) by a TCP connection to TCP 443 at the same address, or a TCP hello naming the same registrable domain, within `FALLBACK_WINDOW`, 10 seconds, and names the domains with `NEVER_MIN`, 3, attempts and none followed. The brief carries one engineering line; `tvgw verify`'s `quic_fallback` warns over the last week, the newest 2 hours left out, saying how many names and never which, since its detail reaches the public hardware log. Nothing changes in the firewall. Old-style Google QUIC, which the extractor does not read, stays outside the watch (owed test T5).
- **An encrypted copy leaves the box, and a drill proves a copy restores.** The daily copies sit on the card that holds the store; October's research adopted an encrypted pull to the owner's computer, and none existed (`make backup` pulled plain copies by rsync). `tvgw backup --export` hands the newest checked copy to `age`, encrypted to `BACKUP_RECIPIENT`, the owner's age recipient (checked by the export alone, never by `config.load`, so a mistyped optional key stops nothing else), on standard output; `make backup` runs it over ssh and names the file only once the command succeeded, so only ciphertext crosses and lands, and the key that opens it never touches the gateway. `age` because it is one small program in Debian 12 and 13 and in Homebrew, encrypts to a public key, so the gateway holds no secret and needs no passphrase, and authenticates what it encrypts, which `openssl enc` does not; no new Python dependency. The old plain pull stays as `make backup-plain`. `tvgw restore --check FILE [--identity KEY]` restores a copy, plain or encrypted, into a scratch directory: `PRAGMA integrity_check`, the version rule above, an open as this tvgw's writer, which migrates it, the check again, and its rows counted; the scratch is removed whatever happens, and the file is only read. It reads no settings, so it runs on the owner's computer, where the key is; the owner step is on [maintenance](use/maintenance.md#keep-a-copy-on-another-computer). The drill is the owner's to run and record; nothing on the gateway can see it.
- **Ready for the next kernel and the next AdGuard.** Linux 6.17 made legacy iptables a kernel build option, which a distribution's kernel may leave out (external note C); tvgw is nftables alone, but a VPN client's kill switch on the same box may not be. `tvgw verify` gains `iptables_legacy`, a WARN at most: a legacy table in `/proc/net/ip_tables_names` or its two siblings, or an `iptables` that is the legacy variant, says to find its owner before a kernel upgrade. AdGuard Home's 1.0 betas are tracked against the pin, never installed: the AdGuard page lists the three things to check before a stable 1.0 is pinned, the schema, the TCP prefix fix's effect on the in-path filler's `unparsed_tcp`, and the DDR fix's on the owed test T8.
- **The gateway's own lookups while the tunnel is down, held for a bounded time, behind `VPN_DNS_SEAL`.** Measured on the reference gateway by the coordinator on 2026-11-08: the red line `the Pi's own lookups left outside the VPN: DNS left by the uplink 32 time(s)` came from a planned reboot at 2026-11-07 19:40, when the VPN client stopped during the shutdown and the gateway's own port-53 lookups left by the uplink until it halted, and possibly at boot before the tunnel came up; every reboot repeats it. With `VPN_DNS_SEAL=on` (and `VPN_IF` not `none`) a table of its own, `inet tvgw_seal`, which neither `tvgw-apply` nor `tvgw.service`'s stop removes, refuses the gateway's own new connections to port 53 leaving by `WAN_IF` while its set holds port 53, and each element carries a timeout of `HOLD_SECONDS`, 300. The dispatcher puts the port in on `WAN_IF`'s `up`, before the tunnel can be up, and takes it out on the tunnel's `up`; `tvgw-vpnseal.service`, ordered after `piavpn.service` and part of it, puts it in when it stops, which systemd does before it stops the client: at shutdown and at every stop or restart of the client. Its start loads the table and takes the port out when the tunnel is up, so a restart never leaves a hold behind a working tunnel. The refusal is a reject, so the C library's resolver, on a connected socket, moves on at once instead of waiting.
  - **What it never holds, by construction.** The TV's lookups: they reach AdGuard Home on the gateway, which asks its upstreams by DoH on port 443, and the table names no port but 53, so a VPN that never comes up costs the TV nothing, and with it AdGuard's cache, the list updates and the TV's store downloads. The coordinator's brief offered refusing the gateway's DoH on the uplink as well; that would take the TV's lookups away while the tunnel is down, from an empty cache after a boot, which the hard rule of this change forbids, so the DoH leak stays counted and not refused. Two users are never held: nginx's (`www-data`), which resolves the vendor's file-service names for the URL filter, so the TV's app store keeps working through an outage; and `systemd-timesync`, so the gateway's clock, on which every certificate check of the TV's DoH depends, never waits on a VPN. Each is a numeric uid in the rule, looked up when it is written, since nft refuses a whole table over a user name it cannot resolve.
  - **It fails open.** A hold ends by the kernel's own clock: a tunnel not back within 5 minutes, or a VPN client that never starts, gets today's behaviour, the gateway's lookups leaving by the uplink and counted. Nothing waits on tvgw for it. A VPN client that needs a plain lookup to connect waits up to 5 minutes for it after a boot or its own restart.
  - **What leaks with it on.** While the tunnel is down: the TV's lookups as DoH by address, encrypted, so the network sees connections to the DoH resolver and not the names (the DoH leak counter); nginx's lookups of the vendor's file-service names and the clock's of its time servers, in plain DNS; and every other plain lookup of the gateway's once a hold has ended. Only `piavpn.service` is ordered; another client's stop is not held, and its tunnel's going is seen only at the uplink's next start. Plain lookups that a VPN client sends to a resolver on the home network while its tunnel is up leave by the uplink at any time; while held they are refused, which is why the dispatcher releases when the tunnel comes up.
  - **Why behind a setting.** It refuses traffic of the gateway's own that worked before, and whether a few minutes of refused lookups at every boot and every VPN restart are worth the leak they close is the owner's call. `off`, the default, is the gateway as it was.

**Consequences.**

- After a rollback over a newer store, every writer stops with the same line until the newer tvgw is back or a copy from before it is put in place; the brief and the other readers go on reading.
- One more fsync a commit, the directory's, on the card, against the commit an up-to-date open no longer makes. Whether a commit is durable now rests on the card honouring its flushes.
- The sampler runs two more read-only commands every 10 minutes, `vcgencmd get_throttled` where it exists (only `throttled=0x...` is read, never an error's text) and `journalctl _TRANSPORT=kernel` over the minutes since the last sample, and reads a handful of files in `/proc` and `/sys`; nine columns more per row, about 144 rows a day under `DB_DAYS`. A disk that logs thousands of errors makes the journal read longer, bounded by its 10-second timeout, past which the counts are NULL.
- `make backup` now needs `BACKUP_RECIPIENT` and `age` on the gateway, and pulls one copy, the newest, where it pulled every copy; an owner who wants the old behaviour runs `make backup-plain`. A pulled copy opens only with the owner's key: a lost key is a lost copy.
- The QUIC watch is only as good as the capture: an hour the capture missed has no attempts, and a fallback whose TCP connection has neither a stored hello nor a stored flow in the window reads as none. A name with fewer than three attempts is never said to fail.
- With `VPN_DNS_SEAL=on`: up to 5 minutes, at each boot and each stop or restart of the VPN client, in which the gateway's own plain lookups by the uplink fail (apt, the update check, the DoH list's download, the CI runner), nginx and the clock excepted; the brief's leak red line should stop firing at reboots. One more unit, `tvgw-vpnseal.service`, which with the setting off removes a table that is not there. Its namespace test (`tests/virtual/test_virtual_seal.py`) proves the refusal, the TV's lookups and web traffic passing, the tunnel's path passing, and the three ways a hold ends; that the stop runs before PIA's at a real shutdown only the gateway can show.
- The power-cut test runs only in CI's namespace tier, as root, with `dmsetup`, `e2fsprogs` and the `dm_flakey` module, which the workflow installs and loads; its numbers are a virtual disk's.

**Owner decisions, held behind settings.**

| Decision | Setting | Default |
|---|---|---|
| Whether the gateway's own plain lookups are held off the uplink, for up to 5 minutes each time, while the VPN's tunnel is down | `VPN_DNS_SEAL` | `off`: the lookups leave by the uplink and are counted, as before |
| The age key a copy that leaves the gateway is encrypted to; the owner keeps the key that opens it | `BACKUP_RECIPIENT` | empty: no export; `make backup-plain` pulls plain copies as before |
