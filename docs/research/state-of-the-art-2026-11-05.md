# Turn tvgw's Counters Into Careful Walls

*Research record, written 2026-11-05 against release 0.3.2: the fourth research pass, on protection, visibility, reliability and invention. It is a proposal, and nothing in it is implemented by being here; what the code took from it is in [the changelog](../../CHANGELOG.md) under 2026-11-05. Its links to repository pages are rewritten for this folder, and two links to notes outside the repository are replaced by the measurement they pointed at.*

tvgw 0.3.2 already does nearly everything the 2025-2026 research and products recommend for a gateway that cannot decrypt, so its largest gaps are not missing tools: they are three things it already measures and does not yet act on. It counts connections to addresses its filter never handed out and refuses none of them; it reports what the TV sends while switched off and lets all of it through; and its 35 checks run every ten minutes, yet nothing undoes a bad change without a person. The field moved toward tvgw's design this year: Microsoft shipped "connect only to addresses the approved lookup server returned" in Windows in November 2025, Firewalla released an experimental learn-then-limit mode for home devices, and a 12-TV peer-reviewed study found that LG's tracking opt-out holds while Samsung's, Vizio's and Roku's do not fully hold. Three candidate inventions appear new and are cheap to try: closing the TV's internet path while the TV is off, a byte ceiling on uploads to destinations nobody has classified, and a firewall change that reverts itself unless a test client standing where the TV stands confirms the TV would still work. The idea with the highest ceiling, limiting the TV to what the app in front needs, also has the most ways to spoil an evening and should stay a report for now. Because every new block must start count-only (it counts what it would refuse and refuses nothing) and each count needs about two weeks, the binding constraint on protection is calendar time: this pass should start those clocks first and build the automatic-undo machinery that enforcement will need in the next two passes. The evidence is thin in known places: one TV, one firmware version and five days of counts from that address rule; no published measurement of webOS 5; and the Texas court papers were not read in the original. Throughout, an idea that tvgw's earlier passes (the October survey, pass 2 of 1 November and pass 3 of 4 November) already adopted, rejected or deferred is labelled so, and "new" means new to tvgw's own record.

## Protection: the field caught up with tvgw's design, and the open work is enforcing what it counts

### What TVs do in 2026, and what a name filter cannot reach

Two peer-reviewed studies now bracket how TVs track their owners. The mechanism is automatic content recognition (ACR): the TV samples the picture or the sound, reduces the sample to a fingerprint, and uploads it to learn what is on screen. A 2024 study of LG and Samsung sets found ACR running on the antenna and HDMI inputs, and found that switching off the vendor's settings stopped all traffic to the ACR hosts ([IMC 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06203v1)). A 2026 Northeastern study, *Watching the Watchers*, repeated the work on **12 TVs across the eight operating systems that hold about 95% of the US market, in 5,943 runs**. It confirmed that the opt-out works on LG webOS, Amazon Fire OS, Sony's Google TV and VIDAA, and it found traffic consistent with ACR continuing after opt-out on Samsung (41 of 60 antenna runs, 18 of 30 HDMI runs), on Vizio (every run) and on Roku's own channel (every run) ([Watching the Watchers](https://watchingthewatchers.github.io/watchingthewatchers/paper.pdf)). LG's ACR listens rather than watches (its traffic fell 97.3% when the sound was removed), uses its own ports and not the web's port 443, repeats every 14 seconds, and used the same host names two years apart. Five of the eight systems fingerprint HDMI, so an external streaming box does not help on a connected TV that has opted in. For the CX this is good news with a caveat. With the viewing agreement declined, Live Plus (LG's name for the ACR switch) off and LG's ACR operator `alphonso.tv` blocked, the measured ACR exposure is zero; but nobody has published a measurement of webOS 5 or any 2020 LG set, and the authors say that without reading the encrypted contents they "cannot know for certain".

The same study shows four kinds of tracking that survive a name filter, each with a 2026 example. The first is tracking on a host the feature needs: free ad-supported channels (FAST, such as LG Channels) are delivered from Amagi host names that contain the channel or programme title, the authors left those names off their blocklist "because ... blocking them breaks playback", and no setting turns the leak off. The second is tracking on shared cloud addresses that cannot be singled out by name: Vizio's suspected ACR endpoint is an Amazon Kinesis host. The third is tracking reached without asking the home's filter: Sony's ACR partner is reached at a fixed address, and a lab LG set sent 67,810 name lookups (DNS, the service that turns a name into a server's address) straight to Google's public lookup server, called a resolver, although it was told to use another ([Watching the Watchers](https://watchingthewatchers.github.io/watchingthewatchers/paper.pdf); [deep dive 05](deep-dive-2026-11/05-tv-privacy-research-2026.md)). The fourth never crosses the home network at all: ads stitched into the stream by the server, each app's own logging, and matching by the household's public address, which no TV setting and no filter removes. A separate 2026 study found **14 of 35 home devices, an LG TV among them, ignoring the resolver they were given** ([Losty et al.](https://miishra.github.io//papers/IEEE_IoT_DNS_Submission_Final.pdf)); tvgw already closes that path by redirecting every lookup to its own filter, and its own count found no bypass and no fixed address on the CX in five days (measured on the reference gateway on 2026-11-02, from its own store; the gate that counted them is design decisions [18](../design-decisions.md#18-dns-gated-egress-starts-as-a-count-in-its-own-table) and [43](../design-decisions.md#43-the-flow-log-hears-the-tv-alone-and-the-gates-day-starts-at-each-answer)). The lesson for the gap analysis is that every bypass with real 2026 evidence is caught by a control tvgw already has or has staged, not by more list entries: an address or port rule, the upload test, or the address gate (tvgw's name for the rule that counts connections to addresses its filter never handed out). No source documents LG moving tracking onto its must-allow hosts (the names the TV needs for its clock, login, store and firmware, which tvgw never blocks); that is a searched-for absence at the level of names, not proof about what rides inside those required calls.

The law is changing what arrives by firmware more than what flows on the wire. Texas sued five TV makers on 15 December 2025 and announced settlements with Samsung (26 February 2026) and LG (11 May 2026); LG agreed to stop collecting viewing data without informed consent and to show a pop-up disclosure on its TVs ([Texas Attorney General](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-secures-major-agreement-lg-protect-texans-privacy-and-stop-data-being); [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-texas-attorney-general-lawsuits-smart-tv-content-tracking/)). For a household that has already declined, that pop-up is a risk and not a protection: a new screen, delivered by an update, on which one press grants what was refused. tvgw's hourly reading of the TV's own consent record is the right tripwire, and nothing in the evidence suggests any function this household needs depends on accepting an agreement; LG's own statement says core functions do not ([LG statement, via ecoustics](https://www.ecoustics.com/news/lg-smart-tv-privacy-acr/)). The petitions and judgments themselves could not be fetched, so their scope and whether the pop-up has shipped are unverified.

### Where the ways round a filter stand in late 2026

The documented way round a home filter is still the old one, a fixed public resolver over plain DNS, which the redirect closes. No primary source from 2025-2026 shows webOS, Tizen, Google TV, Fire OS or Roku OS using encrypted lookups for its own traffic. (DoH, DoT and DoQ are lookups carried inside HTTPS, inside TLS, the encryption under HTTPS, or inside QUIC, where a filter cannot read them.) Lists could not keep up if one did. HaGeZi's list of encrypted-DNS servers holds 3,585 names and its address list about 1,430 addresses ([HaGeZi](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hagezi/dns-blocklists/main/README.md); [deep dive 04](deep-dive-2026-11/04-blocklists-and-verification.md)), while one scan of the internet found about **46,000 DoH services** ([E-DoH](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.12363)). The list is a speed bump. The general answer is the rule tvgw already counts: let the TV connect only to addresses that its own filter handed out.

Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) hides the server name that a device otherwise sends in the clear when it opens an encrypted connection; that clear name, the SNI, is what tvgw reads to name a connection. On the server side ECH is still almost entirely one company: of 1.1 million deployments measured in 2025, Cloudflare accounted for 99.99% ([IFIP Networking 2026](https://dl.ifip.org/db/conf/networking/networking2026/1571261276.pdf)), and Vercel joined in August 2026 under the shared outer name `vercel-ech.com` ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/changelog/encrypted-client-hello-now-supported-on-vercel-cdn)). The new fact is on the device side: Android 17 turns ECH on by default for apps built for it, fetches the key from a DNS record type called HTTPS, and when it finds no key sends a harmless dummy (called GREASE) with the real name still visible ([Android developers](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-config)). tvgw already withholds those records from the TV and already tells the dummy from the real thing (design decisions [36](../design-decisions.md#36-a-known-ech-public-name-is-real-ech) and [46](../design-decisions.md#46-the-blocklists-are-watched-and-adguard-changes-only-on-purpose)), so the defence holds for an Android-based TV; as pass 2 said, it does nothing against a key shipped inside an app. The CX's web engine predates ECH altogether. The one concrete update is small: the list of provider names that marks real ECH holds, as the docs record it, only Cloudflare's, and Vercel's belongs in it.

QUIC is the newer transport that carries HTTP/3 and encrypts more of its opening; it runs over UDP, the simpler of the internet's two ways of sending packets (TCP is the other). Refusing it on UDP port 443, so that apps fall back to ordinary TLS where the name is visible, is still the documented practice at firewalls that want to see names ([Cisco](https://docs.defenseorchestrator.com/cdfmc/t_how-to-block-quic-traffic.html)), and no report of a TV app that refuses to fall back was found. The alternative, reading the name out of QUIC's opening packets, has become unreliable: current Chrome spreads the opening across several packets and scrambles it, and a 2025 study found that China's national firewall does not reassemble split openings and ignores QUIC version 2 ([USENIX Security 2025](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/zohaib)). tvgw should keep the blanket refusal. Its own evidence-only reader of QUIC openings will under-count names for the same reason, so a nameless opening should be recorded as "not reassembled", not as "no name".

Every working design for "only addresses the filter handed out" updates the firewall's list in the path of the DNS answer, before the device can act on it. dnsmasq (the small server tvgw already runs to hand the TV its network address) can add the address before it replies; Cilium, the reference design in data centres, holds the answer for up to 100 milliseconds until the address is admitted and keeps an address for as long as a connection uses it; a 2024 academic firewall records addresses "before forwarding" the reply ([dnsmasq list](https://lists.thekelleys.org.uk/pipermail/dnsmasq-discuss/2022q1/016107.html); [Cilium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cilium/cilium/v1.17/Documentation/cmdref/cilium-agent.md); [He et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10542255)). Microsoft made the same rule generally available in Windows 11 Enterprise and Education on 18 November 2025 ([Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/networkingblog/-/4468743)), and a small vendor has sold it on home-class routers since about 2020 with a published list of what breaks: connections made by fixed address, calling and peer-to-peer apps, and apps that keep using an answer after it expires ([DNS-OARC 45](https://indico.dns-oarc.net/event/55/contributions/1179/)). None of these designs enforces the answer's stated lifetime (its TTL); OPNsense, an open-source firewall, ignores it and prunes unused addresses after a day ([OPNsense](https://docs.opnsense.org/manual/dnsmasq.html)). The dynamic rule is cheap where static lists are not: lists built from a device's own earlier traffic cost 10 of 24 products some function within a week when written by host name, and 6 of 24 by domain ([He et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10542255)). tvgw's own five days fit the pattern exactly: **322 connections fell outside the list, 320 of them within two seconds of the answer that named the address**, because the counter adds an address a moment after the answer has left, and the 2 genuine ones were an address used two minutes after a reboot emptied the list and one used seven hours after its last answer (measured on the reference gateway on 2026-11-02, from its own store, over the gate's count since 2026-10-28; the gate and the feeder's race are design decisions [18](../design-decisions.md#18-dns-gated-egress-starts-as-a-count-in-its-own-table) and [43](../design-decisions.md#43-the-flow-log-hears-the-tv-alone-and-the-gates-day-starts-at-each-answer)).

This pass adds four traps for the dnsmasq build that the October survey did not record. An answer served from dnsmasq's own cache never touches the list, so its cache must be off. dnsmasq sends no lifetime with the address, and on the Pi's kernel a repeat add without one does not extend an entry, so the existing counter must keep running as the refresher. The dnsmasq in Debian 13 (2.91) predates a 2.92 fix for answers that arrive over TCP. And 2.93 fixes a memory-overwrite bug triggered by long names (catalogued as CVE-2026-2291), which matters more once the TV's lookups pass through dnsmasq, where today dnsmasq only hands the TV its address ([dnsmasq changelog](https://thekelleys.org.uk/dnsmasq/CHANGELOG); [dnsmasq list](https://lists.thekelleys.org.uk/pipermail/dnsmasq-discuss/2022q1/016107.html)). There is a second build that leaves AdGuard Home (tvgw's DNS filter) where it is: hold each answer on its way from the Pi to the TV, add its addresses, then release it, which is what the 2024 academic firewall did. It is new code in the packet path, and what happens when it dies must be chosen. My recommendation is an inference from the baseline and not a tested result: prototype both in CI (the automated tests that run on every change) and prefer the second on the gateway, because tvgw's record-withholding rule, its lookup log and `tvgw check` all recognise the TV by the address AdGuard sees, and the dnsmasq build would make AdGuard see dnsmasq instead. Whichever is built, the prior art gives the same rules: keep the day-long lifetime refreshed at each answer and reject exact-TTL enforcement; keep the list across reboots; never re-check a connection that is already open; never let the "blocked" answer (`0.0.0.0`) into the list; and refuse with a reset, not a silent drop, so an app retries at once.

Among products, Firewalla's Device Active Protect (experimental since November 2025) learns what a device uses, blocks nothing while it learns, then limits the device, with a "restart learning" control ([Residential Systems](https://www.residentialsystems.com/technology/connectivity/firewalla-launches-app-1-66-with-new-parental-controls)); this is tvgw's count-then-enforce plan with a reset button. MUD, the standard by which a maker would publish what its device needs, still has no TV vendor behind it. Pi-hole, another DNS filter, now answers a handful of special names by default so that software stops looking for a way round the filter ([Pi-hole](https://docs.pi-hole.net/ftldns/configfile/)). AdGuard Home added no firewall-list feature in 2026; it released v0.107.80 on 3 November with a security update, one release past tvgw's pin, and its released versions carry a bug that can malform the answer to a resolver-discovery lookup, fixed only in unreleased code ([AdGuard Home changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/AdguardTeam/AdGuardHome/refs/heads/master/CHANGELOG.md)).

## Visibility: names beat models, and the weak spots are unnamed flows and unreported change

### What the tools and the research add in 2026

Nothing released in 2025-2026 changes the October verdict that a full monitoring stack is overkill for one TV. Zeek, the standard open-source traffic analyser, shipped version 9.0 and its fingerprint plugin became compiled code ([Zeek releases](https://github.com/zeek/zeek/releases)); Malcolm, a packaged monitoring suite, still asks for "8 CPU cores and 16 gigabytes of RAM"; Raspberry Pi kernels still ship without the type information that modern kernel tracing needs ([Malcolm](https://cisagov.github.io/Malcolm/docs/system-requirements.html); [raspberrypi/linux #6622](https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/issues/6622)). Two changes are worth recording. nDPI, a library that recognises protocols, moved its TLS, QUIC and DNS readers in version 6.0 under terms that make a project earning revenue from them buy a licence or do without them, which matters for an MIT-licensed tool others may package ([ntop](https://www.ntop.org/announcing-ndpi-dual-license-change/)). And ntopng, a traffic dashboard, now sells an AI analyst over traffic records in version 7.0, behind an enterprise licence ([ntopng 7.0](https://github.com/ntop/ntopng/releases/tag/7.0)): the pattern tvgw built for itself has become a product feature. Zeek stays where October put it, as an offline cross-check of tvgw's own extractor.

The more useful 2026 result is a negative one. Large "foundation models" trained to classify encrypted traffic were found to owe their headline accuracy to leaked identifiers. In a 2025 peer-reviewed test with the models' internals frozen and whole connections kept on one side of the train-test split, five such models scored between **1.9% and 29.7% on a 120-class task where a model reading only packet headers scored 71%**, and the authors concluded that "the encrypted payload cannot make any significant contribution" ([SIGCOMM 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16438v1)). For a gateway that reads the server name directly, the name already is the label these models try to guess. The practical tool for the unnamed remainder is the one pass 2 specified and nobody built: name a connection from the DNS answer that produced its address, and record that the name came from DNS. One of pass 2's own ideas was independently confirmed. JA4 is a fingerprint of how a program opens an encrypted connection, which tvgw stores to notice when the TV's software changes. In January 2026 ntop showed one browser producing two JA4 values across six requests and shipped an option that ignores the unstable fields ([ntop](https://www.ntop.org/is-ja4-now-obsolete/)), which is the "stack key" pass 2 proposed. It is no longer a tvgw invention, and it is still not built.

Three limits on what metadata can show deserve plain statement in the brief and the docs. No peer-reviewed method detects ad breaks or ad beacons from encrypted timing and sizes, and where the server stitches ads into the stream the measurement calls may be made by that server and never by the TV, so the brief must never say "no ad tracking seen". No label or law tells an owner what a TV sends: the US Cyber Trust Mark had no labelled product on 28 October 2026 ([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/10/fcc-commissioner-hopes-cyber-trust-mark-products-reach-shoppers-within-year/416878/)), and the EU Cyber Resilience Act's required product information covers support dates and secure use, not data sent or destinations ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)); the gateway's record is the only source. And the rhythm of a video identifies it: published attacks name a title among 20,000 from 12 seconds of traffic, and through an encrypted proxy with up to 90% accuracy from three minutes ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/535186); [SCEP-TI](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1389128625005973)). One inference in this pass's notes does not survive a check against the baseline and is corrected here. The notes reasoned that tvgw's packet capture is itself a title record and that the TV's traffic rides a VPN. The baseline says the capture keeps only the packets the TV sends to open a connection and never what it receives, and that the TV uses the direct uplink ([privacy and retention](../how-it-works/privacy-and-retention.md); [design decision 1](../design-decisions.md#1-the-tv-uses-the-direct-uplink-not-the-vpn)). So the gateway's capture does not hold the segment sizes these attacks need; the internet provider on the uplink does, and no gateway setting changes that. The docs should say so in one sentence.

On detecting change, the evidence favours tvgw's ledger over learned "normal". A 2026 study of 21 home devices watched a trained device model decay within months (one speaker's score fell from 0.938 to 0.446) and found that its drift detector flagged stable periods about half the time ([UNSW and KDDI](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.15465v1)). For one TV with a readable firmware version, the honest design is to re-baseline at known change points and report new names and new software fingerprints as facts, not anomaly scores. On periodic traffic, no better detector appeared; every source agrees that the false alarms come from harmless periodic background traffic and that the cure is a list of known periodic sources, not a better statistic. In one 2026 study all 29 false alarms came from five harmless addresses ([Springer, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10207-026-01335-w)).

On explaining, two findings shape the brief. A 2026 NYU study that showed people their own devices' traffic found that willingness to block followed their satisfaction and their concern about collection, not raw volume ([NYU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19873)), which supports naming the company behind a host. The tracker databases built from web crawls do not cover TV hosts: a check on 5 November found none of LG's or Samsung's ACR and platform domains in DuckDuckGo's Tracker Radar, while AdGuard's companies database has them ([Tracker Radar](https://github.com/duckduckgo/tracker-radar); [companiesdb](https://github.com/AdguardTeam/companiesdb)). And published AI analysts of network logs are mostly scored by how similar their text is to a reference, not by whether their facts are right ([Worae and Mastorakis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13925)); the exact, free guard is to check every value the analyst cites against the data it was given, which pass 2 proposed and nobody built.

The record's own privacy has a cheap half and a hard half. The norm is to keep less, and to encrypt and pseudonymise what is kept ([RFC 8932](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8932.html)). The Pi 5 encrypts at about 1.3 to 1.6 GB per second ([OpenBenchmarking](https://mail.openbenchmarking.org/result/2505191-NE-ENCRYPTIO54&export=txt)), so the cost is nil at tvgw's write rates. The hard half is the key: a headless box that must reboot unattended needs the key somewhere readable, so encrypting the card protects only a card pulled while powered off, and pass 2 already put theft outside the threat model. The piece worth doing is the one October adopted and that exists today only as a manual, unencrypted copy: an encrypted backup pulled to the owner's own computer.

A home gateway's record could also serve as evidence, and tvgw holds most of what that needs. Reasoning from the Texas cases, the settlements and the 2026 study's method ([Watching the Watchers](https://watchingthewatchers.github.io/watchingthewatchers/paper.pdf)), a useful record pairs the TV's consent state at the time of each flow with flow records that let someone else recompute the upload ratio and rhythm (time, destination, server port, bytes each way), keeps attempts as well as successes, and records firmware changes and what was on screen. tvgw's hourly consent readings beside its flow table are a pairing no research dataset and no vendor log gives an owner. What it lacks is proof that the record was not assembled later, and a finer reading of what was on screen. Its limits must be stated first: a gateway that never opts in has no before-and-after to show, and a blocked lookup shows an attempt, not collection.

## Reliability: every recorded incident was self-inflicted, and nothing yet undoes a bad change

### What the state of the art offers one box

The three incidents on tvgw's record were all changes the gateway made to itself that were right in testing and wrong on the box: a per-interface forwarding switch, routing entries rewritten at every renewal, and a tightened directory permission ([incidents](../incidents.md)). The state of the art answers that class with one primitive, apply and then revert unless confirmed. Junos, Juniper's router software, has had it for decades with a 10-minute default; OPNsense's firewall API reverts "after 60 seconds to the original state"; Ubuntu's `netplan try` waits 120 seconds; Debian's `iptables-apply` waits 10 ([Juniper](https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/junos-xml-protocol/topics/task/junos-xml-protocol-configuration-committing-with-confirmation.html); [OPNsense](https://docs.opnsense.org/development/api/plugins/firewall.html); [netplan](https://netplan.readthedocs.io/en/stable/netplan-try/); [iptables-apply](https://manpages.debian.org/trixie/iptables/iptables-apply.8.en.html)). Almost all of them judge health the same way: the operator's own session survives long enough to confirm. That signal does not exist here. The protected device cannot confirm anything, and tvgw deliberately deploys in its quiet window, between 03:30 and 05:30 while the TV is off, which means that nothing real judges a deploy until the evening, when the household becomes the test. No published practice was found that uses an automated test client at the protected device's position as the confirmer. tvgw would be composing it from parts it already has.

The design is short. Before a change, save the live firewall rules, routes and kernel switches as "last good" and start a timer that restores them; apply; then run a confirmer; cancel the timer only if it passes. The restore must replace the whole saved state at once and must never use the stop path, which leaves the TV dark. The confirmer has to stand where the TV stands. A test run from the gateway itself skips the forwarding path, the address translation and the routing rule where two of the three incidents lived, so the test client belongs in a network namespace (a private copy of the network stack inside Linux, the same construction tvgw's CI uses) attached to the TV's port. It should confirm both halves of the promise: a must-allow name resolves and an encrypted connection to it completes; a must-block name is refused and an encrypted-lookup attempt is rejected. Three facts in the baseline will fight such a client, which is my inference and not something the notes tested: the firewall drops any source on that port other than the TV's pinned address; a check warns when a second hardware address appears there; and a probe that borrowed the TV's own address to get through would land in the viewing record and in the baselines of tvgw's hourly judge (the check that removes a machine-made block when viewing drops). The probe therefore needs its own address behind a narrow, named exception, and its traffic must stay out of the record.

For the software itself, the Raspberry Pi 5 now has a real two-slot update primitive (boot a second copy of the system once, and fall back to the first on any reset that was not confirmed), but no mainstream updater supports it out of the box; the official support in RAUC, the common updater, is still an unmerged proposal after almost two years ([Raspberry Pi docs](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/config_txt.html); [RAUC #1599](https://github.com/rauc/rauc/pull/1599)). Home Assistant's own operating system proves the scheme at fleet scale; for one box it is weeks of work and a different install model. The cheapest ladder that still gives rollback runs in this order: keep the previous package and a snapshot of the settings so one command reinstalls them, and refuse to start on a store newer than the code understands; confirm every firewall and DNS apply as above; keep an untouched SD card as the fallback once the system boots from a USB drive. The first and third steps are revivals: `tvgw upgrade` is in decision 53 as not built, the baseline notes that "nothing reads the stored schema version", and the USB-boot move was first on pass 2's "next five".

On hardware, the best 2026 data is a three-year endurance survey in which **177 of 351 microSD cards failed** under continuous writing ([Hardware Busters](https://hwbusters.com/news/a-three-year-microsd-endurance-test-has-written-133-petabytes-and-killed-177-cards/)), but the field consensus is that unclean power loss and cheap cards, not wear, kill small computers, and at tvgw's write rate wear is unlikely to be what fails. Two small points follow. The documentation of SQLite, the database file tvgw keeps its record in, says that in tvgw's mode a transaction committed just before a power cut "might get rolled back", and that one stricter setting (`synchronous=EXTRA`) closes that window at the price of one extra disk sync per commit ([SQLite](https://www.sqlite.org/pragma.html)). And a Pi 5 without its clock battery restarts at the time of its last successful sync, late by the length of the outage, which feeds wrong time into the deploy window, the hourly judge, retention and the brief's day boundary until the network clock answers. The owner declined that battery on 28 October 2026; the new argument does not change who decides.

On staying up, failing open is out for a privacy gateway, and a second gateway sharing an address is not worth it for one TV: a documented home build drops every connection twice per failure and doubles the configuration to keep in step ([jwhitham.org](https://www.jwhitham.org/2024/04/vrrp-router-keepalived.html)). The valuable layer is DNS. Answering from an expired entry while the upstream is down is a standard since 2020 ([RFC 8767](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767.txt)) and AdGuard's version is already configured, but every AdGuard restart empties that cache. Two existing owner decisions therefore carry reliability weight: a second upstream operator, which the owner left empty because a second operator would see lookups, and applying machine rules through AdGuard's API so that no restart is needed.

The largest outages of the period were pushed content, not code. A CrowdStrike content file with 21 fields met code that supplied 20 in July 2024; a Cloudflare configuration file doubled past a fixed 200-entry limit in November 2025 and, because it was regenerated every five minutes, the outage flapped ([CrowdStrike](https://crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf); [Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/18-november-2025-outage/)). Both concluded that pushed content must be validated like user input, staged, and given a way back. tvgw's blocklists are exactly such content, and they still go straight into AdGuard: pass 2 showed that an empty file replaces a list, and a list push on 11 September 2026 cost newer LG sets their clock and store for about a day ([deep dive 04](deep-dive-2026-11/04-blocklists-and-verification.md)). The cheap safeguards are bounds on how much a list may shrink or grow, a replay of the names this TV actually looked up against the new list so that each update becomes a short reviewed difference, and a kept last-good copy.

On testing, the tools that transfer to one small box are specific. Network faults (loss, delay, a dead resolver) can be injected into the namespace copy of the gateway that CI already builds. LazyFS, a test file system that discards unsynced writes on command and has been used to find lost-write bugs in widely used databases, proves whether tvgw's own writes survive a power cut ([VLDB 2024](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p3017-ramos.pdf)). Atheris feeds random input to Python code and supports the Pi's Python ([Atheris](https://pypi.org/pypi/atheris/3.1.0)); the hand-written reader of connection openings is the part of tvgw that the TV's own packets reach. No off-the-shelf tool verifies rules for nftables, the Linux firewall tvgw uses. A formal model reportedly found two bugs in the nftables rule compiler and optimiser, which is a reason not to use the optimiser flag in production and a reason to keep testing the loaded rules by sending packets, as tvgw's matrix does ([secondary report](https://letsdatascience.com/news/basis-uses-llms-to-verify-nftables-compiler-b8680ab1)). Whole-system deterministic simulation does not pay for a system whose state lives mostly in the kernel and AdGuard.

On operating without alerts, systemd (the Linux service manager) already supplies bounded self-repair: restarts with growing delays, a unit that runs when another fails, and a reboot as a capped last step ([systemd](https://www.freedesktop.org/software/systemd/man/latest/systemd.service.html)). The missing piece is the signal. A test from the TV's position every ten minutes is the one number that means "the TV would work right now", including when the TV is off; it is the same probe as the confirmer. Its reach should not be overstated, and here I part from the notes, which say all three incidents would have shown in it. It would have shown the 26 October outage (the TV had no route) within one cycle. It would show the 30 October fault (open connections cut at every renewal) only if it held a connection open across a re-apply. It would not have shown the 27 October fault at all, because that one lost the recording and not the path. A household target built on the probe is useful mainly as a brake: when the month's failure allowance is spent, behaviour-changing deploys wait.

## Invention: no other system keys its rules to the TV's own state

No single system matches tvgw's combination. IoTrim, a 2021 research system that finds which destinations a device can lose without breaking, is closest in purpose ([IoTrim](https://www.petsymposium.org/popets/2021/popets-2021-0075.pdf)); Firewalla is closest as a product; adam:ONE (the home-router product mentioned above) and Microsoft's Windows feature are closest to the address gate; IoT Inspector, a university tool that shows what home devices contact, is closest on visibility. What none of them has is the device's own state (power, the app in front, the consent record) read over its local interface and joined with its traffic; blocks on probation that undo themselves on evidence from the device's use; and a plain daily account with no cloud. The candidates that build on that ground are the ones where tvgw can be first. The table lists every candidate that appears new in some form. "Appears new" means searches found nothing, which is absence of evidence; no claims-level patent search was done, and none of this is legal advice.

The prior art behind the table is public: Firewalla's learned limits and upload alarms ([Firewalla](https://help.firewalla.com/hc/en-us/articles/360020926913)); firewall byte quotas ([nftables wiki](https://wiki.nftables.org/wiki-nftables/index.php/Quotas)); a device-side patent on network policy for the app in front ([US 10,862,893](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10862893)); research gateways that release traffic by time or by evidence of use ([Bark](https://jhong93.github.io/pdf/bark-iotdi18.pdf); [FIAT](https://yunmingxiao.github.io/publication/fiat)); the EU data protection supervisor's evidence collector for websites ([EDPS](https://www.edps.europa.eu/sites/default/files/edpsweb_press_releases/edps-2019-08_wec_en.pdf)); TrackerControl's requests from observed tracking ([TrackerControl](https://github.com/TrackerControl/tracker-control-android)); request-chain classification on the web ([Khaleesi](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/iqbal)); and fault injection for small devices ([µChaos](https://iccs-meeting.org/archive/iccs2024/papers/148380231.pdf)). One caution belongs with the address gate: the home-router vendor lists its mechanism as patented, and Cisco holds a 2024 patent on resolver-assisted access control ([US 12,143,359](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12143359)); these matter only if tvgw's gate were ever distributed commercially, and they were not examined at the level of claims.

Three of these give the most protection for the least risk, in this order. Power-state silence comes first because it is the only way to refuse the names that cannot be refused while someone is watching, it covers most hours of every day, and its worst failure is small if any doubt opens the gate. One version of it must be ruled out: a smart plug that cuts mains power, because LG tells owners not to unplug an OLED set when turning it off ([LG support](https://www.lg.com/us/support/help-library/lg-oled-tv-how-to-fix-reoccurring-pixel-refresher-notifications--20154629491040)). The address gate comes second. It is not an invention, and on this TV today it would have stopped nothing hostile, so its present value is insurance; but it makes the resolver the single door, which is what gives every name-level rule force at the address level. The upload budget comes third: the gate limits where the TV may send, the budget limits how much, and it needs no name. The measured day shows how much of the TV's traffic goes to names nobody has classified: 28 such names carried 2.7 GB ([what it stops](../what-it-stops.md)).

Four candidates should be dropped or reshaped. An HDMI self-test that compares content recognition on and off needs the viewing agreement accepted, which is out of bounds; only a one-arm check remains, and it proves little. "Starving" a tracker by cutting the connection after it opens lets the identifiers through in the first packet, and a local stall turns a refusal the app handles in milliseconds into a long timeout. Decoy traffic cannot forge the TV's authenticated reports, and published work separates decoys from real queries with ordinary classifiers ([PETS 2010](https://www.freehaven.net/anonbib/papers/pets2010/p2-peddinti.pdf)). A timed "pause all protection" hands every queued identifier to every tracker for the length of the window; the useful core is a report of what was just refused, released one name at a time. Sharing profiles between households stays where pass 2 left it, a hand-sent pack with a public vocabulary, because research shows both that one or two dozen homes with the same product are often needed and that sets of domains identify a household ([He et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10542255); [deep dive 07](deep-dive-2026-11/07-measurement-and-presentation.md)).

## Conclusion

The project's own method has become its bottleneck. Counting before blocking is right, and the research supports it at every turn, but it means protection now advances at the speed of the calendar and of the undo machinery, not of ideas. Protection ranks first, yet the fastest way to raise it is to build the two reliability pieces that every new control depends on: a probe that stands where the TV stands, and a revert that needs no person. Once those exist, each counter that has run clean for two weeks can be promoted on probation instead of on faith.

The other shift is in where tvgw can be special. Other systems have better lists, bigger models or more devices. None of them reads the television's own account of itself (off or on, which app, which agreements) and uses it to decide what the network should allow. Power-state silence is the first use of that; the app-in-front rule is the ambitious one. And the honest boundary deserves a permanent place in the brief: the household's public address, titles in free-channel host names, ads stitched in by the server and each app's own logging are outside any gateway's reach, and saying so plainly is part of the protection.

## What to build first in this pass, in rank order

The order puts the most protection for the least risk first. Anything that could refuse traffic starts count-only in this pass; nothing new enforces until its count is clean and the automatic undo of item 5 exists. "CI" includes the namespace copy of the gateway and its reachability matrix (the table of test connections that must pass or must fail); "the gateway" means the reference Pi in front of the CX. Each item names a before-and-after measure where one exists.

1. **Day-one closures with an existing way back.** Move AdGuard Home to v0.107.80 with `tvgw adguard upgrade` in the quiet window; add `vercel-ech.com` to the real-ECH names; add a counter for UDP 123 by destination; run the owed one-line tests (T8: what the TV receives for a resolver-discovery lookup; T3: does the CX ever ask for the HTTPS record type; T5: is there any old-style Google QUIC); read `dnsmasq --version` and whether Debian's build carries the CVE-2026-2291 fix; compare the 2026 study's ACR blocklist ([released list](https://github.com/IlgarNEU/Watching-the-Watchers/blob/main/Blocklist/Blocklist.txt)) with the five profiles without copying list text. Test: CI runs the real AdGuard binary's answer-form tests against the new version and a sample connection with the Vercel outer name. Verify on the gateway: `adguard_version`, `list_sentinels` and `resolver_must_allow` pass, `tvgw adguard diff` is clean, and the clock, store and one stream work that evening; the kept `.bak` copy is the undo. Nothing new is blocked.

2. **Power-state silence: size it, then count it.** First query the store for bytes, names and connections during off-hours by category; that is the before-measure and the off-hour baseline pass 2 wanted. Then add a rule that only counts and samples what would be dropped while a "TV off" flag is set; the flag clears on any doubt (state unknown, TV unreachable, cable link change), and a first packet after silence triggers one read of the TV's power state. Test: in CI, flag set moves the counter and drops nothing; flag unknown counts nothing; the reachability matrix is unchanged. Verify on the gateway over two weeks: compare the counter with the brief's "While off or asleep", use one 26-hour `tvgw tvprobe` run as ground truth for how often the flag was wrong, and record the delay between first packet and state read at every wake. Starts count-only, and on the LG profile only: on a Samsung, opening the TV's local socket can switch the set on, so the state read needs its own design there. Enforcement is a later pass, on probation, undone automatically on any wake where a clock or must-allow lookup fails. A smart plug must never cut the TV's power.

3. **The in-path filler for the address gate, counting beside the passive one.** Build both variants in CI (dnsmasq in front of AdGuard; hold-the-answer with AdGuard unchanged) and put the better one on the gateway feeding a second count-only list. Test: 10,000 lookups with a client that connects the instant the answer arrives and zero misses; answers over TCP; `0.0.0.0` never enters the list; entries refresh on the Pi's kernel; with the filler killed, answers still flow. Verify on the gateway: for two weeks the in-path list's would-refuse count is a handful a week at most, each explained by a reboot or an app's cache, against the passive list's 320-of-322 race rate as the before-measure; the list survives a reboot. `DNSGATE` stays `count`. Enforcement waits for decision 18's condition and will need exceptions for LG's fixed firmware fallback and the gateway itself.

4. **Upload budget, count-only.** From the flow table take the hourly payload (content bytes, not packet overhead) sent to each destination outside the expected categories, using the brief's existing upper-bound estimate so that acknowledgement packets are not counted as upload; set the ceiling at ten times the largest clean hour; load it as a quota that counts and logs. Test: in CI an upload past the ceiling moves the counter and is not dropped, and a long stream's acknowledgements do not. Verify: two weeks with no hit on any destination the owner recognises as wanted, and a positive control computed offline from the public LG trace, which stays out of the repository. Check first that the Pi's kernel supports a quota per destination. Starts count-only.

5. **A probe from the TV's position, then confirm-and-revert.** In CI: `tvgw-apply` saves last-good state, arms a restore timer, applies, runs the probe, and cancels the timer only on a pass. Test: inject a bad rule and time the restore; recreate the 26 October no-route fault and confirm the probe catches it; hold one connection open across a re-apply to cover the 30 October fault; kill the apply halfway and confirm that boot restores last-good. On the gateway: add the probe as a new check at warning level every ten minutes (decision 58's warnings-first rule), with its own address behind a narrow exception, stored with the gateway samples and kept out of the viewing record; run confirm-and-revert in report-only ("would have reverted") for two weeks to measure false reverts before it is armed. Before-and-after measure: time from a bad apply to a working TV path, with and without it. This item is reliability, ranked here because items 2, 3, 4 and 6 cannot enforce safely without it.

6. **Refuse unusual ports, if the written precondition is met.** Read the last fourteen daily briefs; flip `STRICT_PORTS` only if decision 17's condition holds, and otherwise keep counting. This is an existing plan and the first control to go through item 5: apply with the probe, then a week on probation in which a refusal that coincides with a failed clock or must-allow lookup, or with the judge's drop-in-viewing rule, sets it back to counting. Verify: the port-refusal counter, a full evening of the household's usual apps, and the LG firmware fallback on port 8080 still accepted.

7. **"What was just refused", with company names.** A read-only report of the names refused in the last ten minutes, ranked by retries and first sight, each with its one fix from `tvgw check`; and the company behind each host in the plain brief, from AdGuard's companies database with its required attribution. Test: fixed-input brief tests, and a backwards check, using the 11 September 2026 clock-host block as a test case, that the report names the cause in one step. Verify: over the next weeks, count how many app failures it explains in one step. No blocking; it is what makes any stricter default livable.

8. **Name the unnamed, and prepare for the next firmware.** Build pass 2's DNS-answer naming with its source recorded and its guard (a DNS-guessed name never feeds automatic blocking as if observed); add the stable "stack key"; label unreassembled QUIC openings; add the fan-out query as a report column. Compare two adjacent 14-day windows with no firmware change to get the noise floor, and add the 72-hour "firmware changed" line with an immediate settings re-read. Test: pass 2's store tests T1 to T3, the capture samples, and property tests. Verify: the share of connections and bytes left unnamed before and after, and the A/A false-difference count. No blocking.

9. **A guard on the blocklist channel, report-only.** Keep the last good copy of each list; flag any list that shrinks by more than a fifth or more than doubles; replay the TV's distinct lookups of the last 30 days against each refreshed list and print every changed verdict in the brief. This is pass 2's Stage A with replay added, and it starts the two report-only weeks that Stage B requires. Test: the two harness tests pass 2 still owes (an empty file empties a list; a list's rewrite against a must-allow exception) and a backwards check against the 11 September push. Verify: two weeks of previews on the gateway only, since the names are a viewing record. Open point: whether AdGuard's host check can judge a list not yet applied, or a staging copy is needed.

10. **Tests and health that find failures before the household does.** In CI: inject loss, delay and a dead resolver into the namespace gateway and assert that no unfiltered path opens; write the safe-state table and test it with random packets against a short model of the policy; put tvgw's settings and store on a file system that drops unsynced writes and assert old-or-new, never truncated; feed random input to the connection-opening reader and the settings editor (fuzzing). On the gateway: refuse a store newer than the code and keep the previous package with a settings snapshot; set `synchronous=EXTRA`; promote the checks that have their seven clean days; carry warnings into the brief's "doctor" tier; sample temperature, disk and memory; and run the 1 November validation steps 29 to 58. Verify: the count of fail-closed rows proven under fault, and a restore drill from a backup, recorded.
